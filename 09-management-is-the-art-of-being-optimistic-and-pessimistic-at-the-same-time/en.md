# Management Is the Art of Being Optimistic and Pessimistic at the Same Time

_The art of thinking about the best and preparing for the worst simultaneously._

**Summary:** I argue that managing means sustaining a tension: being optimistic enough to create direction and mobilize people, but pessimistic enough to question assumptions and prepare for what may go wrong. We do not need certainty to make decisions, but we need to recognize uncertainty and build adaptability. Good management, therefore, does not eliminate doubt or depend on perfect plans: it creates confidence to move forward and caution to change course when reality demands it.

---

Management is often associated with the ability to make decisions, organize resources, solve problems, and lead people toward some objective. Much of the time, however, a manager's work takes place in territory that is far less organized than this definition suggests. There is rarely enough information to make a decision with confidence, resources are almost never unlimited, people do not respond in perfectly predictable ways, and plans are constantly subject to events that were not anticipated when they were developed.

Even so, someone is expected to be able to look at this scenario and point to a direction. The team needs to know where it is going, what the priorities are, and why it is worth investing energy in that direction. The manager needs to turn uncertainty into some degree of confidence so that the organization can act. If every decision depends on first eliminating all doubts and risks, no complex organization will be able to move forward.

But there is a paradox in this work. The same manager who needs to convince people that a certain path is possible also needs to be the person most aware of everything that could make that path fail. They need to build confidence without manufacturing certainty. They need to encourage ambition without ignoring constraints. They need to look at the future with optimism and, at the same time, look at the present with a healthy dose of skepticism.

It is this tension that makes management particularly interesting. An excessively optimistic manager can turn a hypothesis into a certainty, a plan into a promise, and a history of success into a false sense of security. An excessively pessimistic manager can see so many risks that they lose the ability to mobilize people and make decisions. In both cases, there is an imbalance.

Perhaps good management lies precisely in the ability to sustain these two perspectives simultaneously. We need to believe that things can work out and, at the same time, seriously consider all the ways in which they can go wrong. Thinking about the best not as a denial of risks, but as the reason why it is worth moving forward. Preparing for the worst not as an expectation of failure, but as recognition that any complex system contains uncertainties that cannot be eliminated.

At its core, managing means making decisions under uncertainty and convincing other people to execute them. And this requires an unintuitive combination: enough optimism to build the future and enough pessimism to survive when the future does not follow the plan.

## Optimism to build a direction

A team needs to know where it is going. People rarely mobilize consistently when there is no clear vision of the future or when the person leading them constantly expresses doubt about the possibility of getting there. The manager does not need to have all the answers, but needs to be able to turn a set of possibilities into an understandable direction. It is part of their job to look at a scenario that does not yet exist and build a narrative concrete enough for other people to see meaning in moving in that direction.

This is especially important because organizations constantly operate with some degree of uncertainty. When an objective is defined, we still do not know exactly what obstacles will appear, which assumptions will prove correct, or which external circumstances will change along the way. Even so, someone needs to make the decision to begin. Waiting for all variables to be known before acting is, in practice, a very efficient way to never act.

The manager, therefore, needs to assume a responsibility that goes beyond simply communicating goals. They need to create conviction. They need to explain why a particular objective matters, what problem is being solved, where that decision intends to take the organization, and why it is worth facing the difficulties necessary to get there. An isolated goal can be achieved. An understood direction can mobilize people.

This ability to build a vision of the future requires a certain degree of optimism. Not necessarily naive optimism, based on the belief that everything will work as planned, but a willingness to believe that there is something better to be built. Without this belief, management tends to become exclusively reactive. The manager starts managing what exists, correcting deviations, and solving problems, but stops building a direction.

And there is an important difference between managing reality and transforming it. Managing means ensuring that the system continues to function. Transforming requires believing that it can function in a different and better way. Every meaningful change begins with a hypothesis about the future. Before there is evidence that this future is possible, someone needs to be willing to bet that it can be built.

This is where optimism stops being a personal characteristic and becomes a management tool. A manager needs to be able to say “let's do it” even when many things are still unresolved. They need to create movement before all conditions are perfectly established. They need to give the team enough confidence to begin solving problems that will only appear once the work actually starts.

This does not mean selling certainty. There is an important difference between believing in an objective and believing that the path to it will be simple. The first is necessary. The second can be dangerous. A manager can be deeply convinced that a particular project is important and, at the same time, recognize that there is a significant chance the original plan will need to be changed. Confidence should be placed in the ability to reach the objective, not necessarily in the precision of the path chosen to get there.

This distinction also changes the way errors are treated. If the manager presents the plan as a certainty, any deviation seems like a threat to their own credibility. If they present the plan as the best hypothesis available at that moment, changes become a natural part of execution. The team can recognize that an assumption was wrong without interpreting it as a failure of the strategy. The objective remains stable while the path can be adjusted.

There is also an important human dimension. People need to perceive that there is someone willing to take responsibility for the decisions made. In uncertain environments, the absence of direction produces anxiety and dispersion. When no one knows exactly what should be prioritized, each person starts building their own interpretation of what matters. The result is usually an organization that is busy, but not necessarily an organization that is coordinated.

Therefore, the manager needs to be a source of clarity even when they do not have all the answers. “We don't know yet” can be a perfectly legitimate answer, as long as it is accompanied by “but we know what we are trying to achieve, we know what the priority is, and we know what the next step will be.” Leadership is not about eliminating uncertainty. It is about preventing uncertainty from eliminating the ability to act.

An organization that is excessively concerned about what could go wrong ends up finding reasons not to start. Every initiative can be delayed by another analysis, another validation, another approval, or another condition that needs to be satisfied. The risk is turning prudence into paralysis.

Optimism, in this context, is the force that sets the system in motion. It allows calculated risks to be taken, hypotheses to be tested, people to be invested in, projects to be initiated, and objectives to be pursued without guarantees of success. But this optimism is healthy only when there is room for doubt. Because believing that we can get there is necessary. Believing that we know exactly how to get there is something else.

## Pessimism to see what can go wrong

The problem begins when optimism stops being a willingness to move forward and becomes a belief that the plan will necessarily work. There is a fundamental difference between believing in a team's ability to execute and believing that reality will follow exactly what was designed in a meeting room. The first stance produces confidence. The second produces fragility.

No plan survives intact upon contact with reality. Not because planning is useless, but because every plan is built on a set of assumptions. It assumes certain behaviors, certain conditions, certain deadlines, certain capabilities, and a certain sequence of events. As long as these assumptions remain true, the plan appears to work. When one of them ceases to be true, reality begins to demand adaptations.

This becomes particularly relevant when dealing with complex and interconnected systems. The greater the number of components, dependencies, and interactions, the greater the number of conditions that need to be satisfied for the expected outcome to occur. An application can work. A team can work. A vendor can work. A process can work. Even so, the combination of all of them can produce behavior that no one anticipated.

It is precisely at the connections that many problems appear.

A complex system does not need to have bad components to produce a bad outcome. Individually reliable components can, when combined, produce unexpected behavior. A seemingly small change at one point in the system can generate much larger consequences elsewhere. A decision made to solve one problem can create another problem in a different part of the organization. An operational exception can remain invisible for months until it encounters a specific combination of circumstances that turns it into a significant failure.

That is why the manager needs to develop a kind of disciplined pessimism. This is not about expecting everything to go wrong, much less turning every decision into an endless discussion about risks. It is about assuming as a premise that the plan contains points of uncertainty and that some of the things that seem unlikely today may eventually happen.

The disciplined pessimist does not only ask “how do we make this work?” They also ask “what could prevent this from working?” They do not only ask “what is the best hypothesis?” They ask “which of our assumptions could be wrong?” They do not only ask “when will we finish?” They ask “what could make this deadline cease to be realistic?” And, most importantly, they do not wait for failure to happen to figure out what to do afterward.

This last part is fundamental. Identifying a risk is relatively easy. True management begins when we think about what we will do with it. What signals indicate that the risk is materializing? At what point should we abandon the original plan? Is there a viable alternative? Who has the authority to make that decision? How much time do we have to react? What resources need to be available for the alternative to actually work?

This type of questioning turns risk into a management variable instead of treating it as an exceptional event.

There is also a probabilistic dimension to this discussion. In a simple system, we can often observe a few elements and have a good sense of what will happen. In a complex system, the outcome depends on so many variables and interactions that predictability decreases. We do not need to know exactly which component will fail to recognize that, at some point, some unexpected condition will probably arise.

This is an important difference between control and resilience. Control attempts to ensure that everything happens as planned. Resilience recognizes that this will not be possible and builds the capacity to absorb deviations, detect problems quickly, and change direction when necessary.

Overconfidence tends to arise precisely when we confuse these two things. A process works for years and we begin to believe that it is reliable under any circumstances. A project repeatedly delivers on time and we begin to consider that deadline a property of the system rather than the result of a particular combination of conditions. A team repeatedly solves problems and we begin to believe that it will always be able to solve any problem.

A history of success is important evidence, but it is not a guarantee.

Therefore, the manager's pessimism should not be directed only at other people's plans. It needs to begin with their own plans. The most valuable question may be precisely the one that challenges a decision that appears correct: “what are we failing to see?” This question creates a small amount of friction before the decision and can prevent a great deal of friction afterward.

The objective is not to build an organization that is afraid of making mistakes. It is to build an organization that does not need to make mistakes to discover how it should have prepared.

This pessimism does not reduce ambition. On the contrary, it increases execution capacity because it allows the organization to move forward without depending on the premise that everything will work perfectly. The more prepared we are to deal with deviations, the more freedom we have to take calculated risks.

The optimist looks at the objective and asks how to get there. The pessimist looks at the same objective and asks what could prevent us from getting there. The manager needs to ask both questions. The first creates movement. The second prevents movement from depending on luck.

## The danger of overconfidence

There is a particularly dangerous trap in complex systems: prolonged success.

When a process works for a long time, we naturally begin to trust it. This is rational up to a point. If a particular combination of people, processes, and technologies has repeatedly produced good results, it is reasonable to conclude that there is something in that structure that works. The problem begins when we turn this evidence into certainty about the future. The fact that something has worked many times does not mean it will continue working under the same conditions, especially when conditions can change without us noticing.

Success has a dangerous characteristic: it rarely requires an immediate explanation. When something goes wrong, we are forced to investigate. When it goes right, we tend to move on. A system that works for years accumulates trust, but not necessarily a proportional understanding of its vulnerabilities.

An organization can go years without facing a particular problem and, as a result, stop considering it relevant. A contingency procedure that has never needed to be used begins to seem unnecessary. A control that has never encountered an adverse situation starts being treated as bureaucracy. Important knowledge becomes concentrated in a few people because they have always been available when needed. An alternative flow stops being tested because the main one has always worked. An exception that has never happened gradually begins to be treated as an impossibility.

The system continues to function. The indicators remain good. The projects continue to be delivered. And precisely because of this, no one has an obvious incentive to question it.

This is one of the reasons why overconfidence can be more dangerous than a visible problem. A known problem mobilizes resources. A vulnerability that has not yet manifested itself competes with all the organization's other priorities and frequently loses that competition. As long as there is no concrete evidence that something can go wrong, it seems more rational to invest energy elsewhere.

This is how organizations can become fragile without realizing it.

Not necessarily because their processes have deteriorated, but because their ability to deal with situations outside the norm has gradually been lost while everything continued to work within the norm.

Overconfidence can also appear in the form of metrics. Good indicators are important, but they can create a false sense of control when they begin to be interpreted as a complete description of reality. A system can be within all relevant targets and still have a vulnerability that is not being measured. What is measured tends to receive attention. What is not measured tends to disappear from the conversation.

Therefore, the manager needs to be able to question positive results without appearing irrationally distrustful. When everything is working, perhaps that is precisely the most appropriate time to ask why. What is making this process work? What assumptions are we making? What would happen if one of those assumptions ceased to be true? Which protection mechanisms still work? If a key person becomes unavailable tomorrow, can we continue? If the main flow fails, do we know how long it would take us to switch to an alternative?

These questions deliberately introduce a degree of discomfort into an organization that is functioning well.

This may seem unnecessary to someone who looks only at the current result. But there is a difference between an organization that is functioning and an organization that is prepared to continue functioning when conditions change. The first may simply be benefiting from a favorable scenario. The second has built mechanisms to absorb change.

The manager's role, therefore, is not to eliminate the organization's confidence. It is to prevent confidence from turning into complacency.

Questioning a process that works does not mean believing it is bad. Testing an alternative plan does not mean believing the main plan will fail. Reviewing a control does not mean distrusting the people who execute it. Simulating a crisis situation does not mean expecting a crisis.

It means recognizing a basic characteristic of any complex system: some failures are only perceived when the conditions necessary to produce them finally appear.

And when that moment comes, it will be too late to start thinking about what should have been done.

That is why mature organizations need to create mechanisms for questioning their own success. They need to review processes even when the indicators are good, test contingencies even when they seem unnecessary, and preserve knowledge even when the right people remain available. They need to create controlled situations in which they can discover that a particular assumption was wrong before reality discovers it for them.

## A manager cannot be just a firefighter or a visionary disconnected from reality

A manager cannot be just a firefighter, condemned to react to problems as they appear. Solving crises is an important skill, and some situations will inevitably require quick responses, difficult decisions, and the ability to improvise. The problem arises when this dynamic stops being exceptional and starts defining the manager's very role. If every day there is a new fire to put out, perhaps the problem is not the number of fires, but the absence of a system capable of preventing them or, at least, absorbing them without depending on a single person.

There is an important difference between being good at solving problems and building an organization that needs fewer emergency interventions. The first requires responsiveness. The second requires prevention. A manager who spends all their time solving consequences may even be perceived as indispensable, but runs the risk of becoming part of the mechanism that keeps the organization dependent on them. Each problem solved individually can be a missed opportunity to correct the cause that will make the same problem appear again.

The firefighter looks at what is happening now. Their horizon is the next problem, the next urgency, the next decision that needs to be made. This perspective is necessary at certain moments, but insufficient for management. An organization cannot permanently depend on someone's ability to react faster than problems appear. At some point, it is necessary to move beyond the logic of putting out fires and start asking why so many fires continue to happen.

At the opposite extreme is the visionary disconnected from reality. This is the manager who is capable of seeing great possibilities, setting ambitious objectives, and building convincing narratives about the future, but who does not devote the same attention to the conditions necessary to turn that vision into reality. They look at where the organization could go, but do not look closely enough at the system that will have to take it there.

A vision can be absolutely correct and still be a poor execution strategy. A company can have a huge opportunity in front of it and lack the operational capacity to take advantage of it. A project can make economic sense and lack sufficient resources, people, or technology to be executed. A transformation can be necessary and still fail because the organization lacks the capacity to absorb the pace of change.

The problem is not thinking big. It is confusing the possibility of a future with the current capacity to build it.

The manager needs to occupy the space between these two extremes. They need enough vision to lead the organization and enough contact with reality to understand where that vision can fail. They need to be able to look at the horizon without stopping to observe the ground they are traveling over.

This may be one of the most difficult parts of management because it requires two different forms of thinking. One is prospective: where do we want to go, what can we build, what opportunities exist, and what do we need to change to take advantage of them. The other is systemic: what do we depend on, where are the points of fragility, what constraints exist, what happens when a particular part stops working, and what consequences can arise in other parts of the organization.

Without the first, the manager manages the present. Without the second, they imagine the future.

Knowing the organization's reality, therefore, does not mean knowing only its processes, indicators, and org charts. It means understanding how the system actually works when things do not happen as expected. Who can solve a problem when the usual person responsible is unavailable? Which process depends on a specific person? Which controls exist only because a particular team knows an exception that is not documented? Which systems depend on other systems? Where are the points at which a small failure can produce a disproportionate consequence?

It is at this level that operational knowledge becomes managerial knowledge. A manager needs to know not only how the system works, but also the conditions under which it stops working. They need to understand its recovery mechanisms, its points of risk concentration, and its critical dependencies. They need to know which parts are redundant and which have a single possible path. They need to identify where there is room for error and where a small deviation can quickly turn into a larger problem.

This also changes the manager's relationship with the team. When they deeply understand operational reality, they can establish ambitious objectives without turning ambition into fantasy. They can say “we need to get here” and, at the same time, honestly discuss what will be necessary for that to happen. The team recognizes that the vision is not merely a presentation phrase, but a direction built on a concrete understanding of reality.

At its core, the manager needs to constantly move between two points. They must leave reality to imagine what can be built and return to reality to discover what will be necessary to build it. Then they must repeat that movement.

The firefighter looks at the problem and asks how to solve it. The visionary looks at the future and asks how to reach it. The manager needs to ask both questions, but add a third: what needs to exist in the system for this future to be possible and continue functioning when things inevitably go off plan?

## Teaching the team to be optimistic and cautious

Perhaps one of the most important responsibilities of a manager is to teach their team to think in the same way. It is not enough for the manager to be careful, question assumptions, and think about alternatives while the rest of the organization operates based on overconfidence. If every decision needs to go through a single person so that someone can ask the difficult questions, the problem has merely moved. The organization remains vulnerable; it has simply centralized its ability to see risks.

The objective, therefore, should not be to form one person responsible for identifying all problems. It should be to spread a way of thinking. To make cautious optimism cease to be a characteristic of the manager and become part of the way the team makes decisions, executes processes, and interprets results.

A mature team needs to learn that questioning a decision does not mean opposing it. Asking “what could go wrong?” does not mean believing it will go wrong. Testing a hypothesis does not mean a lack of confidence in the person who proposed it. Likewise, pointing out a risk should not automatically be interpreted as pessimism or resistance to change.

This may be one of the greatest cultural challenges in management. In many organizations, there is a tendency to associate enthusiasm with commitment and questioning with a lack of alignment. The employee who says “let's do it” is perceived as someone who buys into the idea, while the one who asks “what if it doesn't work?” may be seen as someone creating difficulties. This dynamic is dangerous because, over time, people learn exactly which questions they should not ask.

A healthy organization needs to produce the opposite effect. It needs to create space for someone to demonstrate enthusiasm for an idea and, in the same conversation, question its assumptions. A person can deeply believe that a project is important and still ask what its failure points are. They can advocate for a change and, simultaneously, propose an alternative plan in case a particular hypothesis is not confirmed.

The manager's message needs to be clear: we do not want pessimistic people. We want people who know how to think about what can go wrong without losing their willingness to make it go right.

This distinction is important because optimism and caution serve different functions. Optimism provides energy to move forward, allows calculated risks to be taken, and helps the team see possibilities that do not yet exist. Caution prevents that energy from turning into recklessness. It forces the organization to test assumptions, observe warning signs, and preserve alternatives when the scenario changes.

When these two characteristics are incorporated by the team, the quality of decisions changes. People stop asking only “what should we do?” and also begin asking “why do we believe this will work?”, “what would need to be true for this decision to work?” and “how will we know quickly if we are wrong?”.

These questions do not need to turn every decision into a bureaucratic process. On the contrary. When this type of thinking becomes part of the culture, it tends to become faster and more natural. People begin anticipating problems before they are formally presented. They start creating alternatives without needing to be asked. They identify dependencies, raise hypotheses, and flag vulnerabilities while there is still time to act.

This is when the manager truly begins to scale their capacity. They stop being the person who needs to see all the risks and become the person who taught the organization to see them.

A team that thinks this way also becomes less dependent on certainty. It can move forward even when information is incomplete because it understands that decisions are hypotheses that need to be tested against reality. If reality confirms the hypothesis, it moves forward. If it does not, the path is adjusted.

There is a fundamental difference between a team that blindly believes in the plan and a team that believes in the plan while knowing exactly why it might fail. The first depends on things happening as expected. The second is prepared to continue functioning when they do not.

In the end, perhaps this is one of the greatest contributions of a manager: not only to give the team direction, but to teach people how to navigate uncertainty. To create an organization in which it is possible to look at the future with confidence without losing the ability to question the present.

The objective is not for everyone to think like pessimists. It is for everyone to learn to be cautious optimists.

## A difficult but necessary balance

In the end, perhaps management is less about choosing between optimism and pessimism and more about being able to sustain both at the same time. This balance is difficult because the two forces pull in different directions. Optimism leads us to believe in what does not yet exist. Pessimism forces us to recognize everything that could prevent that future from happening. One sets the system in motion. The other prevents movement from being confused with certainty.

The manager needs to be optimistic about the direction and cautious about the path. They need to believe in people's potential without ignoring their limits. They need to trust processes without ceasing to question their failure points. They need to encourage execution without turning speed into recklessness. They need to look at an opportunity and see what can be built, but also look at the same scenario and ask what conditions need to exist for it to actually work.

This balance is difficult because confidence is contagious. When an organization begins to succeed, it is natural for people to question less. When a manager demonstrates a great deal of confidence, the team tends to follow. This is necessary to create movement, but it also creates an additional responsibility. The greater the confidence placed in a decision, the greater the discipline needs to be to question its assumptions.

Therefore, the manager should not seek to eliminate doubt. They should learn to live with it without allowing it to paralyze the organization. They do not need certainty to decide, but they need to know which uncertainties they are assuming. They do not need to predict every problem, but they need to build a team capable of recognizing them when they arise. They do not need to build a perfect plan, but they need to ensure that the organization can adapt when the plan no longer makes sense.

Thinking about the best does not mean ignoring the worst. Preparing for the worst does not mean expecting it to happen. The balance lies precisely in being able to do both simultaneously.

Complex systems do not need to fail to teach us that they could have failed. Organizations do not need to face a crisis to discover that they depended too heavily on one person, one process, or a single alternative. And managers do not need to wait for the next fire to realize that they should have thought beforehand about how to deal with it.

Management maturity emerges when this preparation stops being a reaction to problems and becomes part of the way the organization thinks. When people can pursue ambitious objectives without turning their assumptions into certainties. When they can defend a decision and, at the same time, question it. When they can move forward with confidence knowing that they may need to change direction.

Perhaps this is one of the most important functions of a manager: to create enough confidence for the team to move forward and enough caution for it to know what to do when reality does not follow the plan.

At its core, it is a difficult but necessary balance: believe that it will work out, without ever believing that it cannot go wrong.
