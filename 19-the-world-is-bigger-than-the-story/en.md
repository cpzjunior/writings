# The World Is Bigger Than the Story

_What Harry Potter, Tolkien, Star Wars, and RPGs teach us about expanding fictional universes_

**Summary:** I argue that a franchise only becomes truly expandable when its universe is larger than any story told within it. More than creating new works or accumulating lore, it is necessary to build a world with rules, institutions, cultures, and a history of its own, capable of supporting new protagonists, places, and periods without losing its identity. It is this logic that allows universes such as Middle-earth, Westeros, Star Wars, and the great RPG settings to continue existing beyond their original narratives. In the case of Harry Potter, the opportunity lies in treating Hogwarts as a gateway to a much larger Wizarding World, rather than as its limit.

---

When I saw the confirmation of Hogwarts Legacy 2, my first reaction was neither excitement nor disappointment. It was a question: what else is there to expand in Hogwarts that has not already been addressed in the first game?

I do not mean this as a criticism of the game, which I actually thought was excellent, much less as a prediction about what the sequel will be. It is entirely possible that the developers have found new ways to explore the school and its surroundings, or that the second game will be much broader than the first. Perhaps it itself will demonstrate that Hogwarts still has far more possibilities than we imagine.

The question caught my attention because Hogwarts Legacy solved an interesting problem: how do you make a Harry Potter game without Harry Potter? Instead of continuing the familiar story, the game went back in time and placed the player in the role of a new Hogwarts student. The school became the main anchor of the experience. The player could choose a house, attend classes, learn spells, explore its corridors, visit Hogsmeade, fly around the surrounding areas, and build their own story within that universe.

It is a solution that allows an extremely well-known intellectual property to be used without depending on its original protagonists. Harry, Hermione, and Ron do not need to be present. The story can be different, the characters can be different, and the time period can be different. Even so, when the player walks through the corridors of Hogwarts, they immediately recognize the universe they are in.

But there is an interesting consequence to this choice. To tell a story that is not about Harry Potter without ceasing to feel like Harry Potter, it is natural to rely on the work's most characteristic elements. And Hogwarts may be the most important of them. This creates a tension that goes beyond Hogwarts Legacy. A franchise can free itself from its protagonists without necessarily freeing itself from its places. And if the franchise's identity comes to depend on those places, they can eventually become just as important as the characters themselves in defining the limits of what can be told.

Hogwarts is an extraordinarily rich setting. But Hogwarts is not the wizarding world. Around the school there is an entire society, with institutions, professions, communities, traditions, conflicts, creatures, different forms of magic, and centuries of history. Harry Potter introduced us to a small part of this world. Hogwarts was our main window into it.

Perhaps, therefore, the next stage of a franchise like this is not to figure out how to make Hogwarts increasingly larger. Perhaps it is to figure out how to make the world that exists beyond it large enough to sustain its own stories. And this question does not belong only to Harry Potter. It lies at the heart of a characteristic that separates some of fiction's greatest franchises: the ability of their universes to continue existing and producing stories even after the story that introduced them has come to an end.

## Going Beyond the Main Story

There is an important difference between telling a story and building a universe capable of housing many stories. A narrative can be deeply tied to its protagonist and still be excellent. The protagonist concentrates the conflict, gives meaning to events, and functions as our gateway into that universe. Harry Potter is a great example of this. It is through Harry's story that we come to know the wizarding world, Hogwarts, its characters, its rules, and its possibilities.

But a franchise can take a step further when it begins exploring what exists around the main story. Instead of asking only what happens to the characters we already know, we can start asking what happened before them, what is happening at the same time in other places, and what might happen afterward.

That is when the universe begins to take on a life of its own. There is a wizarding society that did not begin with Harry and does not end with him. There are institutions, professions, families, schools, creatures, traditions, conflicts, and historical events that can be explored independently of the original narrative.

It is important, however, to distinguish the expansion of a franchise from the expansion of a universe. A franchise can grow indefinitely without its universe becoming significantly larger. We can have new movies, games, and series, new protagonists, and even new stories, while continuing to explore the same places, institutions, and conflicts. Having more works does not necessarily mean having a larger world.

Expanding a universe is different. It means increasing what can happen within it. New places, historical periods, institutions, cultures, systems, and relationships allow stories that previously would not even have been possible to come into existence. The goal is not simply to tell more stories, but to increase the set of stories that world is capable of supporting.

Tolkien built his work this way. The War of the Ring is only a specific period in the history of Middle-earth. The same is true of Westeros, where the events of Game of Thrones represent only part of a much longer history.

In RPGs, this characteristic becomes even more apparent. Forgotten Realms and The Elder Scrolls were built so that different characters, campaigns, and stories could occupy different regions and periods without the universe ceasing to be recognizable. It is precisely this ability to accommodate different stories that transforms a setting into something larger than any of the narratives told within it.

## Every Story Takes Place in a World

Every story takes place in some world. Even the simplest narrative presupposes a reality in which its events can occur. That world may be explicitly constructed, like Middle-earth, or appear only as a backdrop. Even so, there are always rules that determine what is possible, who the people in that place are, how they live, and which cause-and-effect relationships make sense within that reality.

When we create a story, therefore, we are inevitably creating or using a world. The question is how much of that world exists beyond the story itself. There is an important difference between a story that builds only the world necessary to tell its narrative and a universe that has a life of its own beyond it.

In the first situation, the setting is primarily in service of the story. We learn what we need to know to follow the characters and understand their conflicts. If we want to continue the work, it is natural to look at the same characters, the same conflict, or what happened afterward.

In the second, the story is merely a manifestation of that world. The universe has rules, institutions, cultures, geography, and a history of its own that do not necessarily depend on the characters we are following. Because of this, we can change protagonists, regions, or historical periods without abandoning the work's fundamental identity.

Tolkien is one of the clearest examples of this. The War of the Ring is not the history of Middle-earth. It is a story that takes place within the much larger history of that world. Frodo did not create Mordor, Aragorn did not create Gondor, and Gandalf did not create the long history of the peoples who inhabit Middle-earth.

We can remove the characters from The Lord of the Rings and still have thousands of years of events, cultures, wars, peoples, kingdoms, and transformations. We can go back to the First Age, follow the rise and fall of Númenor, or simply tell a smaller story somewhere in Middle-earth. The narrative we know is only a window into a world that continues to exist beyond it.

This difference is fundamental to understanding why some universes can support so many different works. It is not enough to have a good story. What surrounds it must have enough consistency for other stories to emerge naturally. And perhaps this is one of the most important characteristics of a truly expandable fictional universe: the story can be enormous, but the world needs to feel larger.

## The Importance of Worldbuilding

The strength of Tolkien's worldbuilding lies not only in the number of names or events. It lies in the feeling that the characters are arriving late to a story that began long before them. The First Age has already passed. The Second Age has already passed. Númenor has already existed. Sauron's rise has already happened. Great wars were fought before Frodo was born. This creates a peculiar sense of temporal depth. The main story is not the beginning of that world. Nor does it need to be its end.

This structure allows for extraordinary narrative freedom. A new work can go back thousands of years in time, move forward to another era, or simply explore events that exist around the main narrative. The universe remains recognizable because it has a historical continuity much larger than any of its stories.

George R. R. Martin uses a similar logic, although differently. Game of Thrones is not the history of Westeros. It is a specific story within the history of Westeros. Aegon's Conquest happened centuries earlier. The Dance of the Dragons happened long before the events of Game of Thrones. Robert's Rebellion had already ended when the story began. That is why House of the Dragon does not need to be a continuation of Game of Thrones to be a story about Westeros. The same universe can support completely different characters, periods, and conflicts.

And this is precisely where the worldbuilding of Harry Potter can become interesting. The story we know occupies only a specific period within a society that, within the narrative itself, already has a history of its own. Hogwarts was founded centuries before Harry. The Ministry of Magic already existed. Wizarding families already had their own histories. Different schools, traditions, and magical communities existed independently of the events we follow in the books.

This material already offers different possible directions for expansion. We can look at other eras, other communities, and other institutions. We can explore events that were only mentioned, follow characters who were never at the center of the narrative, or develop aspects of wizarding society that the books only needed to present superficially.

## RPGs as a Laboratory for Universes

Perhaps no medium has demonstrated this difference better than RPGs. An RPG needs more than a good story. It needs a world in which the player can make decisions, explore different paths, and encounter situations that were not necessarily designed as a single linear narrative. That is why the great RPG settings offer a particularly interesting perspective on what it means to build a universe capable of generating stories.

Forgotten Realms is one of the best-known examples. Over decades, the setting has accumulated regions, cities, gods, planes, peoples, organizations, historical conflicts, and countless characters. This depth is precisely one of its greatest characteristics, although it can also make entering the setting somewhat more difficult for someone encountering this universe for the first time.

What is interesting, however, is that all this depth is not concentrated in a single story. It is possible to tell an adventure in Waterdeep, another in Baldur's Gate, another in Icewind Dale, or another in Candlekeep. We can change protagonists, eras, regions, and scale. Each story can function independently, but they all share the same world structure.

Candlekeep is particularly interesting when thinking about Hogwarts. It is a place with its own identity, recognizable and rich enough to support an entire story. But Candlekeep is not Forgotten Realms. It is a part of a much larger universe. This distinction allows a setting to have extremely important places without turning any of them into the limit of the experience. We can start in Candlekeep, pass through Waterdeep, and end on another continent. We can tell another story hundreds of years later without needing to abandon the same universe.

Hogwarts could occupy a similar position in the wizarding world. This does not mean diminishing the school's importance. On the contrary, Hogwarts could remain one of the most important and recognizable institutions in that universe. The difference would be treating it as one of the world's great anchors, rather than necessarily as its boundary.

## Skyrim and the World That Continues Beyond the Player

The Elder Scrolls presents an even more interesting approach because it makes this independence part of the player's own experience. In Skyrim, there is a main story, and the Dragonborn occupies a central position within it. But that story does not need to organize the entire experience. The player can face Alduin immediately or spend dozens of hours doing practically anything else. They can join a guild, explore ruins, participate in political conflicts, investigate local stories, meet different peoples, or simply travel across the province without following the main narrative.

This works because Skyrim is not merely the story of the Dragonborn. It is a province of Tamriel, and its history is only one among many that take place on that continent. The player can be extremely important to certain events without their existence being necessary for that society to continue existing, with its own conflicts, institutions, and stories.

This is a particularly valuable characteristic for a franchise. The protagonist can be fundamental to the narrative without being fundamental to the existence of the universe. When the story ends, there are already other places, conflicts, peoples, and historical periods that can serve as starting points for a new narrative.

That is why a future The Elder Scrolls work can simply choose another region, another era, and another protagonist. There is no need to find a justification to bring back the Dragonborn or directly continue their story. The universe already offers other possibilities.

Perhaps this is one of the biggest differences between a franchise based on a story and a franchise based on a universe: in the first, the next work needs to find a way to continue. In the second, it can simply choose where to look next.

## Lore Does Not Need to Be an Excess of Information

There is an important distinction between depth and quantity of information. A universe does not necessarily become more interesting because it has more names, events, characters, and details. Expanding a world can happen through the accumulation of information, but also through the way its elements relate to one another.

Forgotten Realms is a good example. Its depth is one of its most striking characteristics. Decades of stories have added regions, characters, religions, conflicts, peoples, and events to the setting. For those who already know this material, this historical dimension is an important part of the experience. For someone arriving now, however, the amount of information can make entering the universe more intimidating.

This suggests that building an expandable universe does not necessarily mean building an encyclopedia. What may be more interesting is building a universe capable of generating stories. There is a difference between descriptive lore and generative lore. The first tells us what happened. The second establishes conditions under which new events can naturally arise.

A chronology with a thousand wars can be impressive. But institutions with their own interests, consistent rules, cause-and-effect relationships, and conflicts that can take different forms may be even more important for a franchise. These are the elements that allow a new story to be created without requiring an entirely new world to be invented for each work.

Avatar: The Last Airbender offers an interesting example. The fundamental system is relatively simple: there are four elements and different bending traditions. From these rules, however, different cultures, religions, martial arts, technologies, institutions, and political conflicts emerge.

The simplicity of the rules does not prevent the world from having depth. In fact, it can help make it more understandable, because the audience can grasp the fundamental rules and observe the consequences that arise when they interact with different societies.

Perhaps this is one of the most interesting points when thinking about the expansion of fictional universes: it is not necessary to indefinitely increase the amount of lore. It is possible to increase the number of stories that a universe can produce from the rules and structures it already possesses. It is not about complexity through accumulation. It is about complexity through interaction.

## Expanding Without Losing the Foundation

When we talk about existing franchises, there is an important distinction between expanding a universe and simply using its elements to tell a new story. Expanding does not mean having absolute freedom to do anything. A new work needs to introduce new elements, reinterpret situations, and often adapt what already exists. The point is not to preserve every detail as though the universe were immutable, but to maintain a recognizable logic that allows the audience to perceive that the new story still belongs to the same world.

The Lord of the Rings is a good example. Peter Jackson changed important elements of Tolkien's work to adapt it to film, cutting characters, rearranging events, and condensing parts of the narrative. Even so, the adaptation preserved what gave Middle-earth its identity. The changes served the new way of telling that story without replacing the fundamental logic of the universe.

This difference becomes even more important when a franchise begins producing stories that are not direct adaptations of the original work. The farther the new narrative moves from the characters and events we know, the more important the rules, history, and structures that sustain that universe become.

Star Wars is one of the most interesting examples because, for decades, it demonstrated the potential of this approach. The expanded universe created thousands of years of history, different eras, republics, empires, Jedi, Sith, wars, characters, and civilizations that did not depend directly on the original trilogy. Luke Skywalker was fundamental to the Star Wars story we knew. But Star Wars did not depend on Luke to exist.

It was possible to go back thousands of years in time, move forward to another era, change planets, protagonists, and conflicts. Historical continuity functioned as a kind of infrastructure that allowed new stories to be built without each one needing to reinvent the universe.

This also shows that a large amount of lore brings its own challenge. The larger the universe, the greater the responsibility to manage what has already been established. A new continuity does not need to reproduce everything that existed before, but it needs to offer some shared structure capable of connecting its different stories.

This is where expansion can follow different paths. A new work can add layers to what already exists, reinterpret elements, or even establish a new continuity. The fundamental point is to know whether these changes expand the possibilities of the universe or replace precisely what allowed it to function as a shared universe.

Healthy expansion adds new possibilities without making what came before irrelevant. The farther a new story can go without ceasing to feel like part of that world, the greater the franchise's potential for expansion.

## From Harry Potter to Wizarding World

This is where Harry Potter becomes especially interesting. The material necessary for expansion already exists. The wizarding world has schools of magic, the Ministry of Magic, Gringotts, St. Mungo's, families, creatures, artifacts, professions, potions, different magical disciplines, wizarding communities, relationships with Muggles, and centuries of history.

What can be developed from this does not necessarily need to be a new layer of lore added from the outside. Many of the possibilities are already implicit in what we know. A profession can become a new narrative perspective. An institution can generate its own conflicts. A school can reveal a different magical culture. A historical period can serve as the setting for a completely independent story.

This is precisely where the work of game designers, screenwriters, and other creators can be important. When a team works on an established intellectual property, it does not need to receive every detail necessary to build a new experience from the original author. The original work provides the rules, concepts, and foundations. From them, different teams can develop systems, institutions, and narrative possibilities, as long as they remain consistent with what has already been established.

J.K. Rowling did not need to have originally created an RPG system for designers to be able to turn the different forms of magic into game systems. Hogwarts did not need to have been conceived as an RPG hub for a game to turn it into an institution that connects characters, knowledge, missions, and different regions. And not every detail of the world needs to have been previously explained for new stories to be built.

Perhaps the most interesting evolution of the franchise is precisely a change in perspective. I am not necessarily talking about changing its name, but about recognizing that Harry Potter is already part of something larger: the Wizarding World.

The difference is not merely semantic. A franchise centered on Harry Potter naturally tends to ask, “what will Harry Potter's next story be?” A universe conceived through the Wizarding World can ask, “what other stories exist in this world?” Fantastic Beasts shows precisely why this distinction matters. The franchise managed to tell a story with another protagonist and in another period, but remained quite connected to the characters, institutions, and conflicts we already knew. Changing the story does not necessarily mean expanding the universe.

This second question opens up much greater possibilities. Harry can remain Harry. Hogwarts can remain Hogwarts. The original stories can remain exactly where they are. At the same time, new protagonists can emerge, new eras can be explored, new regions can be introduced, and new institutions can have their own stories.

This also does not mean abandoning what made Harry Potter special. On the contrary. Hogwarts can remain one of the great anchors of the wizarding world, just as Candlekeep is an important part of Forgotten Realms without representing the entire setting.

We can imagine a story that begins at Hogwarts and then takes the player somewhere else. We can imagine a story that takes place at another school. We can return to a historical period long before the books. We can follow a family, a profession, an institution, or a community that was never at the center of the original narrative. And we can do all of this without needing to put Harry Potter on screen.

Perhaps this is the true opportunity for expansion. Not to make less Harry Potter, but to make Harry Potter just one of the great stories of the Wizarding World. The larger the world becomes, the more the original story ceases to look like a limit and begins to look like what it always was: the gateway.

## A World Bigger Than the Story

That is why, when I think about the announcement of Hogwarts Legacy 2, my initial question about what is still left to do at Hogwarts may not be the most important one. The central question is different: what can still be done with the world that exists beyond it?

We do not know what the developers are preparing. The sequel may be much larger than the first game, and perhaps Hogwarts will be only one part of a broader experience. And it is precisely this possibility that makes the announcement interesting. Not as a criticism of the choice of Hogwarts, but as an opportunity to think about how far the Wizarding World can go when it stops being treated merely as the setting of a story and starts being explored as a universe.

J.K. Rowling can still contribute to this process, working alongside writers, designers, and other creators in building a canonical architecture rich enough to support different stories. The goal would not simply be to accumulate lore, but to develop the relationships between institutions, regions, cultures, rules, and events that are already part of this world.

Perhaps the fan community itself is a small demonstration of this potential. For decades, people have created fanfics, characters, and stories that take the wizarding world beyond the events of the books. This does not, by itself, mean that there is measurable commercial demand for any specific type of new work. But it reveals something interesting: for many people, the story of Harry Potter ended, while the world it introduced continued producing possibilities.

In the end, this is the difference between continuing a story and expanding a universe. Middle-earth does not end with Frodo, just as Westeros does not end with Jon Snow and Tamriel does not depend on the Dragonborn. In Forgotten Realms, a campaign can come to an end without the world ceasing to exist, just as Star Wars continues to be larger than the story of Luke Skywalker. These are universes in which characters pass through part of the story, but are not the story itself. Harry Potter can also finish his journey without the wizarding world needing to end with him.

Harry Potter was the gateway into this universe. Hogwarts was the window through which we came to know it. Perhaps the next step is to look beyond it and discover how many stories can still exist in that world. Because every story takes place in a world. And when that world is rich enough, a story can end without the world needing to end with it.
