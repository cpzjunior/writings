The fictional universe should be structurally larger than any individual story it contains. A franchise becomes genuinely expandable not merely by producing more works or accumulating lore, but by establishing a world with independent rules, institutions, cultures, geography, relationships, and historical continuity capable of generating stories involving different protagonists, places, scales, and periods while preserving recognizable identity.

Hogwarts Legacy demonstrates both the potential and the limitation of this model. It successfully created a Harry Potter experience without Harry, Hermione, or Ron by moving to an earlier period and making Hogwarts the central anchor: players choose houses, attend classes, learn spells, explore the school and Hogsmeade, fly through surrounding areas, and construct their own stories. This proves that the franchise can separate itself from its original protagonists while retaining its identity. However, reliance on Hogwarts also creates another dependency: a franchise can become independent of its characters while remaining dependent on its most recognizable places. If Hogwarts becomes the primary mechanism for signaling “this is Harry Potter,” the school can eventually become a boundary on narrative expansion.

Hogwarts is only one component of the Wizarding World. The broader setting already contains institutions, professions, communities, traditions, conflicts, creatures, magical disciplines, schools, families, artifacts, relationships with Muggles, and centuries of history. The original Harry Potter narrative exposes only a limited portion of this society. Expansion therefore need not mean making Hogwarts progressively larger; it can mean making the world beyond Hogwarts sufficiently developed to sustain stories of its own.

A narrative can be excellent while being deeply dependent on its protagonist. Harry Potter functions this way: Harry is the gateway through which the audience encounters Hogwarts, wizarding society, its rules, characters, and conflicts. A universe becomes more expandable when stories begin exploring what exists around the central narrative: what happened before the protagonists, what occurs simultaneously elsewhere, and what may happen afterward.

A distinction must be made between expanding a franchise and expanding its universe. More films, games, series, protagonists, and narratives do not necessarily make the world larger if they continue using the same places, institutions, and conflicts. Universe expansion means increasing the set of events and narratives that can plausibly occur within the setting by adding or developing different regions, historical periods, institutions, cultures, systems, and relationships.

Tolkien's Middle-earth illustrates this principle: the War of the Ring is only one period within a much longer history. Westeros similarly contains histories preceding Game of Thrones, including Aegon's Conquest, the Dance of the Dragons, and Robert's Rebellion. RPG settings such as Forgotten Realms and The Elder Scrolls make the same principle explicit by allowing different characters, campaigns, regions, and periods to coexist within a recognizable shared world. The setting becomes larger than any individual narrative.

Every narrative presupposes a world governed by rules determining what can happen, who inhabits it, how they live, and which causal relationships are coherent. The relevant distinction is whether the world exists primarily to serve one story or whether the story is one manifestation of a world with independent structure.

In a story-centered setting, the audience learns what is necessary to understand the characters and their conflicts, so continuation naturally focuses on the same characters, conflicts, or aftermath. In a universe-centered setting, rules, institutions, cultures, geography, and history have enough independent existence that protagonists, regions, and periods can change without destroying the setting's identity.

Middle-earth exemplifies the latter. Frodo did not create Mordor, Aragorn did not create Gondor, and Gandalf did not create the histories of Middle-earth's peoples. Removing the characters of The Lord of the Rings still leaves thousands of years of wars, cultures, kingdoms, peoples, and transformations. Stories can move into the First Age, Númenor, or smaller events elsewhere in Middle-earth because the known narrative is only a window into a larger world.

The ability to support multiple works therefore depends less on the magnitude of the original story than on the perceived magnitude and consistency of the world surrounding it.

Tolkien's worldbuilding creates the impression that the protagonists arrive late in an already-existing history. The First and Second Ages have passed, Númenor has existed and fallen, Sauron's rise predates Frodo, and major wars occurred before the main characters were born. This temporal depth permits stories to move backward, forward, or sideways through history while remaining recognizably part of Middle-earth.

George R. R. Martin applies a similar structure to Westeros. Game of Thrones is a particular story within Westerosi history rather than the history itself. Because Aegon's Conquest, the Dance of the Dragons, and Robert's Rebellion already constitute distinct historical periods, House of the Dragon can tell a fundamentally different story without being a direct continuation of Game of Thrones.

Harry Potter already possesses the foundations for comparable expansion. Hogwarts predates Harry by centuries; the Ministry of Magic, wizarding families, magical schools, traditions, and communities already have independent histories. These elements permit stories set in other eras, communities, and institutions, including events merely referenced in the original books or aspects of wizarding society that the original narrative only needed to establish superficially.

RPGs particularly demonstrate the distinction because they require worlds capable of accommodating player decisions, exploration, multiple paths, and events beyond a single predetermined narrative.

Forgotten Realms contains regions, cities, gods, planes, peoples, organizations, historical conflicts, and characters accumulated across decades. Its depth allows independent stories in Waterdeep, Baldur's Gate, Icewind Dale, Candlekeep, and elsewhere, with different protagonists, eras, regions, and scales. Candlekeep can anchor a story without becoming the boundary of Forgotten Realms. A narrative can begin there, move to another city or continent, and occur centuries later while remaining part of the same universe.

Hogwarts could occupy an analogous position within the Wizarding World: a major, recognizable institution and narrative anchor without being the entire setting.

The Elder Scrolls demonstrates an even stronger form of independence. Skyrim has a main story centered on the Dragonborn, but the world does not require the player to follow that story. The player can join guilds, explore ruins, participate in political conflicts, investigate local stories, encounter different peoples, or travel without advancing the central narrative.

This works because Skyrim is a province of Tamriel rather than merely the story of the Dragonborn. The protagonist can be essential to particular events without being essential to the continued existence of the society, its institutions, peoples, conflicts, and other stories.

Consequently, a future Elder Scrolls work can select another region, period, and protagonist without needing to revive or directly continue the Dragonborn's story. A story-centered franchise must find a way to continue; a universe-centered franchise can simply choose where to look next.

An expandable universe does not necessarily require an encyclopedia-sized accumulation of names, events, characters, and facts. Depth can come from relationships between elements rather than from their quantity.

Forgotten Realms demonstrates both the value and potential cost of accumulated lore: decades of additions create historical depth but can also make entry more intimidating. A more important objective than descriptive quantity may be generative capacity.

Descriptive lore explains what happened. Generative lore establishes rules, institutions, interests, causal relationships, and conflicts from which new events can naturally emerge. A chronology containing a thousand wars can be impressive, but institutions with independent interests and consistent interactions may provide greater capacity for generating new narratives.

Avatar: The Last Airbender illustrates this principle through relatively simple foundational rules: four elements and distinct bending traditions. Those rules generate different cultures, religions, martial arts, technologies, institutions, and political conflicts. Simplicity of underlying rules can coexist with substantial world depth because audiences can understand the rules and observe their consequences across societies.

Thus, expanding a universe does not require endlessly increasing lore. It can increase the number of stories generated by the rules and structures already present. Complexity can emerge through interaction rather than accumulation.

Universe expansion does not imply unlimited freedom. New works can introduce elements, reinterpret situations, and adapt established material while retaining the recognizable logic of the setting. The objective is not immutable fidelity to every detail but continuity of the structures that allow the audience to recognize the new story as belonging to the same world.

Peter Jackson's adaptation of The Lord of the Rings changed significant elements, including characters, events, and narrative structure, but retained the fundamental identity of Middle-earth. Adaptation demonstrates that a universe can change in form without losing its underlying logic.

This becomes more important when stories move further away from the original characters and events. The farther a narrative travels from familiar protagonists, the more its connection to the shared universe depends on rules, history, institutions, and structures.

Star Wars illustrates the potential. Its expanded universe developed thousands of years of history, multiple eras, republics, empires, Jedi, Sith, wars, civilizations, planets, and characters that did not directly depend on the original trilogy. Luke Skywalker was central to one Star Wars narrative, but Star Wars did not require Luke to exist. Historical continuity acted as infrastructure for new stories.

Large-scale continuity also creates a management problem: the more established material exists, the greater the need to manage consistency. New works may add layers, reinterpret elements, or establish different continuities, but the critical question is whether these changes expand the universe's possibilities or undermine the shared structures that make it function as a universe.

Healthy expansion therefore adds possibilities without making prior material irrelevant. The farther a story can move while still feeling like the same world, the greater the universe's expansion capacity.

The Wizarding World already contains many of the components necessary for expansion: magical schools, the Ministry of Magic, Gringotts, St. Mungo's, families, creatures, artifacts, professions, potions, magical disciplines, communities, relationships with Muggles, and centuries of history.

These elements can generate new perspectives without requiring an entirely new layer of invented lore. A profession can become a narrative perspective; an institution can generate conflicts; another school can reveal another magical culture; a historical period can support an independent story. Creators working with established IP do not need every future game mechanic or narrative structure to have been explicitly specified by the original author. The original work can provide rules, concepts, and foundations from which designers and writers develop coherent systems and stories.

The conceptual shift from “Harry Potter” to “Wizarding World” is therefore substantive rather than merely semantic. A Harry Potter-centered franchise asks what Harry's next story is. A Wizarding World-centered universe asks what other stories exist within this world.

Fantastic Beasts demonstrates both the possibility and limitation of changing protagonists and periods. It moved to another protagonist and historical period but remained strongly connected to familiar characters, institutions, and conflicts. Changing the story does not automatically constitute expansion of the universe.

A genuinely broader conception permits Harry to remain Harry, Hogwarts to remain Hogwarts, and the original stories to remain intact while allowing new protagonists, periods, regions, institutions, families, professions, and communities to become narrative centers. Hogwarts can remain one of the world's principal anchors without functioning as its boundary, just as Candlekeep is important to Forgotten Realms without representing the entire setting.

Potential stories could begin at Hogwarts and move elsewhere, occur at another magical school, take place centuries before the books, follow a family or profession, or center on an institution or community previously peripheral to the narrative. None requires Harry Potter to appear.

The strategic opportunity is therefore not to reduce Harry Potter but to position it as one major story within the Wizarding World. The original narrative becomes a gateway into the universe rather than the boundary of what the universe can contain.

The relevant question surrounding Hogwarts Legacy 2 is therefore not simply what remains to be done at Hogwarts, because the sequel may itself make Hogwarts only one component of a much broader experience. The more fundamental question is what can be done with the world beyond Hogwarts.

The Wizarding World could be developed through stronger canonical architecture connecting institutions, regions, cultures, rules, and historical events. The objective would not be lore accumulation for its own sake but development of relationships and structures capable of supporting independent stories.

Fan-created fiction, characters, and stories provide an informal indication that the world can generate possibilities beyond the books, although this does not establish measurable commercial demand for any particular form of expansion. The relevant observation is that the conclusion of Harry's story did not eliminate people's capacity to imagine stories within the world he introduced.

The central distinction is therefore between continuing a story and expanding a universe. Middle-earth is not exhausted by Frodo; Westeros is not exhausted by Jon Snow; Tamriel does not depend on the Dragonborn; Forgotten Realms survives the end of individual campaigns; Star Wars is larger than Luke Skywalker's story. Their characters pass through portions of worlds that possess histories, structures, and possibilities independent of them.

Harry Potter can similarly conclude without the Wizarding World concluding. Harry was a gateway into the universe, and Hogwarts was a principal window through which that universe became familiar. The larger the world becomes, the less the original story functions as a limit and the more it functions as an entry point. A story can end without the world that contains it needing to end.
