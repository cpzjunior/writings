The introduction frames the announcement of Hogwarts Legacy 2 as a question about the limits of Hogwarts as the primary setting rather than as a judgment about the sequel itself. Hogwarts Legacy demonstrated that a Harry Potter game could avoid the original protagonists by placing the player in another historical period as a new Hogwarts student, using the school and its surroundings as the recognizable foundation of the experience. However, relying heavily on Hogwarts creates a potential constraint: a franchise can move beyond its original characters while remaining dependent on its original places. Hogwarts is only one part of the Wizarding World, which also contains institutions, professions, communities, traditions, magical systems, conflicts, creatures, and centuries of history. The broader opportunity is therefore to develop the world beyond Hogwarts so that it can independently sustain new protagonists, settings, and periods, as occurs in fictional universes that remain capable of generating stories long after their original narratives end.

A narrative centered on a protagonist can be excellent without constituting an expansive universe, because the protagonist provides the main gateway into the setting and concentrates its conflicts. Harry Potter illustrates this structure: the audience discovers the wizarding world primarily through Harry's experiences. A universe becomes more autonomous when stories begin exploring what existed before the main narrative, what occurs elsewhere simultaneously, and what can happen afterward. Franchise expansion and universe expansion are therefore not equivalent. A franchise can produce many new works, protagonists, and stories while continuing to use the same locations, institutions, and conflicts, whereas genuine universe expansion increases the range of events that can plausibly occur by adding or developing places, historical periods, institutions, cultures, systems, and relationships. Tolkien's Middle-earth, Westeros, and RPG settings such as Forgotten Realms and The Elder Scrolls demonstrate this principle by supporting different characters, campaigns, regions, and eras within recognizable underlying worlds.

Every narrative necessarily operates within a world governed by assumptions about what is possible, who inhabits it, how they live, and how cause and effect operates, but the extent to which that world exists independently of the narrative varies. In a story whose setting primarily serves the plot, the world contains what is necessary to understand the characters and conflicts, making continuation naturally dependent on those same elements. In a universe with an independent existence, the story is instead one manifestation of a larger structure containing its own rules, institutions, cultures, geography, and history, allowing protagonists, regions, and periods to change without destroying the work's identity. Middle-earth exemplifies this distinction because the War of the Ring is only one period within a much larger history containing thousands of years of events, cultures, wars, peoples, and kingdoms; Frodo, Aragorn, and Gandalf are participants in that world rather than its creators. Expandable universes therefore require not merely an important story but enough coherent surrounding structure for other stories to emerge naturally, making the perceived size of the world more important than the size of any individual narrative.

Tolkien's worldbuilding creates temporal depth by making the characters feel as though they arrive late in a history that has already experienced major eras, civilizations, conflicts, and transformations. Because the First and Second Ages, Númenor, Sauron's earlier rise, and numerous wars precede the main narrative, later stories can move backward or forward through time or explore parallel events without losing the identity of Middle-earth. Westeros operates similarly: Game of Thrones is one period in a much longer history that includes Aegon's Conquest, the Dance of the Dragons, and Robert's Rebellion, allowing House of the Dragon to tell a different story in the same world rather than simply continuing Game of Thrones. Harry Potter already contains foundations for comparable expansion: Hogwarts predates Harry by centuries, the Ministry of Magic and wizarding families have their own histories, and multiple schools, traditions, and magical communities exist independently of the books' central events. These elements provide potential directions for expansion through different eras, communities, institutions, historical events, peripheral characters, and aspects of wizarding society that the original narrative only needed to establish superficially.

RPGs provide a particularly clear demonstration of the distinction between a story and a story-generating world because they require settings capable of supporting player decisions, exploration, alternative paths, and situations beyond a single linear plot. Forgotten Realms illustrates this through its accumulation of regions, cities, gods, planes, peoples, organizations, historical conflicts, and characters, with different adventures able to occur in Waterdeep, Baldur's Gate, Icewind Dale, Candlekeep, or elsewhere while remaining recognizably part of the same setting. Candlekeep is particularly useful as an analogy for Hogwarts: it can be a highly distinctive and important location capable of supporting stories, but it is not synonymous with Forgotten Realms and therefore does not define the setting's boundaries. Hogwarts could similarly remain one of the Wizarding World's major institutions while functioning as an anchor within a larger universe rather than as the boundary of what that universe can contain.

The Elder Scrolls, particularly Skyrim, demonstrates how a universe can remain independent even within an individual game's narrative. Although the Dragonborn has a central role in the main story, players can spend extensive time pursuing guilds, local conflicts, exploration, political events, ruins, and other activities without following that central plot, because Skyrim exists as a province within the larger world of Tamriel and contains its own societies, institutions, histories, and conflicts. The protagonist can therefore be essential to particular events without being essential to the continued existence of the setting. This structure gives the franchise considerable narrative flexibility: a future installment can select another region, period, or protagonist without requiring the Dragonborn to return or directly continue the previous story. A story-centered franchise generally needs to find a mechanism for continuation, whereas a universe-centered franchise can simply select another part of an already existing world as the basis for the next narrative.

Depth should not be confused with the quantity of lore. A world does not necessarily become more expandable merely by accumulating names, dates, characters, and historical events; what matters is also how its elements interact and whether they can generate new situations. Forgotten Realms demonstrates both sides of this issue: its decades of accumulated material create substantial historical depth for existing fans but can also make the setting more intimidating to newcomers. An expandable universe therefore does not need to function as an encyclopedia; it needs structures capable of generating stories. Descriptive lore records what happened, while generative lore establishes rules, institutions, relationships, incentives, and conflicts from which new events can naturally emerge. Avatar: The Last Airbender illustrates this through a relatively simple foundational system of four elements and bending traditions that nevertheless generates different cultures, religions, martial practices, technologies, institutions, and political conflicts. The relevant form of complexity comes less from endlessly accumulating information than from allowing a manageable set of rules and structures to interact in ways that produce many possible narratives.

Expanding an established universe requires more than reusing recognizable elements, but it also does not require absolute preservation of every existing detail. New works inevitably reinterpret, rearrange, condense, or add to established material, while still needing to preserve enough of the underlying logic for audiences to recognize the shared world. Peter Jackson's adaptation of The Lord of the Rings changed characters, events, and narrative structure while retaining the elements that made Middle-earth recognizable, demonstrating how adaptation can alter a story without replacing the fundamental identity of its universe. Star Wars provides another example of the potential of historical continuity: its expanded material explored different eras, planets, civilizations, Jedi, Sith, wars, and political structures far removed from the original trilogy, demonstrating that Luke Skywalker could be central to one Star Wars story without being necessary for Star Wars itself to exist. Large-scale expansion also creates a continuity-management problem, because new works must decide how to relate to established material through addition, reinterpretation, or new continuities. The central principle is that expansion should increase the possibilities available within the universe while preserving enough shared structure for different works to remain recognizably connected.

Harry Potter already contains many foundations for a much broader universe, including magical schools, the Ministry of Magic, Gringotts, St. Mungo's, families, creatures, artifacts, professions, potions, magical disciplines, communities, relationships with Muggles, and centuries of history. These elements can support new narratives without requiring completely new lore: professions can become narrative perspectives, institutions can generate conflicts, schools can reveal different magical cultures, and historical periods can support independent stories. Game designers, screenwriters, and other creators can derive new systems and narrative possibilities from the rules and concepts established by the original work without requiring every detail to have been explicitly designed for those purposes; Hogwarts, for example, can function as a game hub even though it was not originally conceived as an RPG system. The important conceptual shift is from thinking primarily in terms of Harry Potter to thinking in terms of the Wizarding World: the first framing naturally asks what happens to Harry next, while the second asks what other stories can occur in the same world. Fantastic Beasts demonstrates both the possibility and limitation of this approach, since it changes protagonist and period while remaining substantially connected to familiar characters, institutions, and conflicts. A broader Wizarding World could retain Harry and Hogwarts while also introducing new protagonists, eras, regions, schools, professions, families, institutions, and communities, making Hogwarts an important anchor comparable to Candlekeep rather than treating it as the universe's boundary.

The announcement of Hogwarts Legacy 2 therefore raises a broader question than what remains to be done at Hogwarts: what can be done with the world beyond Hogwarts? The sequel may itself expand substantially beyond the first game's scope, but the larger opportunity is to treat the Wizarding World as a universe with enough canonical architecture, institutional relationships, regional differences, cultural structures, rules, and historical continuity to support many independent stories. J.K. Rowling could contribute to such development alongside writers, designers, and other creators, while the objective would be less about accumulating lore than about developing the interactions among the world's existing elements. The long-running fan practice of creating fanfiction, characters, and stories beyond the books does not establish measurable commercial demand for any particular form of expansion, but it does demonstrate that audiences can continue imagining possibilities after Harry's story ends. The broader pattern appears in Middle-earth, Westeros, Tamriel, Forgotten Realms, and Star Wars, where individual protagonists occupy only portions of worlds that remain larger than their narratives. Harry Potter can similarly function as the gateway into the Wizarding World and Hogwarts as its principal window without either defining its limits; the potential of the franchise lies in allowing the original story to end while the world continues to generate new stories.
