I enjoy watching videos about what to do after winning the lottery because they often begin as fantasies and end as case studies in failure: fortunes consumed, families divided, impulsive decisions, excessive concentration, and failed businesses. Although I rarely play the lottery, these cases provide a useful thought experiment about how wealth can be destroyed and how those failures might be prevented. This led me to consider how I would architect R$ 100 million as a solution architect rather than beginning with the question of where to invest. As with software architecture, the process should begin with requirements, properties, boundaries, dependencies, and trade-offs, with individual investments treated as implementation components rather than the starting point. The exercise deliberately excludes detailed legal, tax, and regulatory analysis because those require specialized expertise; instead, it explores how systems-architecture concepts can inform the structure of complex wealth. At this scale, wealth becomes a system that must fulfill requirements, withstand failures, control access, preserve information, distribute responsibilities, and remain functional as people and circumstances change.

Every architecture begins with requirements. In this hypothetical scenario, the functional requirements are to sustain a standard of living without recurring consumption of principal, support family members without compromising continuity, preserve sufficient capital to withstand different scenarios, and create the conditions for a legacy to future generations. These requirements are specific to the hypothetical case rather than universal principles, since different people may prioritize consumption, liquidity, long-term preservation, risk tolerance, family responsibilities, or different time horizons. Non-functional requirements are equally important: resilience, liquidity, reduced concentration, security, governance, manageability, and the ability to evolve. A system can satisfy a primary financial objective while remaining poorly architected—for example, by depending excessively on one institution, being too illiquid, becoming too complex to govern, or concentrating exposure in a single jurisdiction, currency, or economic assumption. Architecture therefore involves conflicting requirements and trade-offs: liquidity can reduce return potential, redundancy adds cost and complexity, security introduces friction, and diversification can reduce observability. There is no universally optimal configuration; the appropriate architecture depends on requirements, constraints, risks, and chosen trade-offs.

Once requirements are defined, wealth can be considered as a set of components with distinct responsibilities rather than as one undifferentiated pool. Liquidity, income generation, preservation, growth, and business ownership are different concerns and may warrant different components, but separation should remain proportional to complexity. Simple wealth may be adequately represented by something like a modular monolith, while substantial wealth involving multiple stakeholders, asset types, jurisdictions, businesses, and generations may justify clearer boundaries. A family holding company can be understood architecturally as an ownership and governance boundary, while a family office represents a different responsibility centered on management, administration, coordination, and governance; neither is automatically necessary, and their concrete implementation depends on legal, tax, succession, and regulatory considerations outside this exercise. The important architectural distinction is between ownership, management, and governance. Every boundary creates costs, dependencies, and governance requirements, so structures should not be added merely because they are possible. The relevant question is what responsibility each component has and why that responsibility needs to be separated.

After defining components, the architecture must consider failure modes. Diversification is better understood initially as risk management rather than as a checklist of asset classes: the critical issue is identifying dependencies and determining which failures could compromise the system. Dependence on one asset class, institution, currency, jurisdiction, or decision-maker can create critical vulnerabilities. Threat modeling provides a useful analogy: identify relevant assets, threats, vulnerabilities, and impacts, then distinguish ordinary component failures from failures capable of compromising the entire system. A single point of failure is a component whose failure has disproportionate consequences, such as a concentrated asset, a critical institution, an administrator holding exclusive knowledge, or undocumented rules existing only in the founder's memory. Redundancy can reduce dependence on individual components, but numerical diversification is insufficient when supposedly separate assets share the same underlying risk. International exposure and different asset classes can reduce dependencies on a single jurisdiction, currency, economy, or market behavior, but they do not eliminate all correlated risks. Fault isolation complements redundancy by limiting blast radius: higher-risk components can exist provided their failure does not compromise essential functions such as expenses, obligations, or preservation. Separate liquidity reserves for the individual, the holding structure, and operating companies can prevent an operational crisis in one component from becoming a crisis for the entire wealth system. Redundancy reduces dependence on specific components, while isolation limits propagation when failures occur; resilience requires both.

Security is an architectural concern because the central question is not merely where assets are held but who can do what. Least privilege means each participant receives only the access necessary for their role, while separation of duties prevents ownership, custody, authorization, and execution from being concentrated in a single person or credential. Trust boundaries also matter because family members, administrators, institutions, managers, companies, and jurisdictions may operate under different assumptions about identity, authority, and responsibility. A resilient structure should make clear who has access, what powers they possess, how those powers can be revoked, and what happens when someone leaves a role. Defense in depth means combining multiple protective layers—governance, segregation of responsibilities, institutional and geographic diversification, documentation, access controls, liquidity, and succession mechanisms—rather than relying on any one control. Diversification cannot solve governance problems, a holding company cannot solve operational security, a custodian cannot replace access policies, documentation cannot replace segregation of duties, and succession rules cannot fully compensate for lost operational knowledge. The purpose of these layers is not to make mistakes impossible but to prevent one error, compromised credential, or unavailable individual from compromising the entire system.

The founder is itself a component and can become a critical dependency. A structure may initially operate efficiently because one person knows where everything is, understands the rules, controls access, knows the professionals involved, and makes all decisions, but this simplicity becomes fragility when the system depends on that person's continued availability. The relevant continuity questions include who can understand and operate the structure if the founder dies, becomes incapacitated, loses information, or otherwise stops performing the role; who can access required information; who can make decisions; which powers need to transfer; which obligations remain; and which rules exist only in personal memory. This is analogous to business continuity and disaster recovery. The same issue becomes more significant when wealth is intended for future children and generations, because the architecture must accommodate people who do not yet exist and cannot reasonably be assumed to share the founder's characteristics or preferences. Governance therefore needs to scale as participants, family branches, spouses, children, grandchildren, wealth, and divergent interests increase. Too much formalization can create unnecessary bureaucracy, while too little can leave the system dependent on tacit knowledge and personal relationships. The objective is not to eliminate human judgment but to prevent the system from depending permanently on one person or on knowledge that disappears with them. The most significant change a long-lived wealth architecture must accommodate may therefore be generational transition.

Any architecture faces the opposing risks of underengineering and overengineering. An underengineered wealth structure may be too concentrated, illiquid, dependent on one person, or otherwise unable to absorb foreseeable failures; it may work perfectly under normal conditions while failing when an assumption changes. An overengineered structure can accumulate so many entities, jurisdictions, institutions, accounts, rules, and processes that complexity itself becomes a source of risk by creating interfaces, dependencies, and cognitive burdens that are difficult to understand and manage. Complexity should not be mistaken for protection merely because a structure is difficult to explain. Each component should have a clear responsibility, each redundancy should reduce a relevant dependency, each control should mitigate a concrete risk, and each layer should contribute a desired property. Conversely, simplicity is not automatically resilient: removing a layer can increase failure impact, and insufficient governance can make a system dependent on informal knowledge and relationships. The central architectural trade-off is therefore between complexity and resilience, with additional components carrying operational, cognitive, and financial costs. Good architecture is neither maximal simplicity nor maximal sophistication; it is complexity justified by the requirements and risks the system must address.

A wealth architecture should also be evolutionary rather than treated as a permanent configuration. Family composition, wealth size, asset relevance, jurisdictions, economic conditions, regulatory environments, technology, and individual circumstances all change, so the initial architecture is only one version of the system based on the requirements and assumptions known at that time. Evolution does not mean constant restructuring; it means deliberately changing the architecture when the assumptions supporting previous decisions cease to hold. Architectural deterioration can occur without any component becoming intrinsically defective: an appreciated asset can become an unintended concentration, an institution can gradually become responsible for too many functions, or a structure designed for a small family can become inadequate as generations enter. Observability is therefore important. As with technology systems, the wealth structure should provide sufficient visibility into resource distribution, concentrations, institutional dependencies, currencies, jurisdictions, liquidity sources, volatility, and other relevant dependencies to identify when the system is diverging from its intended state. This creates a feedback loop in which requirements and assumptions guide the architecture, operation produces results, observation reveals system behavior, and new information can trigger deliberate adaptation. The objective is not to find a perfect configuration and preserve it indefinitely, but to create a system that can be understood, observed, and modified without requiring complete reconstruction whenever relevant conditions change.

The central conclusion is that architecture cannot eliminate risk; it can only structure uncertainty so that foreseeable failures have limited impact, critical dependencies are visible, responsibilities are clear, and changes in assumptions do not automatically compromise the whole system. For wealth intended to persist over decades, maximizing each component's individual return may be less important than preserving the system's ability to survive the scenarios that matter, because an otherwise attractive asset may be inappropriate if its concentration conflicts with broader requirements. Future children cannot be predicted in terms of their interests, professions, choices, or needs, so the objective should not be to design their lives in advance but to create a sufficiently resilient and flexible system capable of accommodating them. Legacy therefore means more than transferring assets: it means leaving a structure capable of converting those assets into opportunities without destroying the capital that makes those opportunities possible. The decisive architectural test is whether the system continues functioning when the original architect is no longer its operator. In that sense, owning wealth means possessing resources, while having a wealth architecture means deliberately structuring those resources so they can continue fulfilling their purpose through economic changes, individual failures, changing family structures, and new generations. The objective is not to predict the future precisely, but to create a system capable of functioning when the future differs from what was imagined.
