Cloud selection should be driven by workload characteristics rather than familiarity, provider size, or default preference. AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud remain the dominant general-purpose hyperscalers because they integrate compute, storage, databases, networking, containers, serverless, analytics, AI, security, and observability, but the market also includes providers with strong regional, ecosystem-specific, simplified, or specialized propositions. OCI, IBM Cloud, Alibaba Cloud, and Tencent Cloud can align strongly with particular ecosystems or geographies; DigitalOcean, Hetzner, OVHcloud, Scaleway, and Akamai/Linode can offer simpler infrastructure with potentially more predictable or competitive costs; Heroku, Render, and Railway provide higher-level application platforms; and GPU-focused providers such as CoreWeave can be more competitive for AI workloads where accelerator availability, architecture, pricing, location, interconnect, and scaling matter more than breadth of managed services. AI demand is increasing the importance of specialized infrastructure, while hyperscalers simultaneously compete through proprietary hardware, models, managed AI services, and ecosystem integration. A provider does not need to replace the primary cloud: a workload-specific alternative can coexist with a dominant platform.

Major clouds provide recognizable service correspondences: AWS EC2, Azure Virtual Machines, and Google Compute Engine; S3, Blob Storage, and Cloud Storage; VPC, Virtual Network, and Google VPC; EKS, AKS, and GKE; ECR, Azure Container Registry, and Artifact Registry; Elastic Load Balancing, Azure Load Balancer, and Cloud Load Balancing; CloudFront, Azure Front Door, and Cloud CDN; RDS, Azure Database services, and Cloud SQL; DynamoDB, Cosmos DB, and Firestore; Redshift, Synapse, and BigQuery; EFS, Azure Files, and Filestore; SQS, Service Bus, and Pub/Sub; IAM, Entra ID, and Cloud IAM; Secrets Manager, Key Vault, and Secret Manager; CloudWatch, Azure Monitor, and Cloud Monitoring; Lambda, Azure Functions, and Cloud Run functions; Fargate, Azure Container Apps, and Cloud Run; CloudFormation/ARM/Bicep; Terraform as a cross-provider provisioning approach; SageMaker, Azure Machine Learning, and Vertex AI; and Bedrock, Azure AI Foundry, and Vertex AI for model consumption and AI applications. These correspondences create a common vocabulary and reduce the learning cost of changing clouds, but similar service categories do not imply interchangeable behavior, architecture, pricing, quotas, consistency, access control, replication, integrations, operational experience, or feature sets. Differences become larger at higher abstraction levels, and proprietary services such as DynamoDB, BigQuery, Cosmos DB, or Cloud Run can create substantially greater architectural dependency. AI platforms differ especially in available models, accelerators, APIs, training capabilities, data pipelines, vector databases, managed services, regional availability, pricing, and capacity. An equivalence matrix therefore answers where a familiar concept exists, not which provider is appropriate for a specific workload.

Single-cloud architecture has a major simplicity advantage: fewer technologies, integrations, operational models, security and identity systems, networking patterns, observability mechanisms, billing models, and troubleshooting environments. For organizations without a concrete multi-cloud requirement, this reduction in complexity can outweigh the benefits of additional provider diversity. Terraform can reduce provisioning differences and allow shared modules, pipelines, and practices, but it abstracts infrastructure declaration rather than provider behavior; an AWS VPC, Azure Virtual Network, and Google VPC remain materially different resources. New provider-specific services can also outpace mature infrastructure-as-code support. Deliberate portability has a cost: restricting an application to services with close equivalents across providers can prevent use of services that would otherwise improve simplicity, cost, performance, or productivity. Portability should therefore be treated as a property with an engineering cost, not as a free benefit.

Multi-cloud should not be equated automatically with resilience. A workload split between providers can still have shared critical dependencies in DNS, identity, CDN, connectivity, observability, SaaS, or other cross-cutting services. Cloudflare's June 2022 outage illustrates how a failure at a shared infrastructure layer can affect applications regardless of where compute runs. Supplier diversity is therefore not necessarily dependency diversity. Resilience requires examining the complete dependency chain, not merely distributing compute across providers. Conversely, multi-cloud can have valid motivations unrelated to availability, including commercial negotiations, regulation, data residency, specialized services, GPU capacity, or cost. A single cloud distributed appropriately across regions and availability zones can provide sufficient resilience for many workloads. Single-cloud and multi-cloud are architectural strategies with different costs, benefits, and risks rather than ideological positions.

Smaller and specialized clouds can be attractive because they do not attempt to provide everything. Workloads requiring mainly virtual machines, storage, networking, or Kubernetes may gain little from the complexity of a hyperscaler's enormous service catalog. Providers such as Hetzner, OVHcloud, DigitalOcean, Scaleway, and Akamai/Linode may therefore compete through simplicity and infrastructure economics. Higher-level platforms such as Heroku, Render, and Railway can provide value by abstracting infrastructure further. For GPU-intensive workloads, the relevant question can shift from "which is my default cloud?" to which provider supplies the required accelerator, quantity, location, capacity, and economics. A specialized provider can serve only one workload while the rest of the organization remains on its primary cloud. Thus, an intermediate strategy is possible: a primary cloud plus targeted external providers where they provide a concrete advantage. A smaller provider's differentiation may be offering fewer capabilities but matching the actual workload with greater simplicity, specialization, or cost efficiency.

Cloud lock-in is multidimensional. Technological lock-in increases with dependence on provider-specific services; an architecture based on containers, PostgreSQL, and object storage generally has more migration options than one deeply dependent on proprietary services. Operational lock-in also arises through internal expertise, processes, automation, observability, deployment pipelines, and organizational knowledge. Economic lock-in can be significant because moving large datasets may incur transfer costs, time, and operational disruption even when the application itself is technically portable. Completely avoiding lock-in is therefore not necessarily rational: a managed proprietary service may substantially reduce operational complexity or improve cost, performance, availability, or productivity, making the dependency an acceptable trade-off. The relevant objective is conscious dependency management: understand which dependencies are strategic, how difficult they would be to replace, how long and costly migration would be, and which architectural components they affect. Lock-in should be a deliberate decision rather than an accidental discovery at migration time.

Cloud evaluation must include the cost of implementing, operating, and changing the architecture, not only the recurring infrastructure bill. Technically equivalent resources can require different engineering effort because of differences in integrations, managed services, automation, networking, identity, security, observability, and deployment. Multi-cloud adds further operational surface area and may require abstraction layers for unified monitoring, security, or management. Terraform reduces provisioning effort but does not remove the underlying platform differences. Team knowledge is another cost: operating multiple clouds expands the knowledge surface required for troubleshooting, incident response, security, and change management. Migration costs also extend beyond compute to data, configurations, identities, networks, pipelines, observability, integrations, and operational processes. Architecture cost should therefore be evaluated at three stages: implementation, operation, and eventual change. A technically cheaper resource can become economically inferior if it requires substantially more engineering, while a more expensive managed service can be rational when its operational savings exceed its price premium.

Compliance and regulation can eliminate providers before price or feature comparisons begin. Requirements vary by industry, jurisdiction, data type, security, privacy, residency, auditing, and business continuity. Hyperscalers generally provide extensive certifications, regions, encryption, key management, audit logging, identity controls, and regulatory tooling, while smaller or regional providers may have different geographic and certification profiles. Provider certification does not automatically make an application compliant: compliance remains dependent on how the customer uses security, access control, encryption, logging, retention, and other controls under the shared-responsibility model. Data location must include not only primary storage but also backups, logs, managed services, support, and integrations, which may have different geographic characteristics. Compliance can therefore act as an eligibility filter before cost, performance, portability, or feature comparisons.

Cloud pricing cannot be evaluated reliably from public list prices alone. Reserved Instances, Savings Plans, committed-use discounts, enterprise contracts, volume, credits, startup/research/education programs, migration incentives, and partnerships can materially alter actual costs. Free tiers and promotional credits are useful for experimentation but do not necessarily represent production economics. A temporary credit can reduce entry cost without making the platform cheaper over time. Likewise, comparing an isolated resource such as a GPU can be misleading because total workload cost also includes storage, data transfer, networking, load balancing, observability, and other dependencies. The relevant metric is total workload cost under the actual commercial conditions, not the list price of a single resource.

Cloud selection should begin with workload requirements: required services; data location; total cost including transfer; availability and resilience requirements; existing team skills; acceptable lock-in; regulatory constraints; provider-specific capabilities that materially benefit the workload; and the effort required to implement, operate, and eventually migrate the architecture. Infrastructure price is only one component. A cheaper compute resource can become more expensive after engineering and operational costs, while portability can itself require additional engineering. Conversely, avoiding provider-specific dependencies solely for hypothetical future migration can impose real costs immediately. Discounts, credits, free tiers, and contracts should therefore be incorporated into the total-cost analysis rather than treated as substitutes for recurring economics.

There is no universal optimal cloud or universal optimal number of clouds. A single cloud can be appropriate when integration and simplicity dominate; multiple providers can be justified by concrete requirements; a smaller provider can fit a specialized workload; and a technically sophisticated architecture can add complexity without proportional benefit. Cloud selection is a trade-off among simplicity, cost, portability, lock-in, availability, resilience, compliance, and operational complexity. The objective is not to eliminate trade-offs or use every available provider, but to understand and deliberately accept the relevant ones. Familiarity remains a legitimate factor, but knowing the alternatives transforms the default provider from an automatic answer into a conscious choice.
