Historically, the author preferred AWS because familiarity with its services, organization, documentation, and ecosystem made it feel like the simplest choice. The growing importance of GPUs for AI workloads challenged that default by making price, availability, hardware, geographic location, scalability, and commercial terms more important. This led to a broader view of the cloud market, which includes hyperscalers, regional providers, simpler infrastructure platforms, and specialized GPU providers. The goal is not to identify a universally superior cloud, but to understand the main players, their strengths and service equivalents, and when alternatives to the obvious choice are worth considering. Familiarity can be a valid reason to choose a cloud, but it should not substitute for understanding the available alternatives.

AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud remain the principal general-purpose public clouds because they provide comprehensive ecosystems covering compute, storage, databases, networking, containers, Kubernetes, serverless, analytics, AI, security, and observability. Their integrated service catalogs are a major advantage but can also increase migration complexity. Other providers have more specific strengths: Oracle Cloud Infrastructure aligns naturally with Oracle environments, IBM Cloud with large organizations and Red Hat, and Alibaba Cloud and Tencent Cloud have particular relevance in China and Asia. Providers such as DigitalOcean, Hetzner, OVHcloud, Scaleway, and Akamai/Linode deliberately offer simpler infrastructure propositions that can be attractive when workloads mainly need compute, storage, and networking without extensive managed-service dependencies. AI and GPU demand are further diversifying the market because hardware availability, accelerator architecture, price, interconnects, scalability, and location can matter more than the breadth of a service catalog; specialized providers such as CoreWeave therefore compete for particular AI and high-performance workloads while hyperscalers simultaneously invest in specialized hardware, foundation models, managed AI services, and ecosystem integration. Consequently, an organization does not necessarily need to use the same provider for every workload: a hyperscaler may be appropriate for general infrastructure while a specialized or smaller provider may be advantageous for a particular GPU, regional, or cost-sensitive workload. The relevant question is therefore not which cloud is universally best, but which infrastructure fits each workload.

Major clouds provide recognizable equivalents for many fundamental capabilities: EC2, Azure Virtual Machines, and Compute Engine for compute; S3, Blob Storage, and Cloud Storage for object storage; VPC, Virtual Network, and Google Cloud VPC for networking; EKS, AKS, and GKE for Kubernetes; and corresponding services for registries, load balancing, CDNs, databases, messaging, identity, secrets, observability, serverless, containers, infrastructure as code, and AI/ML. This correspondence gives engineers familiar with one platform a useful vocabulary for learning another and makes high-level architectural comparison easier. However, similar service categories do not imply equivalent behavior, architecture, operations, pricing, quotas, consistency, access control, replication, supported engines, extensions, integrations, or migration characteristics. Differences become even more significant at higher abstraction levels, and each cloud also has services without direct equivalents, such as DynamoDB, BigQuery, Cosmos DB, or Cloud Run, which can create stronger architectural dependencies. AI services illustrate the issue further because providers differ in available models, accelerators, APIs, training and inference tooling, data pipelines, vector databases, integrations, pricing, regional availability, and capacity. An equivalence matrix is therefore useful for finding something similar to a known service, but insufficient for deciding which platform is appropriate for a particular architecture. Learning equivalences reduces the cost of learning another cloud; understanding differences enables meaningful platform selection.

A single-cloud strategy provides operational simplicity by concentrating IAM, networking, observability, billing, security, deployment, troubleshooting, automation, and organizational knowledge on one platform, which can be especially valuable for smaller teams or organizations without a concrete reason to operate multiple clouds. Infrastructure-as-code tools such as Terraform make multi-cloud provisioning easier and can standardize parts of the workflow, but they do not make provider resources behaviorally interchangeable: AWS VPCs, Azure Virtual Networks, and Google Cloud VPCs retain different characteristics, limitations, and integrations. Pursuing portability can also require restricting the architecture to services with relatively close equivalents, potentially sacrificing provider-specific capabilities that could otherwise improve simplicity, performance, or cost. Multi-cloud also does not automatically provide greater resilience because applications can retain shared dependencies such as DNS, CDN, identity, connectivity, observability, or SaaS providers; supplier diversity therefore does not necessarily equal dependency diversity. The example of Cloudflare's June 2022 outage illustrates how a cross-cutting dependency can affect workloads hosted across different compute providers. Resilience must therefore be assessed across the entire dependency chain, not merely by counting clouds, and a single cloud distributed across regions and availability zones may provide sufficient resilience for many workloads. Multi-cloud can nevertheless be justified by commercial negotiations, regulation, data location, specialized services, GPU capacity, or cost rather than availability alone. Single-cloud and multi-cloud are consequently architectural strategies with different costs, benefits, and risks rather than ideological positions.

Hyperscalers provide enormous breadth, but that breadth may be unnecessary for applications requiring only relatively basic infrastructure such as virtual machines, storage, networking, and Kubernetes. Providers such as Hetzner, OVHcloud, DigitalOcean, Scaleway, and Akamai/Linode can therefore be relevant when simplicity, predictable infrastructure, or cost is more important than access to hundreds of managed services. Platforms such as Heroku, Render, and Railway move further up the abstraction stack, allowing developers to focus more directly on applications while hiding infrastructure complexity. The appropriate level of abstraction depends on the workload: additional platform capabilities provide value only when they solve relevant problems rather than simply adding complexity. GPU-intensive workloads provide another case where specialized providers may be more competitive because accelerator type, quantity, availability, location, price, and scaling capacity can matter more than general-purpose service breadth. A smaller or specialized provider also does not have to replace the primary cloud; an organization can retain one main platform while assigning particular workloads to another provider where there is a concrete advantage. This creates an intermediate model in which multi-cloud is selective rather than an attempt to make the entire application portable. A smaller cloud's differentiation can therefore come not from offering more capabilities than a hyperscaler, but from offering fewer capabilities in a simpler, cheaper, or more specialized form that better matches the workload.

Cloud lock-in increases as applications become more dependent on provider-specific services, although technological dependency is only one dimension. Operational lock-in can arise from processes, automation, internal expertise, observability, pipelines, and organizational practices built around a provider, while economic lock-in can emerge from the cost, time, and operational complexity of moving large amounts of data. An architecture using containers, PostgreSQL, and object storage may generally provide more migration options than one deeply dependent on proprietary services, but avoiding proprietary services also has a present-day cost if those services could materially reduce operational effort, improve performance, or lower costs. Complete elimination of lock-in is therefore neither necessarily possible nor necessarily desirable; some dependencies may be strategically valuable and rational to accept. The important distinction is between conscious and accidental dependency. Each significant dependency should be assessed in terms of replacement effort, migration time, cost, and affected architectural components so that the organization understands which dependencies exist, why they were accepted, and what removing them would require. The objective is not zero lock-in at any cost, but deliberate control over the dependencies created by the architecture.

Cloud comparisons often understate the engineering cost required to implement and operate an architecture. Two providers may offer technically comparable resources at similar prices while differing substantially in the integrations, managed services, automation, security, networking, identity, observability, and operational effort required to reach production. Multi-cloud generally increases this complexity because teams must accommodate different platform models and often build additional abstraction and unification layers. Terraform reduces provisioning differences but does not eliminate provider-specific behavior, while centralized observability and security across multiple clouds require additional components that themselves must be operated and maintained. Knowledge requirements also expand because teams must understand multiple platforms and their distinct troubleshooting, incident-response, security, and change-management characteristics. Migration costs must likewise account for data, configurations, identities, networks, pipelines, observability, integrations, and operational processes rather than merely replacing compute resources. Consequently, architecture cost should be evaluated at three points: implementation, ongoing operation, and eventual change. Infrastructure price is only one component, and a cheaper resource can become more expensive overall if it requires substantially more engineering or operational work; conversely, a more expensive managed service can be economical when it eliminates greater internal effort.

Compliance can eliminate a cloud from consideration before price or technical features are compared. Requirements vary according to industry, jurisdiction, data type, security, privacy, residency, auditing, and business continuity, with regulated sectors such as banking, insurance, healthcare, and government often imposing constraints that do not apply to ordinary applications. Major clouds provide extensive certifications, regions, encryption, key management, audit capabilities, identity controls, and regulatory tooling, while smaller providers may satisfy particular requirements without offering the same geographic coverage or certification portfolio. Provider certification does not automatically make an application compliant because responsibility is generally shared, and customers must correctly configure access controls, encryption, logging, retention, and other controls. Geographic availability also requires careful examination because backups, logs, managed services, support, and integrations may have different storage or processing locations from the primary application data. A regional provider can sometimes have an advantage precisely because its infrastructure is concentrated within a relevant jurisdiction. Compliance should therefore act as an early eligibility filter: first determine which providers can satisfy the workload's regulatory requirements, then compare price, performance, simplicity, portability, and other characteristics among the eligible options.

Cloud pricing cannot be evaluated reliably from public list prices alone because actual expenditure depends on usage volume, commitments, discounts, credits, partnerships, and enterprise contracts. Mechanisms such as Reserved Instances, Savings Plans, committed-use discounts, and negotiated contracts can materially alter the effective cost, while startup, research, education, migration, and other programs can reduce initial expenditure. These incentives must nevertheless be distinguished from long-term economics because promotional credits can reduce entry costs without making the platform cheaper once they expire. Existing contracts, expertise, support arrangements, and commercial partnerships can likewise make a provider more economically attractive than a simple infrastructure-price comparison suggests. Free tiers are useful for experimentation and learning but do not necessarily represent production economics because workloads can become substantially more expensive as they scale. Resource-level comparisons can also be misleading: a cheaper GPU, for example, may require more expensive storage, networking, data transfer, load balancing, or observability services. The relevant metric is therefore the total cost of running the workload under its actual commercial and architectural conditions, rather than the isolated price of an instance, GPU, or other resource.

Cloud selection should begin with workload requirements rather than provider familiarity: required services, data location, transfer costs, availability objectives, existing team expertise, acceptable lock-in, compliance requirements, provider-specific capabilities, and the cost of implementing, operating, and eventually migrating the architecture all matter. Infrastructure price is only one component of total cost because a cheaper resource can require additional engineering, integrations, operations, or replacement for managed services that another provider already supplies. Portability has a similar trade-off: designing for migration flexibility can require additional engineering from the beginning, while accepting provider-specific dependencies can reduce current costs but increase future migration costs. Discounts, credits, free tiers, and contracts belong in the calculation but should be considered alongside recurring and operational costs rather than treated as substitutes for them. A single cloud can be appropriate when simplicity and integration dominate; multiple clouds can be appropriate when there is a concrete reason to distribute workloads; and a smaller or specialized provider can be appropriate for a particular workload without replacing the primary platform. Cloud selection therefore consists of deliberate trade-offs among simplicity, cost, portability, lock-in, availability, resilience, compliance, and operational complexity. Knowing alternatives does not require using them; it allows the default to become a conscious decision rather than an automatic one. In the author's case, greater familiarity with alternatives did not eliminate the preference for AWS, but changed it from an automatic answer into a choice made with greater awareness of the available trade-offs.
