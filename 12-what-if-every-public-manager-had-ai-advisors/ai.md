The introduction frames the proposal around the use of AI in political participation, using the United Kingdom’s petition system as a starting point: citizens can use LLMs to research, structure, and formulate demands, potentially increasing their ability to participate effectively. This raises a complementary question: if AI can strengthen citizens’ ability to communicate demands, it might also strengthen public officials’ ability to understand and analyze them. Rather than replacing decision-makers, the proposed approach would create an advisory layer in which AI organizes information, analyzes proposals, identifies risks, and provides alternative perspectives while leaving responsibility for political decisions with humans.

The other side of participation: Public participation generates large quantities of complaints, suggestions, requests, reports, and other information, but raw information does not automatically become useful knowledge for decision-making. Public institutions must identify recurring demands, distinguish isolated cases from broader problems, connect citizen expressions with existing data, and separate descriptions of problems from proposed solutions. AI could support this process by classifying and grouping inputs, identifying patterns, relating demands to available data, and presenting different interpretations without deciding which demands deserve action. Its role would therefore be to transform dispersed societal input into structured analytical material, allowing officials to work from a broader representation of the problems being raised.

A virtual advisor: The proposed AI advisor would divide responsibilities between humans and machines: officials would retain responsibility for context, priorities, competing interests, and decisions, while AI would research legislation and policies, compare experiences, identify potential impacts and risks, organize arguments, relate proposals to public demands, and monitor relevant developments. The value would come not only from speed but from applying this analytical process systematically across much larger volumes of information than human teams could easily process. AI could perform an initial screening of documents, projects, and public expressions, identifying subjects with greater recurrence, complexity, potential impact, or need for investigation, thereby allowing human advisors to concentrate more on interpretation, negotiation, contextual judgment, and decision-making.

Using multiple AIs to simulate different perspectives: Rather than relying on a single model, the proposal suggests using language models developed by different organizations and giving them the same information and analytical criteria so that they independently assess the same issues. Differences among models would be preserved rather than treated automatically as errors, because divergent analyses may expose different risks, affected groups, interpretations, or information gaps. For example, if one model disagrees with several others, the useful question would be why it differs rather than simply counting votes. Over many cases, systematic differences could also reveal recurring characteristics of individual models and areas in which particular systems identify considerations others miss. The purpose of multiple AIs would therefore not be to create an artificial majority that replaces human judgment, but to provide an analytical layer containing independent perspectives and making convergence and disagreement visible.

A second opinion before the decision: The proposed experiment would submit each proposal independently to multiple models using a predefined protocol requiring them to state their assessments, arguments, benefits, risks, potential impacts, uncertainties, and information deficiencies. Their responses would then be compared for convergence and divergence rather than reduced to a single recommendation. Repeated analyses could reveal patterns in how different models approach fiscal, social, implementation, or other issues, while disagreements could identify questions or consequences requiring additional investigation. The usefulness of AI would therefore be measured partly by its ability to expand the set of questions considered before a decision, including consequences, missing information, and questionable assumptions, rather than by its ability to determine whether a policy should be approved or rejected. Human decision-making would remain unchanged in formal responsibility, while the analytical field available beforehand would become broader.

From participation to public policy formulation: The proposed architecture could operate before a legislative proposal exists, processing citizens’ suggestions, complaints, and proposals into structured inputs for policymaking. AI could group similar expressions, identify recurring problems, connect demands with regions or population groups, and provide representatives with an organized picture of public concerns. Multiple models could then research existing legislation, comparable policies in other jurisdictions, possible interventions, and estimated costs, benefits, risks, and indirect effects within the limits of available information. The result would not necessarily be a finished policy but a structured starting point for legislative work, potentially defining a public problem, outlining alternative responses, and identifying unanswered questions. AI could subsequently assist with drafting and checking proposals against existing legislation and possible regulatory conflicts, while human representatives would retain responsibility for judging relevance, feasibility, and appropriateness. This would establish a continuous process connecting citizen participation, AI-supported analysis, and human policymaking without eliminating political representation.

Uncertainty, limitations, and auditing: LLM-generated analysis can be coherent and apparently well supported while still being wrong, because models may misinterpret information, omit relevant variables, rely on inappropriate premises, or present unsupported claims as facts. Public policy also contains irreducible uncertainty, incomplete information, competing impacts across groups, and legitimate conflicts among priorities and values that cannot be transformed into purely technical questions by AI. The proposed system should therefore preserve uncertainty rather than conceal it, recording the information and sources used, assumptions adopted, and limitations identified, and explicitly indicating when available evidence is insufficient. Multiple models do not solve these problems automatically: agreement may result from shared sources or limitations, while disagreement may reflect genuine ambiguity rather than one model simply being wrong. An auditing layer should consequently preserve model versions, inputs, prompts, sources, outputs, caveats, cross-model comparisons, and subsequent human review, creating a reconstructable record of what information and analysis were available when a decision was made. Such records would allow long-term evaluation of model quality, recurring errors, appropriate use cases, and failure conditions. The experiment should explicitly seek evidence of failure rather than merely demonstrate that AI works. Multiple perspectives, visible uncertainty, and traceability are what would turn model outputs into an observable and evaluable decision-support process.

A proposal for an experiment: The proposed experiment would function as an artificial advisory panel in which multiple AI models receive identical information about public demands, legislative proposals, or public problems and independently analyze them according to predefined criteria. The system could monitor legislative agendas, organize citizen input, identify recurring demands, research relevant legislation and comparable experiences, identify potential impacts, and preserve convergence, divergence, sources, assumptions, and uncertainties. Officials would receive this material as a second analytical layer rather than as a single recommendation, seeing different perspectives, consensus points, disagreements, identified risks, and unresolved information gaps while retaining responsibility for the decision. Evaluation would cover the entire process rather than merely model outputs, including analytical consistency, risk detection, relevant-information discovery, disagreement frequency, errors, human validation requirements, time, and cost relative to traditional advisory processes. The central hypothesis would therefore be whether multiple models combined with controlled information, human review, and traceability can provide a useful advisory layer for public officials. The experiment should also identify where the approach fails, including decisions dependent on local knowledge or judgments poorly captured by structured analysis, differences in model strengths, excessive disagreement, or excessive need for human intervention. The conclusion returns to the original observation about AI-assisted citizen participation in the United Kingdom: if AI can strengthen society’s ability to formulate and communicate demands, it may also strengthen the public sector’s ability to understand and analyze them. The proposal is consequently not to substitute AI for political decision-makers but to investigate whether several AI systems can work alongside them, providing additional information, perspectives, and auditable analytical capacity while making clear where the technology is useful, where it fails, and how its conclusions can be challenged.
