# Can AI Make Waterfall Agile?

_If AI makes code cheaper to produce, can it also reduce the cost that has historically separated waterfall from Agile?_

**Summary:** I argue that AI can make more prescriptive, waterfall-like processes compatible with rapid learning cycles because it drastically reduces the time between planning, building, and validating. This does not turn waterfall into Agile, but it weakens the old opposition between the two by allowing detailed planning without necessarily requiring long periods before feedback. In this scenario, agility depends less on how we plan and more on how quickly we discover that a decision was wrong.

---

A few days ago, I watched a video about agile development in the age of AI. The initial provocation was simple, but difficult to ignore: if artificial intelligence is radically changing the way we produce software, does Agile still need to work the same way?

The more I thought about it, the more interesting it became to realize that there are quite different positions in this discussion. On one side, there are those who see AI as a reason to deeply rethink practices and frameworks that have consolidated around Agile. On the other, there are those who see the principles of Agile as even more important in a scenario where our ability to produce software is growing rapidly.

Both positions start from the same observation: AI has changed the economics of software development. But perhaps there is a third question, one that is less frequently discussed: what if AI is not making Agile less necessary, but instead changing some of the conditions that have historically made waterfall less agile?

For a long time, we have treated waterfall and Agile as almost opposing models. On one side, planning, specification, and sequential execution. On the other, short cycles, constant feedback, and adaptation. This opposition makes sense when there is a large gap between deciding, implementing, and validating. If turning a detailed specification into functional software takes months, waiting until an advanced stage of the process to discover that a decision was wrong can be extremely expensive. In this context, reducing cycle size and seeking feedback early stops being merely a methodological preference and becomes an economic necessity.

But what happens when the cost and time required to turn a specification into functional software drop drastically? If something that used to take weeks can be produced in hours, perhaps it becomes possible to specify more, plan more, and still get feedback quickly. Perhaps a more prescriptive approach can coexist with validation cycles short enough to preserve some of the agility that previously required a more incremental process.

This does not mean that waterfall and Agile have become the same thing. Nor does it mean that the traditional problems of waterfall have disappeared. Changing requirements, dependencies, mistaken decisions, and lack of understanding still exist. The point is that AI can change the cost of dealing with some of them. If we can reach a functional version of what we planned more quickly, we can also discover more quickly whether the plan was correct.

The question, then, is no longer simply “Agile or waterfall?” It becomes: how much time is there between a decision and the possibility of discovering that it was wrong? Perhaps this is a more interesting definition of agility. And perhaps this is precisely where AI can change the discussion.

## The Historical Problem with Waterfall

It is common to treat waterfall as if its main flaw were simply planning too much. That interpretation is convenient, but it oversimplifies the issue. Planning, specification, and predictability are not problems in themselves. In many contexts, they remain necessary and even desirable.

The problem appears when there is a long gap between a decision and the possibility of validating that decision. Imagine an organization that spends months specifying a system, then months building it, tests only when a significant portion of the solution is already complete, and finally delivers it. If an important assumption is wrong, the problem may only be discovered after a large amount of work has already been done. The more decisions remain unvalidated, the greater the accumulated risk tends to be, and the more expensive it can be to discover that one of them was wrong.

It was in this context that the principles associated with agile development gained strength. Instead of waiting until the end to discover whether we are building the right thing, we reduce cycle size. We build something, put it in front of the customer, observe the result, and use that learning to decide the next step. The idea is not to eliminate planning, but to reduce the amount of time during which we need to rely on decisions that have not yet been confronted with reality.

Agility, in this sense, is not simply execution speed. It is learning speed. And this distinction will be important for understanding the impact of AI. If the historical problem was not planning itself, but the time and cost required to validate what was planned, then we need to ask what happens when technology drastically reduces that cost.

## What Does It Mean to Be Agile?

Agile is not Scrum, Kanban, or Extreme Programming. Nor is it Planning Poker, story points, sprints, or dailies. These are different ways of operationalizing a broader idea, expressed through the values and principles of the Agile Manifesto. This distinction matters because a practice can stop making sense without the principle that originated it ceasing to make sense. If an AI can estimate a story more accurately than a team playing Planning Poker, that does not mean Agile is dead. It simply means that we have found a different way to perform a particular activity. The same applies to any other practice: its value lies in its ability to produce the outcome that justified its adoption, not in its continued existence by tradition.

Likewise, if an organization uses a long-term roadmap, that does not mean it has stopped being agile. Every relevant project requires planning. Managers need to make decisions about budget, capacity, hiring, dependencies, and communication. What distinguishes an agile approach is not the absence of planning, but the willingness to revise that planning as new information emerges.

The question, therefore, is not whether to plan or not to plan. It is how long it takes us to discover whether what we planned is working. A roadmap can exist for months while its hypotheses are continuously revised. A specification can be detailed without its decisions being considered final. An estimate can guide a decision without being treated as a promise. Planning continues to exist, but it is no longer confused with certainty.

This is precisely where AI makes the discussion interesting. If it reduces the cost of turning decisions into software, it may also change the cost of maintaining rapid feedback cycles. What previously required a necessarily incremental approach may, in certain contexts, be planned further in advance without necessarily losing the ability to adapt. Perhaps the question is no longer whether to plan first or learn later, but how quickly we can do both.

## AI Is Reducing the Cost of Turning Decisions into Software

The transformation brought about by generative AI is not just about writing code faster. It is reducing the cost of turning an intention into a technical artifact. Code, tests, documentation, queries, scripts, configurations, prototypes, and many other tasks can be produced with a much smaller amount of human effort.

This changes the economics of development. For decades, one of the important constraints in software was the human capacity to turn what had been specified into something functional. Now we can begin to imagine a scenario in which this capacity is no longer the primary bottleneck. But there is a fundamental difference between reducing the cost of building something and reducing the cost of discovering whether we should have built it. AI can make implementation much faster. That does not mean that the problem we are trying to solve has become clearer. If we do not know what the user wants, producing code faster simply allows us to reach a wrong answer more quickly.

It is precisely this difference that makes the discussion about waterfall interesting. Imagine a system with relatively stable requirements, strong compliance needs, low volatility, and little need for user experimentation. In this context, there is value in specifying a great deal before implementation. Knowing business rules, integrations, regulatory requirements, and acceptance criteria in advance can reduce risks. Historically, however, the problem was that turning all of this into software took a long time. A detailed specification could result in months of implementation before it was possible to actually observe the system working.

If AI can turn that same specification into a functional implementation quickly, the relationship changes. We can have a more prescriptive process at the specification level and, at the same time, very rapid cycles at the validation level. This opens up a possibility that seemed contradictory until recently: perhaps certain processes can have more up-front planning without necessarily losing agility. Not because waterfall and Agile have become the same thing, but because one of the characteristics that historically separated them may be becoming cheaper. Planning extensively may remain possible without necessarily meaning that we have to wait a long time to discover whether the plan was wrong.

## Agile Is Not an Absence of Planning

There is a caricature of Agile according to which agile teams simply start developing and figure out what to do along the way. That is not it. Agile has never meant the absence of planning, processes, or methods. Scrum, XP, and Kanban are different approaches, with their own practices and mechanisms for organizing work, dealing with uncertainty, obtaining feedback, and adapting development. Some of these practices may become less relevant when new tools emerge, but that does not mean the principles they operationalize cease to be necessary.

A roadmap is still necessary. Backlog, priorities, capacity, dependencies, and forecasts continue to have value. An organization needs to know where it intends to go, even while recognizing that the path to get there may change. A bank needs to plan budget and capacity, a company needs to coordinate dependencies, stakeholders need to understand priorities, and projects subject to regulatory requirements need to operate within certain constraints. What Agile questions is not the need to plan, but the idea that an initial plan represents sufficient knowledge about the future to be followed rigidly until the end.

This distinction also helps us understand the culture of estimates. In many contexts, we turn uncertainty into apparently precise numbers and then use those numbers to produce increasingly long-range forecasts. We estimate stories, group those estimates into sprints, extrapolate observed capacity, and try to answer when the entire backlog will be completed. The problem is not estimating, but forgetting that an estimate is a tool for dealing with uncertainty, not a way to eliminate it. When an estimate starts being treated as a commitment, we begin using a planning tool to produce a false sense of certainty.

This is where AI can change some practices without changing Agile. Instead of gathering the team to manually decide whether a particular story is worth 3, 5, or 8 points, we can imagine an AI analyzing the team’s history, similar work, dependencies, affected components, observed complexity, and actual duration of previous implementations to produce a forecast. The team could then discuss the assumptions behind the estimate, identify information the model does not know, and adjust the forecast when necessary. The machine would produce an analysis based on the available data; the team would remain responsible for the judgment. Planning Poker may cease to be useful in a particular context without the need to plan, discuss capacity, or recognize uncertainty disappearing.

The same reasoning applies to Scrum, XP, and Kanban. In Scrum, part of the preparation and estimation work can be automated. In XP, AI can produce automated tests, assist with refactoring, and accelerate implementation cycles. In Kanban, models can analyze historical flow, identify bottlenecks, suggest WIP limits, or detect patterns of waiting and rework. Practices can change because tools change, but the problems they seek to solve continue to exist. The question becomes whether we still need the same practice to solve them.

This also changes the role of ceremonies and interactions themselves. A planning meeting does not need to disappear because AI can prepare a capacity forecast. It can become more interesting precisely because the time previously spent producing information can be used to discuss decisions, risks, and exceptions. A Review can also become more important when the cost of putting functional software in front of users falls. The cheaper it becomes to develop, the greater the risk of developing things that should not exist, and the more important it becomes to confront what we produce with the real behavior of those who use the system.

The consequence is that some Agile practices may become obsolete without Agile becoming obsolete. If AI can produce better estimates, we do not need to preserve Planning Poker out of tradition. If it can generate reliable regression tests, we do not need to preserve manual work simply because it was part of the process. If it can prepare information for a meeting in seconds, we do not need to spend the meeting producing that information. What we need to preserve is the ability to make good decisions, obtain feedback, and adapt the system when reality contradicts our expectations.

This is where the central hypothesis becomes more interesting. If Agile never simply meant planning less, but rather learning and responding more quickly, then AI does not necessarily push development away from Agile. In certain conditions, it may allow more planned and prescriptive processes to also achieve feedback cycles that previously required an incremental approach. If a relatively detailed specification can be turned into functional software in hours, tested, put in front of users, and reviewed quickly, perhaps the iteration cycle of a process traditionally associated with waterfall can become as short as a sprint. In this scenario, the difference between the two models is no longer necessarily the size of the planning, but the speed at which we can turn a decision into learning.

## AIs Did Not Come to Replace Testers, but to Unlock Their True Potential

If AI reduces the cost of developing software, there is an obvious risk: we may increase our ability to produce code much faster than our ability to verify whether that code works. In this scenario, quality can become the new bottleneck. The answer, however, should not simply be to increase the amount of manual testing. If we can automate a significant portion of verification, we should use that capability to change what we expect from a tester’s work.

A machine is particularly good at verifying what we know needs to be verified. Regression testing is a clear example. After a feature is changed, we can automatically run hundreds or thousands of scenarios to verify that existing behaviors continue to work. AI can also help generate test cases, identify relevant combinations, repeat executions, and compare expected results with observed behavior. These are activities in which scale, repeatability, and consistency matter more than intuition.

The problem is that software rarely fails only in scenarios that someone anticipated. Many of the most interesting failures appear precisely at the intersection between features, in unlikely combinations, in sequences that nobody specified, or in behaviors that seem absurd until someone tries them. This is where a tester’s experience becomes valuable. Instead of spending hours filling out spreadsheets, executing trivial cases, or manually repeating what a machine can verify more efficiently, the professional can devote more time to exploration.

What happens if I do this before that? What happens if I interrupt this flow halfway through? What happens when two apparently independent behaviors are combined? What happens when the user interprets a screen in a way the team never imagined? These questions depend less on knowing the specification and more on being able to see possibilities that were left out of it. This is precisely where experience and intuition stop being difficult-to-measure characteristics and take on a concrete role in software quality.

This does not mean that AI replaces the tester. It means that it can allow the tester to spend less time verifying the predictable and more time looking for the unpredictable. The machine expands coverage over what we can formalize; the human concentrates attention on what we still cannot fully formalize. The faster software production becomes, the more important this ability to find problems that were not in the script becomes.

There is also an important change in the nature of testing itself. In an environment where software can be produced continuously, quality should not be a stage that happens only after implementation, but an activity distributed throughout the development cycle. Automation can continuously verify what we already know how to test, while the tester explores system behavior, formulates hypotheses, and looks for situations that have not yet been anticipated. This brings testing closer to the discovery process itself: we are not merely verifying whether the implementation matches the specification, but trying to discover whether the specification covered what actually needed to be considered.

At the limit, the division of labor becomes quite interesting: AI tirelessly tests what we already know how to test; the tester tries to discover what we do not yet know we should test. The first activity scales with computing. The second depends on experience, curiosity, and the ability to formulate unexpected hypotheses.

## The Backlog and Roadmap Become Organized Around ROI

For a long time, an important part of software planning was constrained by the team’s delivery capacity. We have so many people, we can complete approximately this many stories per sprint, therefore we need to decide which backlog items fit within that capacity. Planning ends up starting with an operational question: how much can we deliver? From there, we try to decide what to put within that limit.

If AI significantly increases production capacity, this logic begins to lose strength. If we can implement ten times as much, it does not mean we need to implement ten times as much. The problem stops being only how much the team can deliver and becomes which problems should actually receive our delivery capacity. The constraint stops being exclusively in execution and begins to shift toward choice.

This changes how we should look at the backlog and roadmap. Instead of organizing them predominantly around the team’s estimated capacity, we can start organizing them around the expected return of initiatives. Which problem affects the most users? Which change reduces the most cost? Which feature can increase revenue? Which risk needs to be eliminated first? Which hypothesis needs to be validated before we commit greater resources? Capacity remains a constraint, but it ceases to be the starting point for prioritization. First we decide where there is greater value or risk; then we evaluate how much it costs to pursue that opportunity.

This does not mean that everything can be reduced to a precise ROI calculation. In software, there are investments whose return is indirect or difficult to quantify, such as reducing technical debt, security, observability, reliability, or preparation for future changes. Even so, there is a relevant economic question: what problem are we solving, what risk are we reducing, and why should this be prioritized over the available alternatives? Even when we cannot calculate the return precisely, we need to be able to explain why that initiative deserves investment.

AI makes this discussion even more important because it reduces the cost of saying “yes” to new ideas. When implementing a feature required weeks of work, the opportunity cost naturally limited the number of things we could try. If a team can produce a prototype in hours, that constraint decreases. The risk becomes excessive production: a backlog full of things that are easy to build but difficult to justify. We may reach a paradoxical situation in which the organization produces software more quickly and, at the same time, generates less value because its execution capacity has come to exceed its prioritization capacity.

This is where the notion of productivity needs to change. Producing more software does not necessarily mean producing more value. If AI increases our execution capacity but we continue prioritizing work based on how much we can fit into a sprint, we may simply accelerate the production of waste. The advantage will not necessarily be in building faster, but in deciding better what deserves to be built.

The same reasoning applies to automation. If automation has become cheaper, that does not mean every automatable task should be automated. An activity that consumes two minutes per month probably does not deserve the same investment as another that consumes four hours every day. The implementation cost may have fallen, but maintenance, infrastructure, observability, and evolution costs still exist. The ease of building does not eliminate the need to justify the investment. On the contrary: the easier it becomes to build, the more discipline we need to decide what not to build.

Perhaps the deepest change is this: when the ability to produce software is no longer as scarce, the bottleneck becomes the ability to choose relevant problems. The backlog stops being primarily a queue of what the team can do and becomes a portfolio of opportunities, risks, and hypotheses competing for the organization’s investment. And the roadmap stops answering only “what will we be able to deliver?” and starts answering a more important question: “which problems are worth solving first?”

## Code Review Is Not Just About Fixing Code

The reduction in implementation cost also changes the role of code review. When writing code was a relatively expensive activity, it made sense to focus much of the review on bugs, performance problems, violations of standards, and other implementation defects. If we can now produce code in a fraction of the time, the risk begins to move elsewhere. We can reach a situation in which the code is technically correct, well tested, and well documented, and yet still represents a poor engineering decision.

An AI can turn a poor decision into a technically sophisticated implementation with impressive efficiency. We can ask for a robust architecture for a problem that could be solved with a simple function, add an abstraction layer that nobody actually needs, introduce a new dependency to avoid a few lines of code, or build infrastructure disproportionate to the size of the system. The result can pass linters, automated tests, and static analysis tools without presenting any obvious problem. The problem lies elsewhere: should we have built all of this?

This may be one of the most dangerous forms of overengineering in the age of AI. When producing complexity becomes cheap, there is a natural tendency to accept it more easily. The cost of creating an abstraction seems small because AI can implement it in minutes. The cost of living with that abstraction, however, still exists. Someone will need to understand it, debug it, document it, modify it, and explain why it exists two years from now. The cost of construction has fallen; the cost of complexity in production has not necessarily fallen in the same proportion.

For this reason, perhaps the most important question in a code review is no longer just “is this code correct?” but “is this a good decision?” The reviewer needs to question architecture, dependencies, abstractions, scope, and complexity, even when everything appears technically flawless. The goal is no longer just to find defects in the implementation, but also to preserve the quality of the decisions that gave rise to it.

This also changes the meaning of seniority. Knowing many technologies will remain useful, but the relative value of simply knowing how to use them decreases when an AI can explain a library, generate an example, or produce a reasonable implementation in a few minutes. Technical knowledge remains necessary, especially for understanding the consequences of choices, but experience increasingly appears in the ability to recognize when a technology, architecture, or abstraction should not be used. A senior professional is not only someone who knows how to build a sophisticated solution. It is someone who can recognize when a sophisticated solution is unnecessary.

For the same reason, code review should not rigidly follow hierarchy. Seniors also need to have their code reviewed, including by more junior professionals. Not because a junior necessarily has more technical knowledge, but because a seemingly simple question can reveal a decision that was never properly justified. “Why do we need this layer?” or “why can’t we do this directly?” are questions that can be asked regardless of the seniority of the person asking them. In a mature engineering culture, code review is not a mechanism for the more experienced to police the less experienced. It is a collective mechanism for making decisions explicit, sharing knowledge, and creating opportunities for anyone to question what appears unnecessarily complex.

Perhaps this is one of the most interesting effects of AI on engineering: the easier it becomes to produce code, the less value there is in simply producing correct code and the more value there is in preventing poor decisions from turning into complex systems. The machine can write the solution. Engineering remains responsible for deciding whether that solution deserves to exist.

## Go-Live Is Also Part of Development

There is an important difference in how Agile and approaches closer to waterfall deal with discovery. Agile seeks to reduce the size of the cycle between building and discovering by frequently putting working software in front of the customer to learn from the result. In more prescriptive processes, there is a greater expectation that requirements, decisions, and acceptance criteria will be sufficiently defined and validated before go-live. Production entry therefore tends to be treated as the completion of an important validation stage, rather than the beginning of a new stage of discovery.

But perhaps go-live does not need to be a finish line. It can be the beginning of assisted production. The idea is simple: instead of treating production entry as the moment when the software is definitively ready, we can treat it as the moment when it begins to be observed under real conditions. A new feature can initially be released to a limited group of users, closely monitored, and adjusted as the first signals appear. It is a dynamic quite similar to a playtest or a beta phase of a game: the version is already functional enough to reach the public, but its real-world behavior is still part of the discovery process.

This is particularly relevant because not everything we discover after go-live is a bug. We may discover that users interpret a feature differently than expected, that a particular flow is more complex than it seemed, or that a product hypothesis simply does not hold. The software may be working exactly as specified and, nevertheless, the specification may be wrong. Production turns hypotheses into evidence that could not be fully obtained in a controlled environment.

AI can reduce the cost of this cycle. If a relatively detailed specification can be quickly turned into a functional implementation, we can reach go-live sooner, observe real behavior, and make adjustments before expanding the solution’s reach. This brings a practice that can exist in a more prescriptive process closer to a central characteristic of Agile: learning from working software before committing too many resources to a decision.

In this scenario, go-live stops being the opposite of iteration. It becomes part of it. We can plan in detail, build prescriptively, and still maintain a discovery cycle after production entry. The difference is that the first go-live does not need to represent the completion of the solution; it can represent the beginning of an assisted production phase in which the system, its users, and the team begin producing evidence to decide what should happen next.

This creates an interesting possibility: perhaps AI does not need to eliminate up-front planning to make a process more agile. It can simply reduce the time required to reach the first go-live and make it cheap enough to learn from it.

## When Execution Stops Being the Main Bottleneck

AI can estimate, analyze history, prepare plans, generate tests, automate tasks, produce documentation, analyze metrics, and identify patterns. It can take on an increasing share of the mechanical work we use to turn decisions into software. What it does not eliminate is the need to decide what deserves to be developed, what problem we are trying to solve, what risk we are willing to take, what complexity is justifiable, and what to do when reality contradicts our expectations. In fact, the cheaper execution becomes, the more important these decisions become.

This helps put Agile itself into perspective. If developing code becomes cheap, we need to get better at deciding what to develop. If testing becomes cheap, we need to get better at deciding what deserves to be explored. If estimating becomes cheap, we need to get better at identifying the uncertainties an estimate does not capture. If putting software into production becomes cheap, we need to get better at interpreting user behavior. If building sophisticated architectures becomes cheap, we need to get better at recognizing when simplicity is sufficient. Reducing the cost of execution does not eliminate engineering; it shifts part of engineering’s value toward judgment.

This is also why it may be a mistake to associate Agile with the tools and rituals that became popular for operationalizing it. The Agile Manifesto does not require sprints, story points, Planning Poker, or Scrum. These are practices developed in particular contexts to deal with concrete software development problems. If new technologies make it possible to solve these problems in better ways, there is no reason to preserve a practice simply because it has become traditional. What remains relevant are the principles that guide these practices: individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration, and responding to change. And these principles seem, at the very least, as relevant when our ability to produce software increases dramatically.

This is where the initial question gets a less obvious answer. AI can indeed make certain more prescriptive processes compatible with feedback cycles that previously required more incremental approaches. An organization can work with a detailed specification, maintain a long-term roadmap, and still turn decisions into functional software quickly enough to obtain feedback before the cost of change becomes prohibitive. Go-live itself can stop being treated as the finish line and become the beginning of assisted production, in which real users help reveal problems, validate hypotheses, and guide the next adjustments. In this scenario, perhaps a cycle traditionally associated with waterfall could last days or weeks rather than months, approaching the duration of a sprint.

This does not turn waterfall into Agile, but it makes the boundary we used to draw between the two less clear. Perhaps AI is expanding the space of processes capable of operating with rapid learning cycles. Planning can remain detailed, as long as it is not confused with certainty. The roadmap can continue to exist, as long as it is not treated as an immutable commitment. The specification can remain extensive, as long as we can quickly validate its consequences. Go-live can continue to be a major delivery, as long as it is not confused with the end of learning. We can plan a lot and wait little.

In the end, perhaps the most important question about agility remains quite simple: how long does it take us to discover that we were wrong? If AI can reduce that time, it does not make Agile obsolete. Nor does it necessarily turn waterfall into Agile. It changes the cost of the alternatives and, with that, allows us to reconsider which processes are capable of producing rapid learning.

Perhaps the most important change lies precisely in the relationship between planning and agility. For a long time, working in an agile way meant reducing cycle size because waiting months between planning and feedback made any mistake extremely expensive. If AI can reduce that interval from months to days or weeks, perhaps it is no longer necessary to choose between planning in detail and learning quickly. A process can remain quite prescriptive in its initial definition and still be agile insofar as it can quickly discover when its decisions were wrong and correct them.

The technology has changed, the cost of developing software has changed, and the tools have changed. But the fundamental problem remains the same: discovering, as early as possible, whether we are developing what is actually worth developing.
