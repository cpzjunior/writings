The article examines social capital as an often-invisible asset that precedes a company and influences the opportunities available to its founders. Studying successful entrepreneurs raises the question of how much of their later trajectory was already shaped by their preexisting environments, relationships, reputations, and access. Beyond talent, work, capital, strategy, and opportunity, knowing people who trust the entrepreneur or can provide information, introductions, customers, partners, or credibility can materially reduce the friction of reaching opportunities. Social capital is therefore distinct from financial capital and from networking itself: it is the value that can be mobilized through relationships carrying trust, reputation, information, reciprocity, or access. It exists at every scale, from elite entrepreneurial networks to ordinary local communities, and means that companies with similar resources and capabilities may nevertheless face very different access costs because their founders begin from different relational positions.

When a company is founded, it does not actually start from zero because its founder brings a prior professional trajectory, reputation, market familiarity, and relationships with potential customers, suppliers, colleagues, and partners. Social capital becomes practically relevant when these relationships reduce the effort required to obtain meetings, information, validation, partnerships, referrals, or initial trust. It differs from networking because networking describes the activity of creating relationships, whereas social capital describes the value that can subsequently be mobilized through them; a small number of strong, relevant relationships may therefore matter more than a large contact list. Relationships also commonly emerge without an economic purpose through family, neighborhoods, schools, sports, cultural organizations, and other communities, later becoming sources of knowledge or introductions. Consequently, two similarly competent companies with comparable products and funding can move at different speeds because one already possesses access paths that the other must discover and build. Social capital does not guarantee success or substitute for competence, but it changes the cost, time, and friction required to convert resources into opportunities.

Social capital can also span generations because families transmit not only financial resources but knowledge, references, familiarity with institutions, and relationships. This mechanism exists across socioeconomic levels: a family may have long-standing relationships with professionals, suppliers, community members, or organizations that make particular opportunities easier to reach. Someone growing up within such an environment may know whom to approach, how to approach them, and how a particular environment operates long before another person has had the opportunity to develop that familiarity. Financial wealth can be transferred directly, whereas trust cannot simply be inherited; introductions may create access, but the individual still has to cultivate relationships and establish their own reputation. Thus, social capital can be transmitted without being literally transferred, combining inherited relational advantages with relationships built personally over time. The resulting difference between trajectories is partly the distance each person must travel to reach relevant people, information, and opportunities.

Social capital can expand after a company is founded because the company itself creates new relationships with customers, suppliers, employees, partners, investors, and other actors. These relationships can produce a cumulative cycle in which existing access generates opportunities, successful outcomes strengthen reputation, stronger reputation facilitates further introductions and relationships, and those relationships create additional access. The PayPal network illustrates this mechanism: people who worked closely together developed relationships that persisted after leaving the company, contributing to later ventures, investments, and collaborations among members associated with the “PayPal Mafia.” The article does not claim that these relationships alone explain their later success, but argues that the network and the quality of its members likely reinforced one another. Similar cumulative effects can occur when referrals bring early customers, good results strengthen reputation, and reputation makes subsequent customers, partnerships, and hiring easier. Social capital therefore can begin as a founder’s personal asset but become an organizational and relational asset that continues generating opportunities over time.

The same network effects that make social capital valuable can also amplify mistakes and create exclusion. Relationships may open doors, but they do not guarantee that the person entering can perform effectively; if competence is replaced by personal connection, referrals or introductions can become mechanisms for favoritism, nepotism, and poor selection. Reputation can similarly amplify both positive and negative experiences across a connected network. Closed networks create additional risks when opportunities repeatedly circulate among insiders, potentially allowing unsuitable people to remain protected from rigorous evaluation while excluding capable outsiders. Homogeneous networks can also mistake familiarity for quality and consensus for correctness, reducing exposure to different perspectives and experiences. Thus, social capital can reduce friction and connect talent, but it can also concentrate power and create barriers to entry. Relationships can legitimately facilitate access, but should not be treated as substitutes for evaluating competence; the value and consequences of a network depend on the quality of its relationships, how opportunities are allocated, and whether members remain willing to question their own decisions.

Building social capital takes time, which means people who inherit established networks can begin substantially closer to certain opportunities than people who must construct comparable relationships themselves. This does not make family transmission inherently illegitimate: families naturally pass on education, knowledge, experience, stability, values, and relationships. The important point is that these differences constitute part of the starting conditions of individual trajectories. If social capital affects access to opportunities, then programs intended to expand entrepreneurship among people with less access cannot rely solely on money or technical knowledge; they also need to create environments where relationships can develop. Mentoring, incubators, professional associations, communities, and programs connecting entrepreneurs with customers, suppliers, investors, and other business owners can provide value precisely by creating these relational opportunities. The reference to Ayrton Senna’s observation about opportunity coming when someone opens a door illustrates this mechanism, while Silvio Santos’s trajectory demonstrates how recognition, reputation, communication ability, and accumulated relationships can themselves become economic assets over time. This applies at every scale, from major business networks to local merchants and professionals whose reputations and long-term relationships facilitate cooperation. Reducing disparities in access therefore requires creating conditions in which people can build trust, participate in relevant environments, and establish relationships rather than merely providing capital or knowledge.

Social capital exists at every scale and develops through the interactions, professional relationships, communities, and environments people experience throughout their lives. It is broader than networking because its value depends on the quality, duration, and context of relationships rather than simply the number of contacts. It can be inherited through family environments, developed professionally, incorporated into companies, expanded through business activity, and carried into subsequent ventures. Although introductions can provide access, they do not automatically create trust or reputation, which must still be developed by the individual. One of its most important effects is therefore on speed: people with relevant relationships may not be more capable, but they can spend less time locating information, validating ideas, reaching decision-makers, or obtaining initial market access. Understanding how businesses are built consequently requires looking beyond balance sheets, products, and pitches toward the trajectory and network that existed beforehand. A company may be new, but the relationships and reputation that shape its initial path usually are not.
