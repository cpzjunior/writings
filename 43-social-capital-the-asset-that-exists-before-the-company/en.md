# Social Capital: The Asset That Exists Before the Company

_How relationships, reputation, and access to social circles influence the construction and growth of a business._

**Summary:** I argue that every company is born with an asset that does not appear on the balance sheet: the value of the relationships its founders have already built, which I call social capital. It encompasses trust, reputation, information, and access, and can significantly reduce the distance to people, customers, and opportunities. Because these relationships can be accumulated before and during an entrepreneurial journey, two equally competent entrepreneurs can start with very different access costs and follow very different paths. This is why, in social entrepreneurship, it is not enough to provide financial resources or knowledge: we also need to create conditions for people with less access to build connections capable of bringing them closer to new opportunities. After all, before a company exists, there is already a trajectory and a network that can influence its path.

---

I like studying the stories of great entrepreneurs. Not only to understand the companies they built, but to try to perceive what existed before them. Elon Musk, Steve Jobs, Jeff Bezos, Bill Gates, Warren Buffett, Richard Branson, among so many others. Each built their trajectory under different circumstances, with their own skills, markets, relationships, and choices. Even so, when I read these stories, one question frequently arises: how much of what happened afterward was already, in some way, present before the company existed?

It is easy to look at a successful trajectory and organize the explanation around talent, work, capital, strategy, and opportunity. These are fundamental elements. But there is another, less visible component that repeatedly appears in these stories: who that entrepreneur knew, which environments they moved through, what reputation they had already built, who trusted them, and who was willing to open a door. These relationships can provide access to information, people, customers, partners, and opportunities that, for someone outside that environment, would be much more difficult to reach.

That is what we call social capital in this article. We are not talking about the share capital of a business entity, the capital formed by the resources contributed by the partners to establish the company. Here, the concept is different: it refers to the value that can exist in relationships when they carry trust, reputation, information, reciprocity, or access. It is not simply about knowing many people or having a full contact list. In practice, this asset can reduce the friction involved in doing things that are essential to any business: finding someone, getting a meeting, obtaining information, validating an idea, closing a partnership, or earning the trust of a first customer.

I began to think of this as a kind of invisible infrastructure. A company may be born with money, an idea, and a team, but it rarely starts with only that. The founder also arrives with a history, a reputation, and a network that were built before the company.

The most extraordinary examples make this phenomenon easier to see because they work almost like a magnifying glass. When we observe great entrepreneurs, business families, or networks of technology company founders, the effect of these relationships appears on a scale that is difficult to ignore. But social capital is not restricted to these environments. It exists at every scale. The restaurant on the corner also has social capital.

And that is precisely where the concept becomes interesting.

## The Company Does Not Start From Zero

When a company is born, we normally think about what it brings to the market: money, product, technology, team, intellectual property. Depending on the type of business, we also consider the founder, their ability to execute, their market knowledge, and the strategy they intend to put into practice.

But the founder arrives carrying something that does not appear on the organizational chart: their professional trajectory, the relationships they have accumulated, the reputation they have built, and their familiarity with the market in which they intend to operate. They may bring former colleagues, potential customers, suppliers, and partners who already know them, as well as knowing whom to turn to when a problem arises. In some cases, this history is enough to secure a meeting that, for another entrepreneur, would require weeks of prospecting.

This is where social capital stops being an abstract idea and takes on a practical consequence. In this article, social capital is the value that a person or organization can mobilize through its relationships. This value exists when a relationship carries trust, reputation, information, knowledge, or access and, therefore, can make it easier to obtain something that would be harder to reach without it.

An introduction can replace weeks of prospecting. A referral can bring a conversation forward. A reputation built previously can make someone willing to hear a proposal before even knowing the product. Social capital, in this sense, functions as access infrastructure: it does not deliver the outcome, but it can significantly reduce the friction involved in reaching it.

Social capital is also not synonymous with networking. Networking is the deliberate effort to create and maintain relationships. Social capital is the value that can be mobilized from those relationships. Therefore, an extensive network of contacts can be worth less than a few solid relationships that are relevant to the market in which one intends to operate.

And not every relationship needs to have begun with an economic purpose. A group practices a sport for years. Neighbors live alongside each other for decades. Parents meet because their children attend the same school. People frequent the same cultural spaces, associations, or clubs. Some relationships remain superficial. Others become friendships, trust, and familiarity. Over time, someone comes to know who can help in a given situation, who knows a particular field, or who can make an introduction. The relationship came before the opportunity.

This helps explain why two apparently similar companies can follow very different trajectories. Two companies can have comparable products, competent teams, and similar amounts of capital. Even so, one may reach its first customers more quickly, find partners with less effort, or obtain relevant information through relationships that already existed. The other has to discover on its own whom to approach, how to reach those people, and how to build trust. The difference, in this case, is not necessarily what each company possesses, but the friction required to turn those resources into access and opportunity.

It is tempting to attribute this difference solely to execution. Sometimes, it explains it. But equally competent companies can operate at different speeds when one of them already possesses access paths that the other still needs to build. For someone completely outside a market, every door has to be discovered. For someone who already moves within that environment, some doors simply exist.

This does not eliminate the need for competence, nor does it guarantee that a company will succeed. It simply means that two companies can begin the same journey with different access costs. In a business, time and access are also resources. When a company needs to spend less time and energy reaching the people, information, and opportunities it needs, part of that saving can become a competitive advantage.

The company may be new. But the founder rarely arrives alone.

## Family Social Capital

The same logic can span generations. Families transmit much more than financial wealth. They transmit knowledge, experience, references, familiarity with certain institutions, and, above all, relationships built over time. Someone who grows up in a particular environment may know from an early age people and organizations they would only encounter many years later if they had to reach them on their own.

This becomes more evident in very wealthy families, whose relationships can extend to entrepreneurs, investors, executives, professionals, and different institutions. But it would be a mistake to imagine that social capital begins at this level. It exists in virtually every environment. A family may have had the same doctor for decades, a trusted lawyer, a familiar accountant, a long-standing supplier, or a merchant with whom it maintains a good relationship. It may know important people in a profession, neighborhood, school, association, sports club, or religious community. Over time, these relationships create familiarity, trust, and knowledge about whom to approach when a need arises.

Think of someone who decides to open a small restaurant. They may not know investors or major businesspeople, but they may have relatives who work in commerce, a friend who understands accounting, a supplier they have known for years, and customers who come through referrals. None of these relationships is extraordinary on its own. But together, they can reduce the friction involved in getting started because they shorten paths that another entrepreneur would have to discover on their own.

At larger scales, the mechanism is the same. A family that has operated in a particular sector for decades can transmit to a child not only financial resources, but also familiarity with that market, knowledge of its codes, and access to people who already recognize them. The advantage does not necessarily lie in someone doing a favor. Often, it lies simply in knowing whom to approach, how to approach a particular person, and how that environment works.

A person can inherit money. They can also grow up surrounded by relationships that facilitate access to opportunities. But the two things are not equivalent. Money can be transferred directly. A family can introduce someone, open a door, or place them in a particular environment, but it cannot transfer ready-made trust. The relationship will still need to be cultivated, and the reputation confirmed by the person themselves.

This helps explain why starting points can differ even when two people have similar capabilities. One starts closer to the people, information, and opportunities they need. The other first has to discover where they are, find a way to reach them, and build trust. The difference may seem small in an isolated situation, but it accumulates over the course of a trajectory.

Social capital, therefore, can be transmitted without simply being transferred. Part of it lies in the relationships a person inherits through their family; another part will be built by the person themselves. What changes between trajectories is not only the size of the network, but the distance each person has to travel to reach the opportunities they seek.

## The Formation of a Social Capital Cycle

If social capital can exist before the company, it can also grow after the company is born. The founder enters the business carrying a network built throughout their life, but the company begins to create new connections of its own. Customers, suppliers, employees, partners, and investors enter this network, and each new relationship can generate further opportunities.

This is how social capital can become cumulative. A relationship facilitates an initial access. That access can generate an opportunity. The opportunity can produce an outcome. A good outcome strengthens reputation and makes new relationships easier to build. These new relationships, in turn, expand access to other people, information, and markets. What began as a personal network can become a relational asset of the company itself.

In practice, this appears when a company acquires its first customers through referrals, is introduced to new partners, attracts qualified professionals because of the reputation it has built, and begins receiving opportunities that it previously would have had to actively pursue. The result is not merely growth. It is a progressive reduction in the friction involved in accessing relevant resources and people.

The case of PayPal illustrates this movement on a particularly visible scale. The company brought together people who worked intensively with one another and, in the process, formed a network of trust that continued to exist after they left the organization. Former colleagues founded, invested in, and worked together on new companies, giving rise to the group that became known as the PayPal Mafia.

Peter Thiel, one of PayPal's co-founders, later co-founded Palantir and went on to invest in several companies. Other members followed similar paths. It is difficult to separate how much of this outcome came from the relationships built at PayPal and how much came from the extraordinary quality of the people who made up that network. Probably, the two reinforced each other.

The point is not that working at PayPal automatically produces successful future entrepreneurs. It is to recognize that a company can be an environment for the concentration and creation of social capital. People enter bringing their own networks, work together, build trust, and expand their circles. When they leave, they take part of that asset with them, which can be mobilized in new contexts.

This mechanism also helps explain why reputation and distribution can become cumulative advantages. A company that can reach its first customers more easily tends to accumulate more information about the market. If it delivers good results, it strengthens its reputation. A stronger reputation facilitates new introductions, partnerships, and hires. These new forms of access again increase its ability to reach the market.

The cycle, therefore, does not end with the first door that opens. It can repeat throughout the trajectory: network, access, opportunity, outcome, reputation, and an even larger network. This is why social capital can be an entry advantage, but also an advantage that expands over time.

In this process, the company ceases to be merely a user of its founders' social capital. It begins to participate in its formation and can return this asset to the market in the form of new relationships, new businesses, and new opportunities.

## When the Network Amplifies the Error

There is, however, a less comfortable side to this story. The same social capital that reduces friction can also amplify consequences. Relationships do not replace competence. When a personal connection begins to matter more than ability, experience, or results, what appeared to be an advantage can become a problem.

A person may reach a relevant position because someone opened a door, made an introduction, or put their own reputation on the line. This reduces the friction of entry, but does not eliminate the need to deliver. If the person fails to meet the expectations created, the same network that accelerated their entry may begin to collapse.

Reputation works as an amplifier. A good recommendation can open a door. A bad experience can cause the news to reach several others before the person has time to knock on them. The larger and more connected the network, the greater the reach of a positive or negative experience can be.

The problem becomes more serious when the network begins distributing opportunities based on belonging rather than competence. Nepotism, personal favoritism, and recurring hiring among friends and acquaintances are clear examples. In these cases, the relationship ceases to be merely a signal of trust and becomes a selection criterion. Being “trusted” can become more important than being capable of performing the role well. When this happens, the personal relationship begins to replace a legitimate criterion of competence.

From that point on, the problem is no longer merely a poor management decision. An ethical dimension comes into play and, depending on the context, there may also be legal implications. When opportunities are distributed based on personal relationships, favoritism, or belonging, rather than legitimate criteria for that decision, the organization can unfairly exclude people and create legal and governance risks. In addition, repeatedly choosing people based on their relationship with the decision-maker can cause the organization to overlook better alternatives. If the network protects its members from rigorous evaluation, mistakes can be tolerated for longer, important positions can be occupied by unsuitable people, and poor decisions can gain an additional layer of protection.

Very closed networks create another risk: homogeneity. The same people recommend one another, the same environments produce the candidates, and the same references guide decisions. Trust increases, but the organization loses contact with perspectives, skills, and experiences that exist outside that circle. Over time, familiarity can be mistaken for quality and consensus for correctness.

This is how a relational advantage can become a barrier. Those who belong to the network receive information earlier, are remembered for opportunities, get introductions, and have more chances to be heard. Those outside it have to prove more, wait longer, and discover on their own paths that are already available to network members. When this mechanism repeats, opportunities begin to depend not only on the ability to create value, but also on the position someone occupies within the network.

Therefore, recognizing the value of social capital does not mean defending the use of personal relationships as a substitute for objective criteria. Relationships of trust can facilitate access, but they cannot replace evaluation. Referrals can reduce the cost of finding people, but they cannot eliminate the need to verify competence. Families, friends, and professional circles can open doors, but an open door cannot be confused with a guarantee of remaining inside.

Social capital can reduce friction, but it can also concentrate power. It can accelerate a good decision, but also a bad one. It can connect talent, but also prevent new talent from being seen.

A strong network, therefore, is not necessarily a healthy network. Its value depends on the quality of the relationships that compose it, the criteria used to distribute opportunities, and the ability to question one's own choices. When belonging becomes more valuable than competence, social capital ceases to function as access infrastructure and begins to function as a mechanism of closure.

Social capital is an amplifier. What it amplifies depends on the quality of the network.

## Building Takes Time

There is an important difference between receiving social capital and building it. Someone who begins life within an established network can have an advantage that would take years to build for someone starting from outside. Relationships take time. Trust is born from interaction, reputation accumulates through consistency, and familiarity with particular environments depends, to a large extent, on having the opportunity to participate in them.

This does not mean there is anything wrong with being born or raised in a more structured family, with broader relationships and a greater ability to guide its children. Families build their histories, communities build their networks, and people accumulate relationships throughout their lives. It is natural for parents to seek to transmit to their children what they have built: education, knowledge, values, experience, stability, and also relationships that can help them find their own paths.

The point is to recognize that these conditions produce concrete effects. Some people grow up surrounded by references, relationships, and environments that facilitate access to certain opportunities. Others have to discover these paths on their own. This is not about condemning those who received this asset, but about understanding that it is also part of the starting conditions of a trajectory.

I do not say this to defend privilege. On the contrary. If social capital influences access to opportunities, we need to consider its absence when thinking about how to expand opportunities. Money and knowledge are important, but they may not be sufficient when the connections that help transform both into opportunity are missing.

There is a phrase attributed to Ayrton Senna that helps illuminate this dimension: “I think the basis of success in any activity first lies in having an opportunity, which usually appears not because you create that moment, but because someone comes along and opens a door.”

Senna was not talking about social capital, but the phrase offers an interesting lens through which to think about the concept. Many opportunities do not come solely from the competence of the person receiving them. They come because someone introduced, recommended, referred, invited, or simply decided to open a door. For someone who does not have an established network, finding those first doors can be an important part of the trajectory itself.

This is where the concept gains an important application to social entrepreneurship. If we want to expand the entrepreneurial capacity of people who start with less access, it is not enough to offer money, training, or technical knowledge. We also need to create environments in which relationships can be built. Mentoring, incubators, professional associations, communities, and programs that bring entrepreneurs closer to customers, suppliers, investors, and other business owners can have value not only because of what they teach, but because of the connections they make possible.

The story of Silvio Santos is an example of how this asset can also be built throughout one's own trajectory. He started as a street vendor and went through radio and television until he built an audience and a brand of enormous reach. His story is much more complex than an explanation based on social capital, but it shows how recognition, trust, communication skills, and accumulated relationships can, over time, become economic assets.

This process also happens on much smaller scales. The merchant known in the neighborhood, the professional who has built a good reputation in a particular field, the supplier who has maintained relationships for decades, or the person who knows a community deeply also accumulates social capital. The scale changes, but the mechanism is similar: relationships built over time begin to facilitate access, trust, and cooperation.

That is why different starting points matter. Some people receive, from an early age, paths that others will have to discover while building their own careers or companies. For the latter, the challenge can be greater because they need to simultaneously build competence, reputation, network, and market access.

If we want to reduce this distance, we need to look beyond money and knowledge. We need to create more opportunities for people to interact, participate in relevant environments, build trust, and establish relationships that can open new paths. Social capital cannot be transferred like money, but it can be built when we create the conditions for new relationships to emerge.

In social entrepreneurship, perhaps this is one of the most important dimensions: not merely teaching someone how to become an entrepreneur, but expanding the network to which that person can gain access.

## The Network Begins Before the Company

The most extreme examples make this phenomenon easier to see, like a magnifying glass. But social capital exists at every scale and forms through interaction, professional relationships, communities, and the environments we participate in throughout life. Not every relationship begins with an economic purpose, but over time it can generate trust, information, collaboration, or opportunity.

This is what makes social capital broader than networking. It depends on the quality, duration, and context of relationships. A relevant network is not merely a list of contacts, but a set of relationships capable of producing access, trust, and cooperation.

Money can be inherited. A network can also be received. But access is not the same as trust. A family can introduce someone, open a door, or place them in a particular environment, but the relationship will still need to be cultivated, trust confirmed, and reputation built. It is an asset that does not appear on the balance sheet or fit into a line on a pitch deck, but it can significantly reduce the distance between an idea and the market.

This difference appears mainly in speed. Someone who already has relevant relationships does not necessarily have greater competence, but they may need less time to find the right person, obtain information, validate a hypothesis, or get the first conversation. In markets where time and access matter, this reduction in friction can become a real competitive advantage.

Perhaps this is one of the most important characteristics of social capital: it is not merely something a person possesses. It is something that circulates. It can come from the family, be built throughout one's professional life, enter a company, grow within it, and follow people into new projects. It can open a door, generate an opportunity, produce a result, and, when that result strengthens reputation, make the next doors easier to open.

Therefore, understanding the construction of a business requires looking beyond what appears on the balance sheet, in the product, or in the pitch. Before a sales strategy exists, there may already be a network capable of generating initial access. Before there is a recognized brand, there may already be a reputation. Before there is a company, there is already a trajectory.

The company may be new. Its network is not.
