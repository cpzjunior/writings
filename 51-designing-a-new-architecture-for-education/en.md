# Designing a New Architecture for Education

_ A vision of education that combines individual development, mentorship, collaboration, and protection in a society undergoing transformation._

**Abstract:** I advocate a new architecture for education, organized around human development, that combines a solid common foundation with personalized pathways, progressive autonomy, and teachers acting as mentors. Artificial intelligence should expand the capacity to support each student, while projects, clubs, and collaborative networks strengthen learning, a sense of belonging, and protection. Rather than simply modernizing tools or preparing students for the labor market, we need to rethink the educational structure to develop people capable of thinking critically, learning continuously, and assuming responsibility. The challenge is to reconcile common objectives with different learning paths, ensuring that everyone has the conditions needed to develop their abilities and contribute to society.

---

I love anime, and there is a recurring element in Japanese school anime that has always made me feel somewhat envious: the life that exists beyond classes. Students participate in clubs, explore interests, interact with classmates from different school years, and build relationships that make school much more than a place to attend classes and take exams. In some stories, university admission recommendation systems also seem to recognize students' overall trajectories more broadly than their exam scores. It is an idealized representation, of course, but it leads me to consider how the educational experience could be different.

This does not mean that I want to import the Japanese system as it appears in fiction. The same school culture is also associated, in certain contexts, with intense academic pressure, rigid hierarchies, and bullying. It would be a mistake to admire the positive aspects without considering their potential costs. What interests me is identifying good ideas without reproducing the problems that may accompany them.

This curiosity led me to examine other experiences. In the Nordic countries, for example, we find approaches that value student well-being, inclusion, trust, and learning environments less centered on competition. Naturally, these countries have systems that differ from one another, just as Japan and Western countries do. There is no perfect model to copy, but there are experiences that help us understand which practices work, under what conditions, and with what limitations.

This perspective is close to my experience as a solutions architect, startup entrepreneur, and project manager. Faced with complex problems, the best strategy is rarely to reproduce an existing solution in its entirety. It is necessary to understand the needs of those involved, distinguish essential principles from circumstantial characteristics, and combine mechanisms capable of producing better results. Hypotheses then need to be tested, results evaluated, and decisions adjusted.

Why not apply this logic to education? Instead of debating which country has the best education system, we can identify the characteristics we would like to bring together in a single institution: solid academic foundations, freedom to explore interests, preparation for adult life, autonomy accompanied by guidance, excellence without excessive competition, and a community in which every student can learn, ask for help, and contribute.

The question is not whether to choose between Eastern and Western models, but how to design an educational architecture consistent with today's challenges. Artificial intelligence (AI) and robotics make this reflection especially urgent: if the skills valued by society are changing, simply updating content or digitizing classes is not enough. We need to rethink the organization of learning, the role of teachers, student progression, and the conditions necessary for each person to develop their abilities.

## An Architecture Designed for a Different Context

Education is one of the most important structures in any society, but also one of the most difficult to transform. The consolidation of the modern school and large-scale education represented an extraordinary historical advance: it expanded access to knowledge, reduced barriers to literacy, and created conditions for participation in economic, political, and social life.

The challenge lies in adapting this structure to student diversity and societal change. Many institutions remain organized around age-based classes, common curricula, predetermined learning periods, and standardized assessments. This model makes it possible to serve large populations, but it does not always address differences in prior knowledge, interests, aptitudes, and learning rates.

AI and robotics widen the gap between the traditional organization of education and contemporary needs. As automated systems take over cognitive and physical tasks, organizing education solely around the transmission of content that students are expected to reproduce becomes insufficient. Knowledge remains indispensable, but memorization does not guarantee the ability to apply it, question it, or combine it to solve new problems. Reasoning, judgment, adaptability, and responsibility are becoming increasingly important.

Adding digital tools to the existing model does not, by itself, solve this problem. Replacing books with platforms, lectures with videos, or traditional exercises with apps can improve certain aspects of learning without changing the logic that organizes the system.

A more profound change requires revisiting educational objectives, progression criteria, assessment methods, and the distribution of responsibilities between students and teachers. The goal is not to abandon the common curriculum or structured instruction, but to organize them around a broader purpose: developing people capable of learning continuously, thinking critically, making decisions, collaborating, and participating in a society undergoing transformation.

## From Uniformity to Personalized Learning

One of the main problems with the current model is confusing equal opportunity with uniform learning pathways. Offering the same content, at the same pace and with the same level of depth, may seem fair, but students do not start from the same conditions or develop all competencies at the same rate. Some spend excessive time on content they have already mastered; others move forward without consolidating essential knowledge.

Equity should lie in ensuring a solid foundational education, not in requiring everyone to follow identical paths. Everyone needs to acquire essential competencies, but they can do so through different sequences, at different paces, and with different levels of depth. Personalization should allow each pathway to respond to the student's needs and objectives without compromising the knowledge required to understand the world and continue learning.

This goes beyond presenting the same content in different ways or offering exercises of varying difficulty. Personalization means deciding which knowledge needs to be explored in greater depth, which topics can be addressed more functionally, in what sequence they should be studied, and how they will be applied. Technology can support these decisions, but the criteria must be pedagogical.

Consider biology. A student intending to pursue a career in this field will need much more in-depth knowledge than someone who chooses a different career path. This does not mean that the second student can ignore the subject. Both should understand fundamental concepts related to the human body, health, ecosystems, and the environment. The difference will lie in the depth and application of that knowledge.

There is, however, a risk: allowing students to choose only what they already like. This could turn personalization into premature specialization, restricting exposure to fields they have not yet had the opportunity to discover. To avoid this, it is necessary to combine common foundations with regular opportunities for exploration through projects, hands-on activities, clubs, and elective courses.

The curriculum could therefore bring together three components:

- Common foundation: fundamental knowledge and competencies that everyone must master.
- Adaptive pathway: variations in pace, sequence, and depth according to each student's needs and progress.
- Exploration: projects and activities that allow students to discover interests, experiment with different fields, and develop specific aptitudes.

The relative importance of each component would change throughout the educational journey. In the early years, building foundational knowledge would predominate; later, there would be more room for choices, deeper study, and specialization.

Assessment would need to accommodate this flexibility. Completing a course within the allotted time does not, by itself, prove that learning has taken place. A student may finish the program without understanding its essential concepts, while another may master the content well ahead of schedule.

Progression should increasingly depend on the demonstrated mastery of competencies, with additional support when necessary and opportunities to advance when mastery has been established. Exams, deadlines, and common benchmarks would continue to have their place, but they would no longer be treated as substitutes for learning.

## Autonomy as a Progressive Goal

If students need to participate increasingly in decisions about their educational pathways, it is necessary to define how this responsibility will be developed. A child does not initially have the ability to organize their own learning, assess their difficulties, or establish priorities in a well-founded manner. Autonomy must be built, not simply demanded.

In the early stages, instruction needs to be predominantly structured. Teachers introduce knowledge, demonstrate procedures, establish routines, and develop basic reading, writing, reasoning, attention, and organizational skills. Personalization can already help identify difficulties and adjust methods without transferring decisions to children for which they are not yet prepared.

Adolescence, around the age of 14, could serve as an initial reference point for a more deliberate transition. This age would not be a universal cutoff or determine an abrupt change. The important thing would be to gradually expand student participation in decision-making according to their ability to assume responsibility.

Some students may be able to manage projects and organize tasks independently earlier. Others will need prolonged support. In addition, autonomy varies across subject areas: a student may investigate one topic confidently while still requiring substantial guidance in another.

The transition could begin with choosing activities and setting objectives, progress to supervised projects, and eventually lead to the organization of increasingly larger portions of the educational pathway. Throughout this process, students would learn to identify gaps, evaluate sources, manage time, justify decisions, and recognize when they need help.

Freedom of choice in this model would be accompanied by guidance and responsibility. The purpose is not to let young people decide everything on their own, but to prepare them to understand alternatives, assess consequences, and correct their own course. The school would remain responsible for setting boundaries, ensuring essential knowledge, and intervening when necessary.

## The Teacher as Mentor

Personalized education requires teachers to go beyond content delivery and the assessment of results. Their role also comes to include individual support, the identification of difficulties, and guidance for each student in developing competencies.

A teacher acting as a mentor is not merely someone who offers advice or motivation. They are a professional who understands the student's journey, helps establish realistic objectives, proposes appropriate challenges, and monitors progress. They ask questions, demand justifications, and encourage the reconsideration of conclusions, developing the ability to reason independently.

This approach makes it possible to respond to different needs without reducing students to their grades or immediate interests. A student may need additional support in writing while simultaneously being ready to advance in mathematics. Another may deepen their technology knowledge through projects while being encouraged to explore fields they do not yet know.

Mentorship does not replace explicit instruction. Some concepts need to be presented systematically, some procedures require demonstration, and some competencies depend on deliberate practice. Teachers must decide when to explain, demonstrate, question, guide, or allow independent exploration.

For this model to work, working conditions need to change. Individual support requires time, appropriate training, reliable information about student progress, and a distribution of responsibilities compatible with class sizes. It is not enough to add new duties to existing responsibilities.

Technology can reduce repetitive tasks and facilitate progress analysis, but these gains must be used to improve educational support. If they serve only to increase the number of students per teacher or reduce costs, the opportunity for transformation will be wasted.

## Artificial Intelligence as Infrastructure for Personalization

AI can make part of this architecture viable. A teacher who supports dozens of students can hardly, without assistance, produce specific materials for each one, systematically identify recurring errors, and continuously adapt activities. AI tools can generate differentiated exercises, provide alternative explanations, propose problems of varying complexity, and organize information about learning difficulties.

Their main contribution would be to expand teachers' capacity to support students. A system could identify error patterns, suggest activities to consolidate specific concepts, or indicate that a student has already mastered the prerequisites for moving forward. These recommendations would need to be verified and interpreted by the responsible professional, who would decide on the appropriate intervention.

AI can also support independent study, allowing students to ask questions, test explanations, and receive immediate feedback. However, a convincing answer is not necessarily correct, and the apparent understanding of an explanation does not guarantee mastery of the content. For this reason, the educational use of these tools must be accompanied by the development of skills to verify information, compare sources, and recognize uncertainty.

This change directly affects assessment. When technology can produce texts, code, presentations, and reports, the final output is no longer sufficient evidence of individual competence. It is necessary to verify whether students understand the decisions made, can explain the reasoning used, and know how to adapt the solution to new situations.

The response should not be to indiscriminately prohibit AI or accept all its outputs as the student's own work. Institutions need to establish transparent rules about permitted uses. In some activities, using AI will be a legitimate part of the competency being developed; in others, students will need to demonstrate individual mastery without assistance.

The technology itself must be evaluated rigorously. Generating more exercises, producing materials quickly, or increasing the number of interactions does not prove educational improvement. It is necessary to verify whether students retain knowledge, apply what they have learned, and develop greater autonomy. The criterion for success must be learning, not the volume of digital activity.

## The University as a Space for Mentorship and Responsibility

In higher education, this approach can evolve into a model in which students assume greater responsibility for their own academic and professional development. Instead of relying primarily on classes organized around fixed curricula, they would combine theoretical foundations, independent study, projects, research, practical experience, and specialized guidance.

Professors would remain central to setting standards of rigor, discussing complex problems, and supervising specialized work. Their role would also include guiding pathways, identifying opportunities for deeper study, and verifying competencies demonstrated throughout the process.

Projects would gain importance by integrating knowledge and requiring solutions to problems that cannot be solved through the mechanical application of formulas. An engineering student, for example, would need to demonstrate not only theoretical mastery but also the ability to design, test, and justify solutions, recognize limitations, and work as part of a team.

This diversification would not reduce academic rigor. Students would still need to demonstrate mastery of foundational knowledge, sound reasoning, and the ability to apply knowledge in real or simulated situations. The difference would lie in gathering evidence of learning through varied experiences rather than relying almost exclusively on exams and final assignments.

In this context, students with demonstrated mastery in specific fields could serve as teaching assistants, support study sessions, and guide younger peers. This participation would require preparation, communication skills, and faculty supervision; it would not be an automatic reward for earning the highest grades.

Teaching is also a way to learn. By explaining concepts, providing feedback, and recognizing the limits of their own knowledge, teaching assistants develop competencies that complement their education. At the same time, other students gain access to a broader support network, without transferring academic responsibility away from teachers.

## Learning as a Community Experience

Individual development also depends on the relationships students establish with the people around them. Those who fear being ridiculed for asking a question, receive no support when facing difficulties, or feel excluded encounter obstacles that personalized materials alone cannot solve.

Educational institutions must create environments in which asking for help is normal, mistakes can be discussed without humiliation, and intimidation is addressed consistently. This does not mean eliminating conflict, but ensuring that clear rules, protection mechanisms, and adults responsible for intervention are in place.

Peer mentoring can contribute to this goal. A more experienced student supports someone who has just arrived, introduces them to the institution, explains its routines, and provides an initial point of contact for questions. This relationship can facilitate integration, especially during school transitions or changes in educational stage.

For the program to work, responsibilities must be defined, participants must receive guidance, and criteria for escalating difficulties must be established. Students should not be held responsible for resolving situations involving violence, abuse, or psychological distress that require professional intervention. Protection depends on accessible reporting channels and effective institutional responses.

Clubs offer another opportunity for integration. Activities involving programming, robotics, science, philosophy, literature, astronomy, and strategy games allow students from different school years to come together around shared interests. In addition to developing specific competencies, these spaces create opportunities for participation and recognition that do not always emerge in the classroom.

A student who struggles in a particular subject may discover that their abilities are valued in another context. Roles also alternate: someone may be a beginner in one activity and help classmates in another. This dynamic fosters relationships that are less dependent on a single measure of performance and reinforces the idea that everyone can teach and learn.

Even so, clubs and peer mentoring programs do not automatically produce inclusion. They can reproduce hierarchies or exclude participants. Access must be ensured, activities must be monitored, and harmful behavior must be addressed.

Collaboration should also be part of classroom instruction through group projects, team research, and joint problem-solving. To prevent some students from doing all the work while others simply receive the result, each participant must make an identifiable contribution and demonstrate what they have learned.

Over time, students can move from receiving support to becoming active participants in the community. This network across different academic cohorts expands learning opportunities, provided that it remains supervised by professionals and supported by rules governing conduct and protection.

## Preparing for Life, Not Just Exams

Academic knowledge is indispensable, but it does not encompass all the competencies needed for adult life. Managing a budget, understanding contracts, evaluating credit options, interpreting rights and obligations, communicating professionally, and recognizing risks are skills that directly influence individual independence.

These topics can be incorporated into the curriculum through practical situations that bring together mathematics, language, citizenship, and reasoning. Preparing a budget, comparing financing proposals, or analyzing a contractual clause allows students to apply school concepts to concrete problems. The purpose is not to bring forward professional specialization, but to reduce the gap between understanding an idea and being able to use it.

Ethics education also deserves a place. Students need to understand conflicts of interest, transparency, and responsibility, as well as the differences between legality and ethics. They should also learn to recognize fraud, improper favoritism, and corruption. These issues are relevant to anyone who may participate in organizations or assume public or private responsibilities.

Preventing bullying, violence, and risk-taking behaviors associated with substance use requires similar attention. Education should help students recognize dangerous situations, deal with social pressure, and seek support. These initiatives must be age-appropriate and evidence-based, without replacing specialized intervention when necessary.

Preparing for life therefore means developing the ability to make informed decisions, assess consequences, recognize limitations, respect others, and take responsibility for one's own choices.

## The Necessary Institutional Architecture

A proposal of this nature cannot depend solely on the initiative of motivated teachers or exceptionally well-resourced schools. To work at scale, it must be reflected in curricula, schedules, assessment systems, teacher training programs, and funding mechanisms.

The first step is to define the intended outcomes: what knowledge everyone must master, which competencies they need to develop progressively, and in which areas there is room for choice and specialization. From there, it will be possible to organize flexible pathways and establish monitoring methods consistent with the objectives.

Implementation should be managed as an institutional transformation project, with clear responsibilities, adequate resources, and monitoring indicators. It is necessary to verify whether teachers have time to guide students, whether tools are reliable, whether assessment criteria are consistent, and whether opportunities are genuinely accessible to everyone.

Equity is a central condition. Personalization can widen inequalities if advantaged students have access to better technology, mentors, and practical experiences, while others remain in rigid structures with little support. The education system must ensure a solid common foundation and effective access to opportunities for deeper study and exploration.

It would also be unwise to implement all changes simultaneously without evaluating their consequences. Pilot projects can test models of progressive autonomy, mentorship, AI use, and club organization before broader expansion. Evaluation should consider grades and completion rates, but also knowledge retention, application skills, quality of reasoning, motivation, and social integration, while examining differences across student groups.

Finally, education should not be reduced to preparation for the labor market. Employability and technological adaptation are relevant objectives, but society also needs people capable of understanding history, appreciating literature and the arts, discussing philosophical questions, and participating in civic life. A broad education makes it possible to interpret the world and make decisions that extend beyond immediate professional needs.

## An Education Organized Around Human Development

The proposed architecture brings together practices that already exist independently in different institutions: a common foundation of knowledge, personalized pathways, progressive autonomy, assessment based on demonstrated competencies, teachers with an expanded mentoring role, judicious use of AI, and structured learning and protection communities.

Implementation would begin with consolidating foundational knowledge and exposing students to different fields. As students develop, opportunities for choice and deeper study would increase, accompanied by responsibilities appropriate to their abilities. In higher education, this logic would extend to independent study, research, projects, and the participation of more experienced students in guiding newcomers.

AI would support material adaptation and the identification of difficulties, while teachers and teaching assistants would provide human guidance. Clubs, collaborative projects, and peer mentoring programs would expand opportunities for collaboration and a sense of belonging, with institutional mechanisms designed to preserve safety and inclusion.

The success of this architecture would depend on balancing three commitments: ensuring that everyone masters essential knowledge, allowing each person to develop their abilities, and ensuring that no one lacks access to the conditions necessary to learn. Flexibility in learning pathways must not compromise foundational knowledge, just as the pursuit of autonomy must not serve as a justification for withdrawing support from those who need it.

System evaluation must also go beyond the amount of content taught, the number of diplomas issued, or the number of students who pass. It is necessary to observe what students can understand, explain, and do; how they apply knowledge to new situations; whether they can continue learning independently; and how they participate in the communities to which they belong.

Designing a new architecture for education ultimately means replacing uniform learning paths with a structure that combines common objectives with different ways of achieving them. Instruction should provide the foundations, mentorship should guide development, technology should expand the capacity for support, and the community should offer opportunities for collaboration and participation.

The goal is not to produce people who simply depend less on school because they have more efficient tools. It is to develop individuals capable of thinking for themselves, recognizing what they still need to learn, working with others, and taking responsibility for their contribution to society. To achieve this, education must stop organizing itself predominantly around the execution of a uniform program and begin treating each person's development as its central purpose, without abandoning the collective responsibility to ensure that everyone has the conditions needed to progress.
