AI can expand the analytical capacity of public officials without replacing human responsibility for public decisions. A useful experiment would treat AI as an advisory layer operating between public participation/information and human decision-making.

A citizen can use an LLM to research an issue, structure arguments, and formulate a clearer petition or demand. This suggests an analogous capability on the government side: AI could process the large volume of complaints, suggestions, proposals, reports, legislation, indicators, and administrative records received by public institutions, transforming fragmented expressions into structured analytical material. It could classify and group demands, identify recurring patterns, distinguish individual cases from broader problems, relate demands to existing data, and separate descriptions of problems from proposed solutions. The official would retain responsibility for interpreting context, establishing priorities, weighing interests, and deciding.

An AI advisor could research legislation and policies, compare experiences from other jurisdictions, identify potential impacts and risks, organize arguments for and against, relate proposals to population demands, and monitor how problems evolve. Its principal value would be systematic processing of a much larger information volume, allowing human advisors to spend less time searching, organizing, and classifying information and more time on interpretation, context, negotiation, and judgment.

A single AI model should not necessarily provide the entire analytical layer. Public policies have fiscal, social, economic, environmental, legal, and administrative dimensions, and different models may identify different risks or interpret the same evidence differently. Multiple models from different organizations could receive identical information and predefined analytical criteria and independently assess the same issue. Their disagreements should be preserved rather than collapsed into an artificial consensus. If most models support a proposal while one identifies a concern, the relevant question is why: the divergent model may have identified a fiscal risk, a population impact, a different interpretation of the evidence, or insufficient information. Across many cases, recurring differences could also reveal systematic characteristics of individual models.

The purpose of multiple models would therefore not be to create an artificial vote or replace human judgment. Their purpose would be to create an analytical map showing convergence, divergence, arguments, risks, uncertainties, and missing information. The usefulness of AI would include expanding the questions considered before a decision, not merely generating a recommendation. A valuable output may be an overlooked consequence, an untested assumption, or a missing piece of evidence rather than an instruction to approve or reject a policy.

The same architecture could operate before legislation exists. Citizens could submit suggestions, complaints, and proposals in natural language. AI could group related expressions, identify recurring problems, connect demands to affected regions or groups, and provide representatives with a structured representation of societal demands. Multiple models could then research existing legislation, comparable policies, intervention alternatives, potential costs and benefits, risks, and indirect effects. The output would serve as an input to legislative work rather than necessarily becoming a ready-made proposal. AI could subsequently assist with drafting, checking relationships with existing legislation, identifying regulatory conflicts, and organizing supporting arguments, while humans would assess relevance, feasibility, and appropriateness. This creates a continuous flow: society expresses problems and demands; AI organizes and analyzes them; multiple models examine possible responses; human representatives decide which issues advance.

AI-generated analysis must explicitly preserve uncertainty and limitations. LLMs can produce coherent and apparently well-supported conclusions that are nevertheless wrong because of misinterpretation, omitted variables, inappropriate premises, or unsupported claims. Public-policy uncertainty cannot be eliminated simply by increasing information, and legitimate political choices between competing priorities and values cannot be reduced to technical optimization. AI can structure these issues but cannot convert political judgment into a purely technical problem.

Consequently, each analysis should record the information used, sources consulted, assumptions adopted, limitations identified, and cases in which available information is insufficient. Uncertainty should be surfaced rather than replaced by speculation. Multiple models do not solve the reliability problem: agreement can result from shared sources, training patterns, or common limitations, while disagreement can reflect genuine ambiguity rather than one model being incorrect.

The system therefore requires an auditing layer capable of reconstructing each analysis: model and version, supplied information, prompt, sources, generated response, caveats, comparisons with other models, and subsequent human review. An auditable history would allow evaluation of model quality, identification of recurring errors, analysis of when the tool is useful, and reconstruction of what information and analyses were available when a public decision was made. The experiment should explicitly seek failure cases rather than merely demonstrating that AI works. A useful system must make clear whether an analysis is convergent and well-supported, contains substantial disagreement, or lacks sufficient information for a reliable conclusion.

The proposed experiment is an artificial advisory panel for public officials. Multiple AI models would receive the same information about public demands, legislative proposals, or public problems and independently analyze them according to predefined procedures. The system could monitor legislative agendas, organize citizen expressions, identify recurring demands, research legislation and comparable experiences, assess possible impacts, and record convergence and divergence among models while preserving sources, assumptions, information, and uncertainties.

Officials would receive this as a second analytical layer before making decisions: multiple perspectives, areas of consensus, disagreements, identified risks, and unresolved information gaps rather than a single recommendation. Human responsibility for the decision would remain unchanged.

The experiment should evaluate the entire process rather than merely the quality of individual AI responses. Relevant measures include analytical consistency, ability to identify risks and relevant information, frequency and nature of model disagreements, types of errors, amount of human intervention required for validation, and time and cost compared with traditional advisory processes. It should also identify where the architecture fails: decisions may depend too heavily on local knowledge or judgments that cannot be structured effectively; some models may have specific strengths; disagreements may require additional human analysis; and some policy domains may simply be unsuitable for this approach.

The central hypothesis is therefore narrower than whether AI can analyze public policy. The question is whether an architecture combining multiple models, controlled information, predefined analytical procedures, human review, explicit uncertainty, and traceability can provide a useful advisory layer for public officials.

The objective is not to place AI where decision-makers currently are, but alongside them: increasing the amount of information and number of perspectives available while preserving human responsibility and making the machines' limitations visible and auditable. The fundamental research question is whether several AI systems can help humans make public decisions with broader analysis, while allowing everyone involved to determine where those systems are useful, where they fail, and how their conclusions can be challenged.
