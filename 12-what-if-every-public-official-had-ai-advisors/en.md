# What if every public official had AI advisors?

_A proposal for an experiment on the use of artificial intelligence to support participation, analysis, and public policy formulation._

**Summary:** I propose investigating whether artificial intelligence can expand the capacity of public officials without replacing their responsibility for decision-making. The idea is to use multiple models to organize demands from the population, analyze proposals, identify risks, and present independent perspectives, preserving disagreements, uncertainties, and traceability. In this way, AI would function as an advisory layer before the decision, expanding the range of analysis available to the official. The central point is to test, in practice, whether this architecture can produce better decisions precisely by making the information, perspectives, and limitations involved more visible.

---

Recently, I came across a discussion about the use of artificial intelligence by citizens in the United Kingdom to draft petitions to Parliament. The British petition system is particularly interesting because it establishes a formal connection between a public expression and the political process: a petition that reaches 10,000 signatures receives a response from the government and, if it reaches 100,000, it may be considered for debate in Parliament.

The arrival of artificial intelligence in this process creates a new possibility. A citizen can use an LLM to research a problem, organize arguments, structure a proposal, and turn an idea that is still poorly developed into a clearer petition. Recent research has already begun to investigate precisely this phenomenon, including the effects of AI use on the production of petitions and on political participation.

Thinking about this led to a question on the other side of the process. If artificial intelligence can increase a citizen's ability to formulate and present a demand to the government, why could it not also increase the ability of those who receive these demands to understand, organize, and analyze them?

This inversion seems more interesting to me than the much more common idea of using AI to replace decision-makers. The problem I envision is different: how can different artificial intelligence systems be used to expand the analytical capacity of public officials without transferring responsibility for the decision to them?

## The other side of participation

Public participation produces information, but information is not necessarily useful knowledge for a decision. A municipality may receive thousands of complaints, suggestions, and requests over the course of a year. A legislator may accumulate messages from citizens, associations, and organized groups. Government departments may have reports, studies, indicators, and administrative records produced by different areas. The challenge then becomes transforming this fragmented collection of expressions into a structured view of the problems that need to be addressed.

This process requires time and work. It is necessary to identify similar demands, separate isolated cases from recurring problems, understand which issues affect different groups within the population, relate expressions to existing data, and, above all, distinguish what represents a problem from a specific proposal for solving it.

A suggestion submitted by a citizen may be relevant even when it appears only once. Likewise, thousands of expressions about the same subject may represent different symptoms of a larger problem. Without a structure capable of organizing this material, a significant portion of the information produced by society itself may never reach a stage of deeper analysis.

This is where artificial intelligence can assume an interesting role. Instead of deciding which demands should be addressed, a system could act on the volume of information reaching the government. It could classify expressions, group similar topics, identify patterns, relate demands to existing data, and present different interpretations of the problems found.

The objective would be to transform dispersed information into analytical material. The decision about what to do would remain with the public official, but they could arrive at that decision with a broader and more structured view of what the population is presenting.

## A virtual advisor

The idea of an AI advisor starts from a fairly simple division of responsibilities. The public official remains responsible for interpreting the context, establishing priorities, considering the different interests involved, and making the decision. Artificial intelligence acts before that, organizing information and producing analyses that can support this judgment.

In this role, a virtual advisor could research existing legislation and policies, compare experiences from other cities, identify possible impacts, assess risks, organize arguments for and against, and relate a proposal to the demands presented by the population. It could also monitor the evolution of certain problems and update its analyses as new information becomes available.

The main advantage would not simply be the speed at which these tasks could be performed, but the possibility of applying them systematically to a much larger volume of information. A human team may need to select which documents, projects, or expressions deserve more detailed analysis. An AI system could perform this initial layer of screening across the entire available set and indicate which subjects present greater complexity, recurrence, potential impact, or need for investigation.

This would allow human work to be shifted toward where it is most needed. Instead of spending a large portion of their time searching for information, organizing documents, and classifying demands, the human advisor could receive previously structured material and devote more attention to interpretation, context, negotiation, and decision-making.

In this model, artificial intelligence would not be an alternative to a public advisor, but a way of expanding their capacity. The question the experiment aims to investigate is precisely how far this expansion can go and which activities can be performed by AI without compromising the quality of the analysis preceding a public decision.

## Using multiple AIs to simulate different perspectives

If the proposal is to use artificial intelligence to support public decisions, it does not seem appropriate to concentrate all this capacity in a single model. A public policy rarely has only one dimension. The same proposal may involve fiscal, social, economic, environmental, legal, and administrative issues, and different systems may assign different weights to each of them.

Therefore, the proposal would not simply be to create several advisors using the same model with different prompts. The idea would be to use language models developed by different organizations, subjected to the same information and the same analytical criteria, so that each produces an independent assessment of the same issue.

Diversity among the models would be an important characteristic of the system. Different models may interpret a proposal in different ways, identify distinct risks, or reach divergent conclusions. This difference should not necessarily be treated as a failure. In complex decisions, it may be precisely an additional source of information.

Imagine that different models analyze a proposal and most are in favor, while one recommends rejecting it. The most relevant result would not necessarily be the score. It would be understanding the source of the divergence. Did the model identify a fiscal risk that the others did not notice? Did it find a possible impact on a particular group within the population? Did it interpret available information differently? Did it consider the existing data insufficient to support a conclusion?

The system could preserve these differences and present them to the official in a structured manner, showing the points of convergence, the disagreements, and the arguments used by each model. Instead of producing a single answer to a complex question, the technology could produce a map of the different possible interpretations.

This approach would also make it possible to study the behavior of the models themselves. By analyzing a significant volume of public policies, it would be possible to observe whether certain systems exhibit recurring patterns, whether some disagreements appear consistently, and which types of analysis each model seems to perform better.

The use of multiple AIs, therefore, would not aim to create an artificial vote to replace human decision-making. The objective would be to create an analytical layer with independent perspectives, capable of expanding the official's field of vision before they need to make a decision.

## A second opinion before the decision

The most interesting result of this structure may not be to produce a recommendation, but to expand the range of analysis available before a decision. Different models could receive exactly the same proposal, the same information, and the same criteria, but produce different analyses of its possible effects.

The experiment would start from this premise. Each proposal would be submitted to the models independently, following a predefined protocol. Each system would have to present its assessment, make its arguments explicit, and identify benefits, risks, potential impacts, uncertainties, and information it considered insufficient for a conclusion.

The responses would then be compared. Rather than seeking a single answer, the system could identify the points of convergence and divergence among the models. When different AIs reached the same conclusion through similar paths, this could indicate a recurring perception about a particular aspect of the proposal. When they diverged, the divergence itself would become an object of analysis.

Over hundreds of proposals, it would also be possible to observe patterns. Some models might demonstrate greater sensitivity to fiscal issues, others to social impacts, and others to implementation risks. Recurring differences between systems could emerge, as could situations in which certain models identified aspects that the others did not notice.

In this scenario, the usefulness of AI would not necessarily lie in providing the correct answer. It would also lie in expanding the set of questions asked before the decision. A good support tool does not need to conclude that a policy should be approved or rejected. It may be more useful when it can point out a consequence that had not been considered, information that is missing, or an assumption that deserves to be questioned.

The decision would remain human. What would change would be the number of perspectives available when it is made.

## From participation to public policy formulation

The application of this structure could begin even before a legislative proposal exists. Instead of using artificial intelligence only to analyze bills that have already reached the legislature, it could be used to process what comes directly from the population and transform dispersed expressions into inputs for public policy formulation.

Imagine a system in which citizens can submit suggestions, complaints, and proposals in natural language. AI could group similar expressions, identify recurring problems, relate demands to affected regions or groups, and present representatives with a structured view of the issues being raised by the population.

Based on this material, different models could assume the role of analysts. They could research existing legislation, find similar policies implemented in other jurisdictions, identify intervention alternatives, and estimate, within the limits of the available information, the possible costs, benefits, risks, and indirect effects of each alternative.

The result would not necessarily be a ready-made proposal, but a starting point for legislative work. A recurring demand from the population could be transformed into a clearly defined public problem, accompanied by different possible ways of addressing it and the main questions that would need to be answered before a decision.

At a later stage, artificial intelligence could assist in drafting a proposal, relating it to existing legislation, pointing out possible regulatory conflicts, and organizing the arguments supporting its creation. The resulting document would then be submitted to human analysis, which would remain responsible for assessing its relevance, feasibility, and appropriateness.

This would create a continuous flow between participation and policymaking: the population presents problems and demands, artificial intelligence organizes and analyzes this material, different models produce perspectives on possible solutions, and the public representative decides which issues should move forward.

Technology, in this process, would not replace political representation. It could make one of the fundamental functions of that representation more efficient: transforming society's demands into proposals that can be discussed, evaluated, and potentially converted into public policies.

## Uncertainty, limitations, and auditing

The use of artificial intelligence in public decisions also requires recognizing what it cannot do. An LLM can produce an analysis that is coherent, well structured, and apparently well founded and still be wrong. It can misinterpret information, ignore a relevant variable, use an inappropriate premise, or present as fact something that lacks sufficient support. In public policy, a plausible answer is not necessarily a correct answer.

This problem is particularly important because public decisions involve uncertainties that cannot be eliminated simply by increasing the amount of information. A policy may produce effects different from those anticipated, certain information may be incomplete, and different groups may be affected in different ways. Even when the facts are known, there may still be a legitimate choice between different priorities and values. Artificial intelligence can help organize these issues, but it cannot turn a political decision into a purely technical problem.

For this reason, the proposed system would need to be built to preserve uncertainty rather than conceal it. Each analysis should record which information was used, which sources were considered, which assumptions were adopted, and which limitations were identified by the model itself. When there is insufficient information to support a conclusion, this should appear as a result of the analysis rather than being filled in with a speculative answer.

The use of multiple models also does not eliminate this problem. If different AIs reach the same conclusion, that does not necessarily mean the conclusion is correct. The models may share sources, training patterns, or similar limitations. Likewise, a divergence between models does not necessarily mean that one of them is wrong. It may reveal a real ambiguity in the issue being analyzed.

This makes an auditing layer necessary. The objective would not only be to record the result produced by the AIs, but to make it possible to reconstruct how that result was obtained. Which version of the model was used? What information was provided? What was the prompt? Which sources were consulted? What response was produced? Did the model provide any caveat? Did other models reach the same conclusion? Was the result subsequently reviewed by a human?

A system of this kind should produce an auditable record of each analysis. This would make it possible to track not only the official's final decision, but also the information and analyses that were available when it was made. Over time, this history could be used to evaluate the quality of the models, identify error patterns, and determine in which types of decisions the tool is actually useful.

Auditing is also important because the objective of the experiment should not be to prove that artificial intelligence works. It should be possible to find situations in which it fails. A good decision-support system needs to make its own limits visible, allowing the official to know when they are facing a robust analysis, when there are divergent opinions, and when there simply is not enough information for a reliable conclusion.

It is precisely this combination of multiple perspectives, transparency about uncertainties, and the possibility of auditing that transforms a set of LLMs into a decision-support tool. Without this, we would simply have responses produced by language models. With it, we begin to have a process that can be observed, compared, and evaluated.

## A proposal for an experiment

Based on these premises, the experiment could be structured as a kind of artificial advisory panel for public officials. Different artificial intelligence models would receive the same information about public demands, legislative proposals, or public problems and produce independent analyses, following previously defined criteria and procedures.

The system could monitor a legislative agenda, organize expressions received from the population, identify recurring demands, analyze proposals, research legislation and similar experiences, identify possible impacts, and record points of convergence and divergence among the different models. Each analysis should also preserve the information used, the sources consulted, the assumptions adopted, and the uncertainties identified, allowing the process to be subsequently audited.

The result would be presented to the official as a second layer of analysis before the decision. Instead of a single recommendation, they could view the different perspectives produced by the models, the points of consensus, the disagreements, the risks identified, and the information that remains insufficient. The decision would remain the official's responsibility, but it would be made in light of a broader and more structured set of information.

The experiment could then evaluate not only the responses produced by the AIs, but the usefulness of the entire process. It would be possible to measure the consistency of the analyses, the ability to identify risks and relevant information, the frequency of disagreements among models, the types of errors found, and the degree of human intervention required to validate the results. It would also be possible to evaluate time and cost compared with different traditional forms of advisory support.

This would make it possible to test a more specific hypothesis than simply asking whether AI is capable of analyzing public policies. The question would be whether an architecture based on multiple models, controlled information, human review, and traceability can produce a useful advisory layer for public officials.

The experiment could also reveal where this approach does not work. Perhaps certain types of decisions are suitable for this model, while others depend too heavily on local knowledge or on judgments that cannot be reduced to structured criteria. Perhaps some models exhibit specific advantages. Perhaps disagreements are so frequent that an additional stage of human analysis becomes necessary. These results would be just as important as any successful cases.

The idea that gave rise to this article was a news story about citizens using artificial intelligence to expand their ability to participate in the political process in the United Kingdom. The next step seems like a reasonable hypothesis: if technology can help society formulate and present its demands more effectively, perhaps it can also help the public sector better understand, organize, and analyze what it receives.

This is not about putting artificial intelligence in the place of those who make decisions. It is about investigating whether we can put it alongside those who make decisions, offering more information, more perspectives, and an analytical capability that can be audited.

In the end, perhaps the more interesting question is not whether an AI can make a public decision. It is whether we can build systems in which several AIs help human beings make better public decisions, knowing exactly where these machines are useful, where they fail, and how their analyses can be questioned.
