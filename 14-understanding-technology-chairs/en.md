# Understanding Technology Roles

_Technology roles are not distinguished by how much technology each professional knows, but by the type of decision they need to make and the level of responsibility they have for the results._

**Summary:** I argue that a career in technology should not be viewed as a ladder of positions, but as a journey in which each role brings different problems, responsibilities, and competencies. Experience helps, but it does not automatically prepare someone for the next role: it is necessary to deliberately develop the repertoire required before taking on a new responsibility. As scope increases, technical knowledge does not lose importance, but begins to combine with people, processes, business, strategy, and decision-making. Therefore, more important than asking what the next position is, is deciding what problem I want to be prepared to solve and starting to prepare for it today.

---

A career in technology is often presented as a ladder. Developer, senior, tech lead, architect, manager, director, executive. The impression is that all it takes is to accumulate experience, demonstrate competence, and move on to the next rung. The more time in one’s career, the greater the responsibility and, consequently, the higher the position.

But perhaps there is another way to look at this trajectory. Instead of a ladder, we can see it as a journey. At certain points, we can follow a vertical trajectory, taking on increasingly greater responsibilities within the organization. At others, we can choose a specialization trajectory, deepening our technical knowledge and increasing our influence without necessarily taking on a management position. There are also professionals who move between these possibilities throughout their careers.

The fundamental point is that changing roles does not simply represent a more senior version of the same job. It represents a change in the problem we need to solve. What is expected of an architect is not simply to be a more experienced developer. What is expected of a director is not simply to be a manager with more people and a larger budget. With each change of role, the questions we need to answer, the decisions we need to make, and the competencies we need to develop change. And this is precisely where experience alone ceases to be sufficient.

## A Career as a Journey

An excellent developer does not automatically become an excellent architect, just as an excellent architect does not necessarily become an excellent manager, or an excellent manager an excellent director. Accumulated experience certainly helps, but each transition requires new competencies, new references, and, above all, a new way of thinking about the work. The professional stops dealing only with the problems of the role they occupy and begins to take on problems of another nature, which require knowledge that was not always necessary at the previous stage of their career.

There is a particularly strong tendency in technology to associate seniority with hierarchical advancement, as if the natural next step for a technical professional were to lead a team, then a department, and eventually the entire technology organization. This view ends up treating management as a natural evolution of a technical career, when, in practice, management is not an engineering promotion. It is another discipline, with different problems, responsibilities, and success criteria. A good developer is evaluated primarily by the quality of what they build; a good manager needs to be able to create the conditions for other people to build well, in addition to making decisions about priorities, capacity, people, and resources. Previous experience remains valuable, but it does not replace the knowledge required for the new role.

The same logic applies to architecture. Becoming an architect does not simply mean being a more experienced developer or someone who knows more technologies. The architect needs to understand systems as a whole, deal with dependencies, integration, security, scalability, costs, organizational constraints, and the technological horizon, in addition to evaluating the trade-offs involved in each decision. They often also need to convince different groups of a particular approach without having formal authority over the teams that will implement it. The work changes because the problem changes: it is no longer focused on implementing a solution and instead involves defining how different parts of the organization and technology should relate to one another.

There is also a specialization trajectory, which is often treated as an alternative to a management career, when in reality it can represent a professional evolution just as significant as any hierarchical trajectory. A professional can remain deeply technical and continue to evolve as a specialist, taking on increasingly complex problems, becoming a reference in certain domains, and expanding their influence over technical decisions within the organization, without necessarily taking on people management. Along this path, seniority does not need to be measured by the number of people who report to the professional, but by the complexity of the problems they can solve, the quality of the decisions they influence, and the knowledge they can disseminate.

There is no trajectory superior to the others. There are different trajectories, and each requires a different set of competencies. The question, therefore, should not simply be what the next position is, but what kind of problem we want to be prepared to solve. It is from this choice that it makes sense to think about what we need to study, what experiences we need to seek, and what competencies we need to develop before occupying the next role.

## What Changes When We Change Roles

Perhaps the best way to understand the different roles in technology is to ask what problem each one exists to solve. When we are starting our careers, our focus is normally on building. How do we implement a feature? How do we fix a problem? How do we make a system more reliable, performant, or sustainable? As we expand our responsibility, these questions begin to change. This does not necessarily mean that we stop worrying about technology, but that we begin to see problems on a different scale, with more variables, more dependencies, and more people involved in the decision.

The Tech Lead begins to expand this horizon. It is no longer enough to know how to implement a solution; it is necessary to help other people decide how to implement it. This involves establishing standards, reviewing technical decisions, removing impediments, guiding less experienced professionals, and raising the quality of the team as a whole. The result of the work is no longer measured only by what the professional produces themselves and instead also depends on what they can enable the team to produce. It is a first important change in perspective: individual capability remains relevant, but begins to be replaced by the ability to multiply knowledge and guide decisions.

The architect changes the horizon again. The question is no longer only how to build and becomes how to structure the solution. What components should exist? How should they relate to one another? Where are the main risks? What are the trade-offs between cost, complexity, security, performance, flexibility, and schedule? And, above all, how can a decision made today limit or enable the organization several years from now? The architect needs to combine technical depth with a broader view of the system and the context in which it exists. They also need to exercise influence without necessarily having formal authority over the teams responsible for implementation.

The project manager works on another dimension. Their problem is to transform an objective into a coordinated delivery, managing scope, schedule, costs, dependencies, risks, and expectations. They do not necessarily need to build the solution, but they need to understand well enough what is being built to identify risks, challenge assumptions, coordinate different groups, and make decisions when conflicts arise between schedule, scope, cost, and quality. Their work is less related to the technical solution itself and more to the ability to organize the effort required to make it happen.

The technology manager begins to manage a capability, rather than just an initiative. People, budget, vendors, processes, priorities, operations, hiring, talent development, and technological evolution become central to the role. The question is no longer simply how to deliver a particular project, but how to build and sustain an area capable of continuously delivering what the organization needs. This requires an important change in perspective: the manager no longer manages only the work, but also the conditions that allow the work to happen.

The director expands this scope even further. Their responsibility begins to involve multiple teams, capabilities, and priorities, often spanning different areas of the organization. They need to decide where to invest, which capabilities need to be built, which initiatives should be prioritized, which risks can be accepted, which vendors and partners make sense, and how technology should relate to the other areas of the company. At this level, understanding technology remains fundamental, but it is no longer sufficient. Finance, strategy, governance, people management, negotiation, and executive communication begin to occupy an increasingly large portion of the work.

This is where roles such as CIO, CTO, and CISO appear, and they should not necessarily be understood as steps above the technology director. They are different executive roles, with mandates that vary according to the organization. The CIO tends to look at technology as a company capability, connecting systems, information, infrastructure, and transformation to business needs. The CTO may be closer to technology as a product, platform, or competitive differentiator, especially in organizations where engineering and product are central elements of the strategy. The CISO, in turn, has security, cyber risk, information protection, and resilience as their center of gravity. Titles may change from company to company, but the logic remains: the broader the scope of the role, the greater the responsibility for decisions that extend beyond the technical discipline itself.

Finally, there is the CEO, and here the change in perspective is even more significant. Technology ceases to necessarily be the central object of the decision and becomes one of the dimensions of the problem. The question begins to involve the organization as a whole: strategy, market, capital, people, operations, risks, and results. A CEO may have come from technology, finance, operations, sales, or any other area, because their primary responsibility is not to master a specific functional discipline, but to make decisions about the business as a system. For someone who built their career in technology, this may be the greatest change of all: moving from asking which technological solution we should build to asking which business problem we actually need to solve.

## Experience Does Not Necessarily Imply Readiness

One of the things that interests me most about this journey is the difference between experience and preparation. Experience is what we accumulate by performing a particular function over time. Preparation is what we deliberately study, practice, and develop in order to take on a different responsibility. The two are related, but they are not equivalent. It is entirely possible to spend ten years developing software and reach the end of that period with ten years of experience. It is also possible to arrive with one year of experience repeated ten times. Career length, by itself, does not guarantee growth, because experience only produces learning when we are able to reflect on it, expand our repertoire, and seek new challenges.

This distinction becomes even more important when we change roles. A professional can spend years as a manager, accumulating experience managing teams and delivering projects, and still not be prepared to take on a directorship. The new role may require knowledge that simply was not part of their previous routine: budgeting, strategy, organizational structure, portfolio management, executive negotiation, governance, and relationships with other areas of the company. Experience as a manager remains extremely relevant, but it does not automatically cover all the knowledge required for the new responsibility.

Therefore, it may be more productive to think about our careers by looking not only at the role we occupy today, but also at the one we intend to occupy tomorrow. If I want to become an architect, I should not wait to receive the title before I start studying architecture. If I want to take on a management position, I should not wait to receive my first team before I start studying people management. If I intend to reach a director position, I should not wait to receive responsibility for a significant budget before I start understanding finance, strategy, and management. Preparation can and, in my view, should happen before the formal change in position.

This does not mean trying to anticipate everything that will be required or turning a career into a rigid sequence of courses and certifications. It means recognizing that each role has its own set of knowledge, competencies, and ways of thinking, and that part of the responsibility for professional development lies in identifying these gaps before they become a problem. Experience shows us how the role we occupy works; study and preparation help us build a repertoire for the role we want to occupy.

In the end, perhaps one of the best ways to assess whether we are prepared for the next step is to ask not only whether we have done it before, but whether we have already developed the tools necessary to do what will be expected of us. A promotion can formalize a change in responsibility, but it is not what produces preparation. Ideally, when the new role arrives, a significant part of the preparation should already have begun.

## What to Study at Each Stage

The most interesting part of this journey, for me, is precisely understanding how the personal curriculum needs to change as the scope of responsibility increases. There is no definitive list of knowledge that will turn someone into a good technology professional, because what is relevant depends on the role occupied and the problems it needs to solve. Still, there is a fairly clear progression: the more we expand our scope, the more we need to combine technical knowledge with knowledge about people, processes, business, and decision-making.

### The Technical Foundation

At the beginning of a career, technical knowledge naturally occupies most of the space. Computer science fundamentals, software engineering, data structures, operating systems, networks, databases, infrastructure, cloud, security, and the specific technologies of the domain in which we operate form the foundation upon which everything else will be built. Over time, however, the question ceases to be simply knowing how to implement a solution and becomes understanding why one solution is better than another. Performance, maintainability, security, cost, simplicity, scalability, and operational impact begin to become part of the decision. Technical seniority is not only about knowing more tools, but about developing better criteria for choosing among them.

### Technical Leadership

When we take on a technical leadership position, the curriculum begins to incorporate a dimension that does not necessarily appear in the traditional training of a technology professional. Communication, decision-making, prioritization, technical review, mentoring, conflict management, and consensus building become fundamental competencies. The challenge stops being to individually produce quality code and becomes creating the conditions for a team to consistently produce quality software. This requires learning to multiply knowledge, delegate decisions, establish standards, and deal with different levels of experience within the same team.

### Architecture

In architecture, technical knowledge expands horizontally again. Software architecture, integration, messaging, data, security, infrastructure, observability, resilience, scalability, costs, and architectural patterns become part of the necessary repertoire. But there is an even more important competency: learning to work with trade-offs. Architecture rarely consists of finding the perfect solution, because virtually every relevant decision involves some form of compromise. A solution may be simpler but less flexible; more robust but more expensive; faster to implement but harder to evolve. The architect needs to understand these relationships and be able to explain why a particular choice makes sense within the existing constraints.

### Project Management

When entering project management, the center of gravity shifts again. Planning, estimates, risks, dependencies, budget, communication, governance, and negotiation become important parts of the repertoire. The project manager does not need to replace technical specialists, but needs to understand the work well enough to identify risks, challenge assumptions, and coordinate different groups around a common objective. Technical competence remains useful, but the outcome becomes much more dependent on the ability to organize the collective effort than on the ability to directly execute part of the solution.

### Technology Management

In technology management, the scope becomes even broader. People management, hiring, talent development, budget, vendors, metrics, operational capacity, processes, governance, and technology strategy become part of the necessary repertoire. The manager is no longer concerned only with delivering a specific initiative, but with the ability of the area to operate continuously. This means understanding how to build teams, allocate resources, establish priorities, measure results, and build a sustainable operation. It is an important change because the object of management ceases to be only the work and begins to include the very conditions that allow the work to be carried out.

### The Directorate

At the director level, the financial and strategic dimension gains even more weight. It is necessary to understand budget, costs, return on investment, initiative portfolio, risk, governance, organizational structure, transformation, and relationships with other executive areas. The question ceases to be only “which technology should we use?” and becomes “which capability do we need to build, how much are we willing to invest in it, and what result do we expect to obtain?”. The director needs to be able to translate technology into different languages within the organization, connecting technical decisions to financial, operational, and strategic impact.

### Executive Positions

In executive positions, such as CIO, CTO, or CISO, study needs to advance even further into business strategy. Business model, corporate finance, risk management, governance, competitive strategy, organizational transformation, market, and executive communication become as relevant as the function’s specific technical knowledge. This does not mean that the executive can stop understanding technology. On the contrary, they need to have enough repertoire to ask difficult questions, assess risks, and understand the consequences of decisions. The difference is that their responsibility becomes less related to directly choosing a technology and more to defining how technology should contribute to the organization’s objectives.

There is, therefore, a gradual change in the center of knowledge. At first, we need to know how to do. Then, we need to know how to guide how to do it. Next, we need to know how to decide what to do, why to do it, and where to invest. The greater the scope, the less likely it is that we will be able to personally execute what we are responsible for. Our competence becomes increasingly related to the quality of the decisions we make, the ability to allocate resources, and the organization we are able to build around ourselves.

This is precisely why studying for the next role does not simply mean accumulating more technical knowledge. It means deliberately expanding our repertoire to keep pace with the type of problem we want to take on.

## Hard Skills and Soft Skills

I do not particularly like the rigid separation between hard skills and soft skills when we talk about professional development in technology. It is useful for organizing competencies, but it can create the false impression that, as the professional advances in their career, technical knowledge becomes less important and needs to be replaced by communication, leadership, and relationship skills. I do not believe that is what happens. What changes is the composition of the set of competencies required to perform each role well.

A technology director, for example, does not necessarily need to know more about a particular technology than a senior engineer. It would even be strange to expect that, considering the breadth of responsibilities of each role. What is expected of the director is something else: to be able to understand the relevance of that technology to the business, assess its risks, challenge assumptions, understand the costs involved, and decide how much the organization should invest in it. Technical knowledge remains necessary, but it begins to be used primarily as an instrument for making better decisions.

The same logic appears in other roles. An architect may have exceptional technical knowledge and still have difficulty performing their role well if they cannot communicate their decisions, negotiate constraints, and build consensus among different groups. A project manager may master planning and governance methodologies, but will have difficulty leading a project if they cannot understand the technical risks involved, talk to specialists, and translate those risks for different stakeholders. The competencies do not compete with one another; they complement one another.

As the scope of the role increases, therefore, there is no replacement of hard skills by soft skills. What happens is an expansion of the set of competencies. The professional continues to need to understand technology, but also needs to understand people, processes, finance, strategy, risks, and business. And, above all, needs to learn how to connect these dimensions in order to make better decisions.

Perhaps this is one of the most important differences between being good at what we do and being prepared for greater responsibility. At a certain point in a career, technical knowledge ceases to be sufficient not because it becomes less important, but because the problem we need to solve begins to have more dimensions than technology alone can explain.

## The Journey Each Person Chooses

Perhaps one of the limitations of the idea of a career as a ladder is imagining that everyone needs to reach the same role. Some people want to build systems, others prefer to solve architectural problems, develop people, manage large operations, or work on organizational strategy. There are professionals who want to reach a director position or an executive position, and there are professionals who prefer to remain deeply technical, continuously expanding their knowledge and influence without necessarily taking on a management position. These are different trajectories, and not necessarily an evolution from one to another.

A specialist career is not an incomplete career, just as a management career is not necessarily an evolution from a technical career. They are paths that require different competencies and put the professional in front of different problems. What matters is understanding what kind of problem we want to solve, what level of responsibility we want to assume, and what competencies we need to develop to do it well.

From this perspective, the next role ceases to be simply a promotion and becomes a choice. And choosing a role also means choosing a set of problems, responsibilities, and competencies that we will need to develop. If we want to take on an architecture position, we need to study architecture. If we want to lead people, we need to study management. If we want to reach a director position, we need to understand strategy, finance, governance, and how the organization operates. We do not need to wait for the change to happen before starting this preparation.

This is why a career in technology can be understood more as a journey than as a ladder. With each change of role, the questions we need to ask, the horizon of our decisions, the people with whom we need to interact, and the nature of the results for which we are responsible all change. And, above all, what we need to learn changes.

Perhaps a more useful way to think about a career is to stop asking only what the next position is and start asking what the next problem is that we want to be prepared to solve. The answer to this question can indicate not only which role makes sense for us, but also what we need to study today so that we are prepared to occupy it tomorrow.
