# When the Metric Becomes the Goal

_Why social networks rot and what this reveals about our obsession with optimizing everything_

**Summary:** I argue that the problem is not with metrics themselves, but with when we confuse the indicator with what it is supposed to represent. By turning a measure into a goal, we create incentives that change behaviors, alter culture, and can make the system continuously improve its numbers while getting worse at what actually matters. Social networks simply make this process more visible. That is why, before asking how to optimize something, we need to decide whether it deserves to be maximized: optimization is a tool, but value is a choice.

---

A few days ago, I was talking with a colleague about a social network that, in my opinion, was becoming a sewer. The complaint was not exactly new. The content seemed worse, the discussions more artificial, the algorithm increasingly insistent on showing things no one had asked to see. The feeling was that this network, which at some point had been interesting, was slowly becoming unbearable. The conversation could have ended like so many other complaints about the internet, had we not started remembering other social networks we had used over the years. One after another, we found the same story. Some disappeared, others remain huge, some are still good in certain respects, but almost all seem to have crossed some inflection point at which what originally made them interesting began to disappear.

This led us to a fairly simple question: is this inevitable? The easiest explanation would be to attribute the phenomenon to growth. Small communities are naturally different from communities with hundreds of millions of people, and scale can destroy the intimacy and culture that existed at the beginning. We can also blame the users, who change as the platform grows, or the algorithms, which begin deciding what we see. Another possibility is to place the responsibility directly on the companies, which discover increasingly efficient ways to turn attention into revenue. All of these explanations contain some truth, but none seemed sufficient to explain why the same pattern appears repeatedly in such different products. What began to interest me was the possibility that we were observing not a collection of accidents, but a process.

A small social network does not need to solve a particularly sophisticated problem. If a few hundred or thousand people post things, it is possible to follow a large part of what is happening. The community itself functions as a selection mechanism. You know some people, follow others because you share interests with them, and gradually build a sense of what is worth seeing. Growth completely changes this dynamic. When millions of people post millions of things every day, no one can keep up with everything. The platform needs to choose, for each user, which few pieces of content deserve to occupy that scarce space that is our attention. What seemed to be an interface problem becomes a selection problem at scale. To select at scale, you need to turn an extremely subjective question, “is this interesting?”, into something a machine can observe.

This is where indicators come in. The system cannot directly determine whether a post is interesting, relevant, or good; it needs to infer this from the behavior of those who consume it. A click, a comment, a share, or the time spent on a post become proxies for what actually matters. Human experience is reduced to variables that can be observed, compared, and incorporated into the platform's operation. This is unavoidable to some degree: a machine needs objective criteria to make decisions at scale. The problem begins when what was supposed to merely indicate value starts defining what will be valued.

Imagine a platform discovering that content that provokes outrage receives more comments. If the purpose of the system is to increase engagement, distributing more of that content is a perfectly rational decision. After some time, the creators themselves notice the pattern. They observe which formats receive attention, which headlines work, which opinions are shared the most, and which types of posts make the algorithm work in their favor. The system starts rewarding certain behaviors, and users start producing content adapted to those rewards. No one needs to decide that the platform should become worse. The algorithm changes behavior, behavior feeds the algorithm, and the cycle reinforces itself. At some point, the platform is no longer simply showing what people want to see. It is helping determine what people produce in order to be seen.

This is where Goodhart's Law offers a particularly useful lens. The idea, in its most familiar form, is that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. A metric is a simplified representation of some reality. It is useful precisely because it reduces complexity to something we can observe and compare. But this reduction comes at a cost: everything that did not make it into the metric disappears from the evaluation system. While the indicator is merely an approximation, this imperfection is tolerable. When it becomes the target, participants begin adapting their behavior to maximize the number, and the number gradually stops representing what it was originally supposed to represent.

A school can use grades as an approximation of learning. As long as the grade is one piece of evidence among several, it can be quite useful. When it becomes the main objective, however, teachers may teach to the test, students may study what will be tested, and the institution may reorganize its practices to produce better results. The grade goes up, but that does not mean learning has improved by the same proportion. No one needs to be cheating. Teachers, students, and administrators are simply responding to the incentives they have received, adjusting their behavior to what has come to be valued.

On social networks, the mechanism is similar. Engagement can be a reasonable indication of interest, but when distribution, compensation, and visibility depend on it, a fundamental difference emerges between generating interest and generating engagement. A careful discussion can be interesting without producing much reaction; a superficial provocation can generate hundreds of comments; an analysis can require attention and reflection while content designed to provoke anger produces instant interaction. If the system is unable to distinguish these things directly, it will tend to favor whatever produces the strongest signal.

The most interesting consequence is that the culture itself begins to change. At first, a person posts because they have something they want to share. Then they observe what works. Next, they begin adapting what they post to what works. The question is no longer simply “what do I want to say?” but “what works here?”. The difference seems small, but it changes the nature of the activity. Content ceases to be merely an expression of the user and also becomes a response to the incentives of the system. This helps explain why a network can seem increasingly homogeneous even when it has more creators than ever. Diversity of people does not guarantee diversity of behavior when everyone is subject to the same rules of distribution.

It is tempting to reduce all of this to corporate greed, and it would be naive to ignore the role of economic incentives. A commercial platform needs to grow, increase revenue, improve retention, and justify its value to investors. But that explanation still does not fully capture the mechanism. There is no need to imagine ill-intentioned executives trying to destroy communities. A company can make perfectly rational decisions, one after another, and produce an outcome that no one would have chosen as the ultimate objective. The platform wants more users, users want more attention, creators want more distribution, advertisers want more conversions, and investors want growth. Each decision can make sense in isolation. Together, they can produce a worse platform.

That is what makes the phenomenon more interesting than a simple story about technology or greed. The problem does not require a villain. It is enough for everyone to have good reasons to improve a small part of the system. The platform improves its recommendations. The creator improves their reach. The advertiser improves conversion. The user receives content increasingly precisely tailored to their interests. Each component can show a measurable improvement while the experience as a whole deteriorates. A system can become progressively better at hitting its indicators and, at the same time, progressively worse at what originally justified its existence.

Social networks are merely a particularly visible case of a much broader problem. The same logic appears whenever we use a measure to represent something that is more complex than the measure. Citations can represent academic impact. Hours worked can represent productivity. Audience can represent relevance. Followers can represent influence. Growth can represent success. In all these cases, there is a real relationship between the indicator and what we want to observe. The problem arises when we confuse the two. A metric is a reduction of reality. It needs to leave something out in order to be useful. The danger begins when we forget precisely this limitation and treat the number as if it were reality itself.

There is a particular temptation in the idea of optimization because it offers a seductive promise: if something can be measured, it can be compared; if it can be compared, it can be improved; if it can be improved, it can be optimized. The process seems rational and, in many contexts, it is exactly what we need to do. The problem is that the word “better” conceals an earlier question that rarely receives the same attention: better according to which criterion? A platform can be excellent at holding our attention without being excellent at providing us with a good experience. A school can be excellent at raising grades without being excellent at educating. A company can be excellent at increasing revenue without necessarily creating more value. The system may be working perfectly and still be pursuing the wrong thing.

That is why some of the most important things in life resist the logic of optimization so poorly. A good conversation does not need an ideal duration. A friendship does not need to be productive. A work of art does not need to reach a particular audience. A class does not need to produce a specific grade. A community does not need to grow indefinitely. A book does not need to be consumed quickly. These things can be evaluated, discussed, and improved, but there is a difference between improving something and turning it into a maximization problem. When everything needs to produce a number, we risk abandoning precisely what the number cannot represent.

That is what made me return to the initial conversation. The question was no longer just why social networks become bad, but why so many things we try to improve end up acquiring characteristics that make their experience worse. Social networks are a particularly clear example because we can observe the process almost in real time. A community is born because people like being there. It grows. Growth requires selection mechanisms. The mechanisms need indicators. The indicators become incentives. The incentives change behavior. Behavior changes culture. And then, a few years later, we look at what remains and wonder why it got worse.

The answer may be uncomfortably simple: we tried to make it better.

I do not think there is a definitive solution hidden at the end of this story. We do not need to abandon metrics, algorithms, or markets, just as we do not need to believe that all growth necessarily destroys what creates it. The question is more basic. Before asking how to maximize something, we need to ask whether it deserves to be maximized. A metric can help us understand a reality, coordinate people, and make better decisions. What it cannot do, on its own, is decide what that reality should be.

This distinction may seem small, but it is one of the most important for understanding the world we are building. Optimization is a tool. Value is a choice. Confusing the two means giving the tool the authority to define the objective.

Social networks may have been merely the place where we noticed this first. The real problem begins when we believe that everything that can be measured should be improved, that everything that can be improved should be optimized, and that everything that can be optimized will necessarily become better.

Some things are valuable precisely because they cannot be reduced to a number. And some are valuable precisely because they do not need to be maximized.
