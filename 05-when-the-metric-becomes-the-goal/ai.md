Social networks as a case of metric-goal conflation: scale makes attention scarce, requiring machine selection; subjective value (“interesting,” “relevant,” “good”) is operationalized through observable proxies (clicks, comments, shares, dwell time). These proxies are useful approximations but become problematic when distribution, compensation, visibility, or other incentives depend on them. Once participants optimize the metric, behavior adapts to the signal, causing the signal to diverge from the underlying value it represented.

The reinforcing loop: algorithm rewards behavior → users/creators observe rewards → behavior adapts to maximize them → adapted behavior generates stronger metric signals → algorithm further rewards it. Thus a platform can increasingly determine what users produce, not merely what users want to consume. Engagement may indicate interest, but optimizing engagement can favor outrage, provocation, superficiality, and instant reactions over careful discussion, reflection, or intrinsically interesting content. No malicious actor is required; locally rational decisions can collectively produce an undesirable system-level outcome.

Goodhart’s Law captures the mechanism: a measure simplifies a more complex reality; omitted dimensions disappear from evaluation. While used as an indicator among other evidence, the approximation can remain useful. Once targeted, participants adapt to maximize it, reducing its validity as a representation of the original objective. School grades can approximate learning, but optimizing grades can produce teaching-to-the-test and studying-to-the-test without proportional improvement in learning. Analogously, optimizing social-media engagement creates a distinction between generating genuine interest and generating measurable interaction.

Optimization also changes culture. Users initially create because they want to share something; after observing what receives attention, they increasingly ask “what works here?” rather than only “what do I want to say?” Content becomes partly an adaptation to system incentives. Consequently, more creators do not necessarily produce more behavioral diversity when everyone faces identical distribution rules; diversity of participants can coexist with increasing homogeneity of behavior.

Economic incentives matter but are insufficient as a complete explanation. Platforms seek growth, revenue, retention, and investor value; creators seek reach; advertisers seek conversions; users seek relevant content. Each actor can rationally optimize its local objective, and each component can show measurable improvement while the overall experience deteriorates. A system can therefore become increasingly effective at optimizing indicators while becoming increasingly ineffective at the underlying purpose that justified those indicators.

The general problem extends beyond social networks: citations as proxies for academic impact, hours worked as proxies for productivity, audience as proxy for relevance, followers as proxy for influence, and growth as proxy for success. In each case, the indicator has a genuine relationship with the target but is not identical to it. Metrics necessarily reduce reality by omitting dimensions; treating the metric as reality erases those dimensions from consideration.

Optimization is therefore conditional on an antecedent value choice: “better” requires a criterion specifying what counts as better. A platform can optimize attention without optimizing user experience; a school can optimize grades without optimizing education; a company can optimize revenue without necessarily optimizing value creation. A system may function efficiently while pursuing an inadequate objective.

Not every valuable activity should become a maximization problem. Conversation does not require an optimal duration; friendship need not be productive; art need not maximize audience; classes need not maximize grades; communities need not grow indefinitely; books need not be consumed quickly. Such things can be evaluated and improved, but forcing them into quantitative maximization risks sacrificing dimensions that metrics cannot represent.

The broader causal pattern is: community forms around intrinsic value → growth makes comprehensive human selection impossible → scalable selection requires indicators → indicators become incentives → incentives alter behavior → altered behavior changes culture → the resulting system may undermine the original value. The solution is not abandoning metrics, algorithms, markets, or growth categorically, but distinguishing measurement from objective-setting. Metrics can inform understanding, coordination, and decisions; they cannot independently determine what should be valued. Optimization is a tool; value is a choice. Confusing them delegates objective-setting authority to the measurement system.
