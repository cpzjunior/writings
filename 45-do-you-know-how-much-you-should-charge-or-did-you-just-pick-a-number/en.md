# Do You Know How Much You Should Charge, or Did You Just Pick a Number?

_Pricing means deciding how much value to capture today, knowing that the market may change tomorrow._

**Summary:** Price should not be treated as a number chosen for convenience, but as a hypothesis about how much value the market recognizes and how that affects demand, margin, and positioning. Since these answers are not available in advance, the company needs to observe actual customer behavior and turn each decision into learning. The initial price may be wrong, as long as there is a rationale behind it and a willingness to revise it when conditions change. The problem is not getting the price wrong, but not knowing why it was chosen.

---

In “[Good Ideas Break Too](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/boas-ideias-tambem-quebram)”, I wrote about how a company can create value and still fail to turn it into a sustainable business. One of the most important boundaries lies precisely in the ability to capture part of that value. After all, it is not enough to solve a relevant problem. There needs to be an economically viable way to turn the solution into revenue, margin, and eventually cash. And that raises a central question: how much of that value should the company capture?

This decision appears long before the company has all the answers. It can arise while a product is still being designed, when a new feature needs to find its place in the market, or when a business needs to decide whether a given opportunity is worth pursuing. In any of these cases, someone needs to turn a perception of value into a number. And that number begins to influence almost everything that comes afterward: who buys, how much they buy, the operating margin, the pace of growth, and even the perception the market builds around the product. Even so, it is common to treat price as a secondary variable, defined after the decisions considered more important have already been made.

But price does not merely follow those decisions. It also helps define them. By choosing a price, the company is making a bet about how the market will respond and how much value it will be able to capture. And when a solution is still being built or launched, much of the information needed for that bet simply does not exist.

The problem is not starting with a guess. It is choosing a number without knowing why it was chosen and, above all, without building a way to find out whether it was wrong.

## Price Is Not Defined in Isolation

There is a fairly intuitive tension in pricing. The customer wants to pay as little as possible, and the company wants to charge as much as possible. But this dispute hides a fundamental relationship: price and volume are not independent.

Charging more increases the margin per unit, but tends to reduce the quantity sold. Charging less can increase demand, but reduces the contribution from each sale. This relationship had already been explored by Antoine Augustin Cournot in the nineteenth century, when he mathematically analyzed how price and quantity determine total revenue. Later, Alfred Marshall developed the concept of price elasticity of demand, which helps explain this relationship: depending on demand sensitivity, a price change can increase or reduce revenue.

In a simplified scenario, in which the quantity demanded falls linearly as price increases, total revenue takes the shape of a parabola. At first, increasing the price increases revenue. Beyond a certain point, however, the loss in volume outweighs the gain per unit, and revenue begins to fall. The point of maximum revenue occurs when the price elasticity of demand is unitary.

The representation is simplified, but it reveals something important: a price cannot be evaluated in isolation. The same price can produce very different results depending on how customers respond to it. That response can also vary by segment, purchasing context, competition, perceived value, and the maturity of the offering.

Furthermore, maximizing revenue does not mean maximizing profit. Variable costs, fixed costs, taxes, operating structure, working capital, and cost of capital change the equation. Volume also affects costs. Selling less can mean losing quantity discounts, paying suppliers more, or spreading fixed costs across a smaller sales base. Thus, a price can increase revenue and still reduce the economic result.

The company may also be pursuing different objectives, such as generating cash, gaining market share, accelerating adoption, or financing a particular strategy. Price, therefore, needs to be evaluated by the effect it produces on the economics of the business, not merely by the revenue from each sale.

This also explains why average ticket size, in isolation, says little about the quality of the business. Selling to one hundred customers at R$ 100 generates R$ 10,000 in revenue. Selling to fifty customers at R$ 150 generates R$ 7,500. The second scenario has a higher ticket, but is not necessarily better. The result depends on the combination of price, volume, mix, margin, and cost structure.

Ultimately, price, volume, and margin need to be analyzed together. The price that appears most attractive per sale may not be the one that produces the best result for the business.

## Margin Is Also a Strategic Choice

A higher margin provides more room to absorb fluctuations, finance investments, and get through difficult periods without immediately resorting to external capital. In this sense, margin also functions as a buffer.

Retail helps illustrate what happens when that buffer is small. Operations with tight margins depend heavily on volume, turnover, and operational efficiency. While demand behaves as expected, the structure may work well. But a decline in sales, an increase in costs, a currency shift, stagnant inventory, or a greater need for working capital can quickly consume the result. When the margin is small, there is little distance between a healthy operation and one that begins to destroy cash.

In a leveraged operation, this effect can be even faster. Lower margin means lower cash generation, which can increase the need for financing. Financing brings financial expenses, which further reduce the result and increase dependence on future cash. A relatively small operational problem can, under certain structures, turn into a financial problem.

A B2B SaaS that finds an underexplored market helps illustrate the other extreme. A specific product, with little direct competition and relatively low marginal cost, can achieve very high margins. This can be an important advantage, but it can also signal that there is an opportunity that is still relatively uncontested.

A high margin, therefore, can be both a consequence and a source of competitive advantage. The company can use that result to invest in technology, brand, distribution, scale, integration with other systems, or customer relationships and thereby strengthen its position. But if the advantage is not difficult to reproduce, high margins can also attract competitors and increase pressure for lower prices.

Therefore, margin is not merely the result of a pricing policy. It determines how much risk the company can absorb, how much cash it can generate, and how many resources it will have available to strengthen its position. Price influences margin, and margin influences the company's ability to continue operating and investing when the market stops cooperating.

## Price Also Determines the Target Audience

There is another consequence of pricing that tends to receive less attention: price does not merely determine how much each customer pays. It also influences who your customer will be.

A product priced at R$ 10, R$ 100, or R$ 1,000 may essentially solve the same problem, but it will probably be embedded in different markets. The audience, purchasing process, expectations of service, competitors, and even the value proposition needed to justify the purchase all change.

Ferrari is an extreme example of this logic. Its price does not merely serve to capture more value per unit. It also participates in building the position the brand occupies in the market. Exclusivity is part of the perceived value, and the high price itself helps reinforce it. In this case, price is not merely a consequence of positioning. It is also one of the instruments used to construct it.

Therefore, reducing the price does not simply mean increasing the market. It can mean changing the market. A company that aggressively lowers its prices can reach new customers, but it can also alter the perception of the product, attract a different audience, and create expectations that are difficult to reverse later.

Likewise, increasing the price does not necessarily mean losing customers proportionally. It can alter perceived value, select a different audience, and reposition the product.

Price and value, therefore, do not have a one-way relationship. The company tries to capture a portion of the value perceived by the customer, but the price itself also influences that perception.

This is one reason why the question “how much would you pay?” has limited usefulness. What someone states in a conversation does not necessarily correspond to their behavior when faced with a real offer. There is a difference between considering something valuable and actually paying for it.

In the end, the transaction reveals how much of that value the market is willing to recognize.

## Marketing as a Learning Instrument

If price is a hypothesis, marketing can be one of the instruments used to test it. A campaign puts an offer in front of potential customers and makes it possible to observe how different audiences react when we change price, package, commercial terms, discounts, features, or the way the product is presented.

These variations can produce important signals. If a price reduction increases conversion, there is evidence of price sensitivity. If a change in the package improves the result without changing the price, perhaps the problem was more about perceived value than the amount charged. If one segment converts much more than another when presented with the same offer, there may be a meaningful difference in willingness to pay or in the importance of the problem.

But testing is not simply about measuring. If price, audience, channel, message, and offer all change at the same time, we may observe a difference in results without knowing what caused it. The more variables are changed simultaneously, the harder it becomes to distinguish correlation from causation. Therefore, even without a statistically perfect experiment, it is important to know which hypothesis is being investigated and what the result actually allows us to conclude.

Imagine a company with a product sold for R$ 100 that suspects it could charge more. Instead of increasing the price for everyone, it could test a new offer with a specific segment. If conversion falls, this does not necessarily mean the price was wrong. It may indicate that the willingness to pay of that segment was exceeded, that the value proposition was unclear, or that the increase was not accompanied by a proportional perception of benefit.

The point is that the market does not merely need to respond to the company's decisions. The decisions can also be designed to produce information. A promotion can reveal price sensitivity, a new package can show that the problem was in the structure of the offer, and a price change can help understand how different segments respond.

The goal is not to find the perfect price quickly. It is to make each pricing decision slightly less uncertain than the previous one.

## Today's Price Does Not Have to Be Tomorrow's Price

If price is a hypothesis, it does not need to be permanent either. The business changes, the market changes, and with them the conditions that support a pricing decision change as well. Costs, competitors, product maturity, perceived value, scale, and customer profile can change over time. The company itself also learns and begins making decisions with information it did not have before.

Inflation, for example, is a natural justification for price increases. Even if the product, customer, and value proposition remain the same, the loss of purchasing power of the currency and rising costs can reduce the company's real revenue and margin. In this case, adjusting the price does not necessarily mean changing the positioning or trying to capture more value, but preserving the economics of the operation.

This does not mean changing prices constantly. Price changes also have costs: they can affect trust, contracts, customer relationships, and predictability. In some markets, price stability is part of the value proposition itself.

Maintaining a price, therefore, is also a decision. It should not remain unchanged simply because it was chosen in the past, but because it still makes sense given the current conditions of the business and the market.

A change in costs can make a pricing structure unviable. A new competitor can alter the dynamics of the market. An increase in perceived value can create room to capture a larger share of the value created. A new customer segment may require a different pricing structure.

Today's price does not have to be tomorrow's price. What matters is that the change, just like maintaining the existing price, is a conscious decision about the conditions that support it.

## Capturing Value Without Destroying the Business

When a company launches something new, the data needed to define the price does not yet exist. The initial price is therefore a decision based on costs, market references, previous experiences, conversations with customers, and an assessment of the value delivered. The problem is not starting with uncertainty, but treating that first choice as truth.

A price that is too low can generate sales while simultaneously leaving value on the table. A price that is too high can make an opportunity appear nonexistent. Therefore, price needs to be analyzed through the combination of demand, margin, positioning, and the ability to sustain the business. When these conditions change, the decision may need to change as well.

In the end, pricing means deciding how much value to capture without compromising the ability to continue capturing value. There is no universally correct price. There is a decision that is more or less well founded for the conditions of that moment.

The question, therefore, is not only how much to charge, but why to choose that number and what it allows us to learn about the market.

The problem is not guessing the price. It is not knowing why you guessed that number.
