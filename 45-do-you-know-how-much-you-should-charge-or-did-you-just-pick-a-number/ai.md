Price should be understood as a hypothesis about how much value the market will recognize and how that recognition will affect demand, margin, positioning, and the sustainability of the business. A company needs an economically viable mechanism for converting created value into revenue, margin, and cash, and pricing decisions emerge before all relevant information is available. Because price influences who buys, how much they buy, operating economics, growth, and market perception, it is not merely a downstream variable but a strategic bet whose validity must be tested against actual customer behavior. The initial price may be wrong; what matters is having a rationale for it and a mechanism for learning whether it needs to change.

Price cannot be evaluated independently because price and volume interact: higher prices generally increase margin per unit while potentially reducing quantity demanded, whereas lower prices may increase demand while reducing contribution per sale. Cournot's analysis of the relationship between price and quantity and Marshall's concept of price elasticity formalize this relationship, and in a simplified linear-demand model, revenue rises with price until a point of unitary elasticity and then falls. The simplification does not capture all market conditions, since responsiveness varies by segment, context, competition, perceived value, and product maturity, but it demonstrates that the same price can produce very different outcomes. Revenue maximization is also distinct from profit maximization because variable and fixed costs, taxes, working capital, cost of capital, and volume-related efficiencies affect the economic result. A business may instead prioritize cash generation, market-share acquisition, adoption, or another strategic objective, so price must ultimately be considered together with volume, mix, margin, and cost structure rather than through ticket size alone.

Margin is not merely an accounting consequence of pricing but also determines how much operational and financial risk the company can absorb and how much cash it can generate for investment. Low-margin businesses, such as many retail operations, depend heavily on volume, turnover, and efficiency and can see small changes in demand, costs, inventory, currency, or working-capital requirements rapidly erode their economics; leverage can amplify this effect by converting weaker cash generation into greater financing needs and financial expenses. Conversely, a B2B SaaS product serving an underexplored market may achieve high margins because of low marginal costs and limited competition, giving it resources to invest in technology, distribution, brand, scale, integration, or customer relationships. High margins can therefore reinforce competitive advantage, but they can also attract competitors when the advantage is easy to reproduce. Pricing influences margin, while margin determines the resources and resilience available to sustain and strengthen the business.

Price also influences the composition of the customer base and the market in which a product competes. Products solving essentially the same problem at R$10, R$100, or R$1,000 can attract different audiences, purchasing processes, service expectations, competitors, and value propositions. Ferrari illustrates the extreme case in which high price participates in constructing exclusivity and market positioning rather than merely extracting more value per unit. Consequently, lowering price does not simply expand the existing market: it can change the audience, perception, and expectations surrounding the product, while raising price can likewise alter perceived value and select a different customer segment. Price and perceived value therefore influence each other rather than operating in a one-way relationship. This is also why asking potential customers how much they would pay has limited predictive value: stated willingness does not necessarily correspond to behavior when confronted with a real transaction, and the actual purchase reveals more directly how much value the market is prepared to recognize.

If price is a hypothesis, marketing can be used as a learning mechanism for testing it. Campaigns and offers can expose different audiences to changes in price, packaging, commercial terms, discounts, features, or presentation, producing signals about price sensitivity, perceived value, and differences in willingness to pay across segments. However, changing multiple variables simultaneously makes causal interpretation difficult because an observed result cannot easily be attributed to a single factor; therefore, each test should have a defined hypothesis and a clear understanding of what its result can and cannot establish. For example, a price increase that lowers conversion in one segment could indicate excessive price sensitivity, insufficiently communicated value, or an increase in price without a corresponding increase in perceived benefit. The purpose of experimentation is consequently not to discover a perfect price immediately, but to make each pricing decision somewhat less uncertain than the previous one.

A pricing decision is not necessarily permanent because business and market conditions evolve. Costs, inflation, competitors, product maturity, perceived value, scale, and customer composition can all change, while the company itself accumulates information unavailable when the original price was established. Inflation and cost increases can justify adjustments simply to preserve real revenue and margin, without implying a change in positioning or an attempt to capture additional value. At the same time, frequent price changes can impose costs through effects on trust, contracts, customer relationships, and predictability, and in some markets stability is itself part of the value proposition. Maintaining an existing price is therefore also an active decision: it should persist because its underlying conditions remain valid, not merely because it was chosen previously. Changes in costs, competition, perceived value, or customer segments can make either a new price or a new pricing structure appropriate.

When launching something new, the information required to determine the price does not yet fully exist, so the initial decision necessarily combines costs, market references, prior experience, customer conversations, and an assessment of delivered value. The central problem is not uncertainty itself but treating the first estimate as established truth. A price that is too low may generate demand while leaving substantial value uncaptured, whereas a price that is too high may suppress demand and make a viable opportunity appear nonexistent. Pricing therefore requires simultaneous consideration of demand, margin, positioning, and business sustainability, with the possibility of revising the decision as those conditions change. There is no universally correct price; there is a price decision that is more or less justified by the conditions and information available at a particular moment. The fundamental question is not merely how much to charge, but why that number was chosen and what the resulting market behavior can teach the company.
