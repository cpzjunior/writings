The introduction frames software architecture for early-stage startups as a balance between simplicity, uncertain demand, limited resources, and the long-term cost of early decisions. Drawing on experience maintaining the Se Doar NGO platform with limited people and infrastructure, it describes how a modular monolith initially provided appropriate simplicity but eventually exposed different processing profiles across features: some workloads were frequent, others sporadic, and some could run on demand. This led to questioning why the development unit must also be the deployment and scaling unit. The proposed exploration sits between modular monoliths, which preserve operational simplicity through in-process boundaries, and microservices, which provide independent deployment and scaling at the cost of distributed-systems complexity. The central question is whether modules can remain the units of development while individual use cases become the units of execution and scaling when concrete needs arise.

The section explains that microservices primarily address organizational and operational scaling by giving components independent lifecycles, infrastructure, and scaling strategies, but this independence introduces network communication, timeouts, retries, distributed transactions, observability, and other distributed-systems concerns. These trade-offs can be valuable for larger organizations but may impose disproportionate costs on early-stage startups with small teams. Modular monoliths address a different problem: maintaining clear boundaries between responsibilities while keeping interactions in one process, making evolving domain boundaries easier to reorganize without immediately turning every boundary into a deployment decision. Neither architecture is necessarily a mandatory stage of the other; they prioritize different properties. The proposed gap arises when an application benefits from modular development but contains workloads with substantially different processing requirements, creating the question of whether the development unit can remain the module while the processing and scaling unit becomes the individual use case.

The proposed distributed modular architecture separates development granularity from processing granularity. A module continues to contain related use cases, business rules, persistence, dependencies, and tests, but individual use cases can adopt different execution strategies: for example, `CreateOrder` could run as a function, `CancelOrder` inside a conventional application, `GetOrder` through another processing strategy, and `GenerateReport` as an asynchronous job. The module remains the unit of development and evolution, while a use case can become an independent execution and scaling unit only when justified. The approach does not require every use case to become isolated or every module to become a service; functions, applications, containers, workers, queues, or other mechanisms can be selected according to workload characteristics. Infrastructure can be defined alongside modules, while genuinely shared capabilities can belong to a common core, capabilities shared by some modules can form their own modules, and capabilities specific to one context can remain there, avoiding an indiscriminate `Core`. Synchronous dependencies can remain in-process even when the calling use case is independently executed, while asynchronous flows can use queues or streams. Consequently, distribution is selective: the architecture can begin as a single application and later distribute specific use cases without turning entire modules into microservices.

The proposed starting strategy is to use functions for synchronous use cases because they can provide on-demand execution, independent scaling, and limited idle capacity, particularly useful when an early-stage startup has low, irregular, or unpredictable demand. This is not presented as a claim that functions are inherently superior or universally cheaper, but as an initial strategy that can be changed when workload data demonstrates a better alternative. A high-volume operation such as `GetOrder` could later move to a provisioned container or process, while an infrequent operation such as `GenerateReport` could remain a function, with the change occurring at the use-case level rather than requiring the whole module to be reorganized. Asynchronous workloads may instead use workers or other appropriate mechanisms. Cold starts and latency requirements are explicit reasons a function may become unsuitable. The underlying architectural property is therefore not commitment to serverless execution but the ability to change execution strategy without changing the software's organizational structure.

The main proposed advantage is the separation of development and processing granularity: a cohesive module can contain code, tests, business rules, and persistence while individual use cases adopt different execution strategies according to their actual workloads. This can allow infrastructure to follow utilization more closely and avoid forcing the requirements of one workload onto unrelated workloads, although it does not guarantee lower costs because predictable workloads may be cheaper on provisioned infrastructure. The architecture also supports selective evolution from a modular monolith toward greater distribution without requiring a predetermined migration to microservices, which may be useful when workloads such as AI processing and conventional CRUD operations have very different characteristics. However, distribution introduces the usual costs of distributed systems—latency, timeouts, retries, observability, idempotency, and partial failures—and the proposal limits these costs rather than eliminating them by applying them only where justified. In-process library dependencies retain simplicity but reduce deployment independence, making dependency direction and avoidance of cycles important. Independently executing use cases can also duplicate runtimes and dependencies, increasing memory, startup time, or processing costs, while shared resources such as PostgreSQL, queues, caches, and external services can remain bottlenecks; scaling functions cannot overcome a saturated dependency and may worsen it. Operational complexity also increases through additional deployments, configurations, permissions, monitoring, and infrastructure resources, so the intended trade-off is not absolute simplicity but introducing additional complexity only where a concrete benefit justifies it.

The architecture is distinguished from microservices because its primary unit is deliberately different: a module remains the unit of development and evolution, while deployment, ownership, operation, and scaling decisions are not necessarily concentrated at that same boundary. A module may remain entirely inside a conventional application, another may have one use case executed as a function, and another may use asynchronous processing for a specific operation. Distribution is therefore an available mechanism rather than the architectural objective. The proposal is not presented as a universal replacement for either microservices or modular monoliths. It is potentially relevant to early-stage startups with small teams, limited budgets, initially low or variable demand, and workloads likely to grow unevenly, while systems with predictable loads or specialized security, availability, governance, auditing, isolation, or consistency requirements may call for different approaches. Shared-resource bottlenecks remain a fundamental constraint, so the architecture should be understood as a hypothesis for a particular context rather than a general solution.

The section on validation emphasizes that the architecture has not been empirically validated through a long-term real-startup implementation, so there is no evidence yet that it necessarily reduces costs, simplifies evolution, or improves developer experience. The underlying hypothesis is that separating development from processing should produce real benefits without creating more complexity than it avoids. Evidence in favor would include keeping modules simple even when individual use cases use different execution strategies, allowing the system to begin mostly within one process and distribute only concrete workloads, preserving direct library dependencies where appropriate, and avoiding situations where distributing one use case forces many dependencies to become services. Economic evaluation would need to include not only compute costs but also observability, deployments, infrastructure maintenance, and engineering time. Long-term evolution would also matter: if growth eventually requires extensive rewrites, systematic replacement of dependencies with APIs, or an unavoidable migration to microservices, the architecture may have merely postponed complexity; if distribution can continue to be introduced selectively, that would provide stronger evidence for the approach. Discovering that a traditional modular monolith is equally effective would also constitute a valid experimental result.

The conclusion presents the distributed modular architecture as an experiment rather than a settled architectural recommendation. Operational complexity may emerge too early, organizational growth may eventually make microservices more appropriate, and many applications may simply remain better served by a modular monolith. The proposal is intentionally scoped to early-stage startups with small teams, limited resources, and uneven workloads, and it does not seek to eliminate microservices when properties such as isolation, ownership, or independent deployment become more valuable. Its narrower proposition is to begin with a simple modular architecture while preserving the ability to distribute only the workloads that actually require it, thereby deferring infrastructure and operational decisions until concrete needs emerge. The unresolved question is whether this separation can preserve the simplicity of modular development while allowing processing capacity to be provisioned at the granularity of the use cases that actually need independent treatment.
