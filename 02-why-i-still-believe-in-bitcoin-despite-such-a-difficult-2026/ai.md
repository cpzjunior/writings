The introduction acknowledges that Bitcoin’s recent performance fell short of the author’s expectations, particularly regarding the speed of institutional adoption and its transition from speculative asset to established asset class. However, the author distinguishes disappointment with investment performance from abandonment of the underlying investment thesis, recognizing that the mistaken element may have been timing rather than direction. The author does not claim to predict Bitcoin’s price or persuade others to invest, but instead wants to assess what role Bitcoin could occupy in the global financial system if adoption, institutionalization, and scarcity continue to develop.

The Law of Supply and Demand section argues that Bitcoin’s core structural characteristic is its fixed maximum supply of 21 million units, which cannot expand in response to economic growth, government deficits, or increased capital circulation. Scarcity alone does not create value, but a rigidly limited supply combined with potentially increasing demand from individuals, companies, funds, and institutions creates a supply-demand dynamic in which increased demand must be reflected through price if supply remains constrained. The author rejects the simplistic claim that monetary creation automatically produces proportional Bitcoin appreciation, acknowledging the roles of interest rates, credit, productivity, money velocity, fiscal policy, and expectations. Instead, the relevant asymmetry is between fiat currencies, whose supply can be expanded through political and economic decisions, and Bitcoin, whose monetary policy and maximum supply are predictable. The thesis does not require Bitcoin to replace dollars, gold, equities, or fixed income; rather, Bitcoin could benefit if it captures an increasing share of the growing global pool of capital seeking diversification, wealth preservation, or alternative stores of value.

The From Risk Asset to Store of Value section argues that Bitcoin remains predominantly a volatile risk asset, but that institutional adoption and a larger, deeper, and more liquid market could gradually reduce its volatility and contribute to its maturation. The author compares Bitcoin with gold because both can function as stores of value, while noting that Bitcoin has characteristics such as a known maximum supply, network-verifiable ownership and transfers, and the possibility of self-custody. The author does not intend to abandon traditional financial institutions but sees Bitcoin as a scarce asset that could preserve part of wealth as the monetary base and financial system expand. Bitcoin’s cryptographic verification also provides a digital alternative to the physical verification of gold, although custody and security risks remain. If institutionalization continues, the author believes Bitcoin could increasingly be treated as a digital store of value that coexists with gold rather than necessarily replacing it.

The Institutionalization Is More Important Than Appreciation section argues that institutional development is a more informative signal than short-term price performance for evaluating a long-term Bitcoin thesis. Price can fluctuate or remain stagnant without necessarily invalidating a decades-long thesis, whereas the creation of infrastructure for institutions, asset managers, companies, and governments to buy, custody, trade, finance, and regulate Bitcoin indicates increasing integration with traditional finance. The author identifies regulatory clarity as particularly important, citing the potential passage of the U.S. CLARITY Act and similar frameworks elsewhere as mechanisms that could reduce legal uncertainty and facilitate institutional product development, access, and liquidity, while acknowledging that U.S. legislation would not automatically produce global adoption. The section then shifts from asking how much Bitcoin will be worth to asking what can be done with it, particularly through collateralized lending. If Bitcoin can be pledged as collateral rather than sold to obtain liquidity, it could become part of corporate balance sheets, financing structures, and credit operations, although volatility would require mechanisms such as appropriate loan-to-value ratios, margin calls, and liquidation procedures. The author views the development of these functions by 2030 as important evidence for or against the thesis.

The Why Bitcoin and Not Another Cryptocurrency? section clarifies that the thesis concerns Bitcoin specifically rather than cryptocurrencies generally. The author acknowledges that other crypto projects may possess interesting technologies and use cases but argues that technical superiority does not guarantee that a token will retain users, developers, liquidity, or relevance, particularly when projects depend heavily on companies, foundations, teams, or technologies that could later be displaced. Bitcoin is preferred because its principal differentiating characteristic is perceived to be resilience rather than technological novelty: it has survived multiple cycles of speculation and collapse, attacks, forks, regulatory changes, confidence crises, and repeated declarations of its demise. This history does not guarantee permanence, but it provides a track record that newer cryptocurrencies have not had sufficient time to establish. The author therefore prefers an asset whose proposition is relatively simple and whose relevance has persisted across different cycles: a digital, decentralized monetary network with limited supply. This simplicity makes the long-term thesis easier to monitor because it depends less on predicting which technological platform will dominate the future.

The Pyramid and Bubble Criticism section addresses the argument that Bitcoin is a pyramid scheme or equivalent to Tulip Mania. The author distinguishes Bitcoin from a pyramid scheme because Bitcoin has no central operator promising returns through recruitment or distributing new participants’ money to earlier participants. Speculative behavior exists, but the author notes that speculation also occurs in equities, real estate, commodities, and other traded assets. The author accepts that Bitcoin can experience bubbles and that such bubbles may recur, while maintaining that the existence of speculative excess does not establish that an asset has no underlying economic value. Bitcoin has a functioning network, a defined monetary policy, a verifiable maximum supply, global liquidity, and increasingly developed financial infrastructure. The author therefore considers the more relevant question to be what economic function Bitcoin could perform as its infrastructure matures and how far institutional adoption could extend, rather than whether speculative episodes have occurred.

The Artificial Intelligence section introduces AI as a potentially important variable for Bitcoin’s long-term environment. The author expects the technological competition between the United States and China to require enormous investment in datacenters, semiconductors, energy infrastructure, computing capacity, and AI systems, potentially moving trillions of dollars and reshaping the organization of capital, labor, and productivity. The author speculates that increasingly autonomous AI agents could eventually participate directly in economic transactions, negotiate services, move capital, and interact with financial systems, creating possible demand for forms of value transfer that are global, verifiable, and independent of any single national financial infrastructure. The author explicitly avoids claiming that Bitcoin will necessarily become the currency of AI or dominate everyday payments, instead considering whether an asset that is decentralized, globally transferable, and supply-constrained could become relevant for large-scale value transfer and settlement across jurisdictions. The section further connects AI investment with the possibility of sustained capital expansion and monetary growth, arguing that an economy requiring enormous amounts of capital could increase demand for scarce assets. The author acknowledges that the AI-Bitcoin connection may prove incorrect and therefore treats it as a hypothesis to evaluate over time rather than a prediction. By 2030, the author expects the thesis to be materially stronger if Bitcoin has evolved from primarily a speculative asset into a store of value, institutional asset, collateral, and persistent component of global financial infrastructure.

The 2030: A Horizon of Events, Not a Price Target section defines 2030 as a period over which the author expects to evaluate developments rather than as a date associated with a specific Bitcoin price. The relevant indicators are continued institutional adoption, development of financial infrastructure, greater regulatory clarity, and clearer recognition of Bitcoin as a store of value; price is regarded as an eventual consequence of these developments rather than the primary metric. The author remains disappointed with Bitcoin’s recent performance but considers that disappointment useful because it reinforces the distinction between asset performance and thesis validity. The ultimate objective is therefore not to determine Bitcoin’s exact value in 2030 but to observe what role it has established within the global financial system. The conclusion maintains that the author does not need to predict the highest or lowest price or persuade others to agree, but instead needs to observe whether real-world developments continue in the direction implied by the thesis, while acknowledging that the thesis could ultimately prove incorrect.

The disclaimer states that the text represents only the author’s personal view of Bitcoin and is not an investment recommendation, suggestion, or advice. It emphasizes that investment decisions should be based on each person’s objectives, risk tolerance, and circumstances, and that investors ultimately bear responsibility for their own financial losses.
