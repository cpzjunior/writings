# Why I Still Believe in Bitcoin Despite Such a Difficult 2026

_There is a fundamental difference between being disappointed with the performance of an investment and losing faith in the thesis that led you to invest in it_

**Summary**: I still believe in Bitcoin not because I expect rapid appreciation, but because its structural thesis remains intact: a supply limited to 21 million units combined with potentially growing demand, in a world of monetary expansion and financial digitization. I see institutionalization, regulation, and the maturation of infrastructure as more important signals than short-term price. Among cryptocurrencies, I choose Bitcoin because of the resilience it has already demonstrated and the simplicity of its proposition. My bet, therefore, is not on tomorrow's price, but on the role that a scarce, decentralized, and global asset can play through 2030.

---

I expected more from Bitcoin. I think it is important to start here because it would be dishonest to write this text pretending that the past few years lived up to my expectations. When I began building my position in Bitcoin, I imagined that institutional adoption would advance more quickly, that the market would understand the importance of a limited money supply sooner, and that by this point we would be closer to treating Bitcoin as an established asset class than as a speculative asset. That is not exactly what happened. The performance disappointed me, the path was more difficult than I imagined, and some of my expectations regarding the speed of adoption were clearly wrong.

Still, there is a fundamental difference between being disappointed with the performance of an investment and losing faith in the thesis that led you to invest. It is like being disappointed in someone who did not do what you expected them to do, when that expectation was exclusively your own. The frustration is legitimate, but it says as much about the person who created the expectation as it does about the person who failed to meet it. In the case of Bitcoin, no one promised me the return I expected, nor the timeframe in which it would happen. The expectation was mine. Perhaps I was more wrong about the timing than about the direction. I still believe in Bitcoin and, perhaps precisely because of that, this is a good time to explain why.

I am not writing this to convince anyone to buy Bitcoin. Nor do I intend to present a price target or pretend that I can predict the behavior of such a volatile asset over the next few years. My relationship with Bitcoin is much simpler and, at the same time, more ambitious: I want to discover what role this asset will play in the global financial system and, from there, understand what its true potential may be. I am not trying to predict tomorrow's price, but to assess how far an asset with these characteristics can go if the thesis of adoption, institutionalization, and scarcity proves correct.

## The law of supply and demand

The simplest part of my thesis lies in the old and familiar law of supply and demand. There are only 21 million bitcoins that can ever be issued, and that quantity does not change because the global economy has grown, because governments have increased their deficits, or because more capital has begun circulating through the financial system. Scarcity alone does not make an asset valuable. An asset can be extremely rare and still fail to attract anyone's interest. What makes Bitcoin interesting is the combination of a rigidly limited supply and the possibility of increasingly greater demand. If more people, companies, funds, and institutions want to own a share of those same 21 million bitcoins, the pressure on price tends to be very different from what we observe in assets whose supply can increase to meet demand.

I also do not believe in the simplification that every instance of monetary creation necessarily produces equivalent inflation or that every additional dollar created should generate a proportional appreciation in Bitcoin. Monetary reality is much more complex and involves interest rates, credit, productivity, money velocity, fiscal policy, and expectations. What interests me is a more structural asymmetry: fiat currencies have a supply that can be expanded according to political and economic decisions, while Bitcoin has a predictable monetary policy and a known maximum supply. This characteristic does not depend on price and was not created to justify past appreciation. It is simply part of the nature of the asset.

And that is precisely why my thesis does not depend on Bitcoin replacing the dollar, gold, stocks, fixed-income securities, or any other asset. I do not need to imagine the end of the traditional financial system to believe in Bitcoin's potential. On the contrary, the more capital exists in the world and the greater the number of investors looking for alternatives for diversification, store of value, or wealth protection, the greater the potential demand for a share of the 21 million existing bitcoins. If that demand grows while supply remains limited, the law of supply and demand itself tends to make the adjustment happen through price. For me, that is where the thesis's asymmetry begins: Bitcoin does not need to conquer the entire financial market. It only needs to capture an increasing share of it.

## From risk asset to store of value

Today, Bitcoin is still predominantly treated as a risk asset, and this is reflected in its volatility. Large price movements over relatively short periods are still common, making it difficult to view it in the same way we view gold. My thesis is that this may change as institutional adoption advances and the market becomes larger, more liquid, and deeper. The greater the amount of capital involved and the more diversified the participants, the greater the market's ability tends to be to absorb large orders without producing such extreme movements. I do not believe Bitcoin needs to stop being volatile to fulfill its function, but I believe that a progressive reduction in this volatility could be precisely one of the signs that the asset is maturing and becoming less predominantly speculative.

This is where the comparison with gold begins to make sense. Gold has thousands of years of history as a store of value, and its role within portfolios is well established. Bitcoin is still building that reputation, but it has some characteristics that may favor this evolution: its maximum supply is known and limited to 21 million units, its existence and movement can be verified by the network itself, and its transfer does not depend on physical transportation. There is also the possibility of self-custody, which I consider an important characteristic, although it is not the reason I intend to abandon the traditional financial system. I have no interest in avoiding banks, brokerages, or other institutions. My intention is much simpler: to own a scarce asset that can preserve part of my wealth over time, including in an environment in which the amount of money in the economy continues to grow.

I also consider the difficulty of counterfeiting relevant. Gold depends on physical processes to verify weight and purity, while Bitcoin has a cryptographic verification layer that makes it possible to confirm the existence and movement of coins on the network. This does not eliminate risks, especially those related to custody and security, but it creates a characteristic that may become increasingly relevant as the financial system digitizes. If institutionalization continues to advance, Bitcoin may gradually stop being seen as a purely speculative bet and start being treated as a digital store of value, coexisting with gold rather than necessarily replacing it.

## Institutionalization is more important than appreciation

That is precisely why, despite my frustration with performance, I pay more attention to institutionalization than to price. Price can fall, rise, or move sideways for long periods without necessarily invalidating a decades-long thesis. The infrastructure being built around Bitcoin, on the other hand, says much more about the asset's future. If financial institutions, asset managers, companies, and governments continue creating mechanisms to buy, custody, trade, finance, and regulate Bitcoin, this means the asset is progressively ceasing to be merely a speculative bet and beginning to occupy a position within traditional financial infrastructure. I do not expect this transformation to be complete now, nor do I believe Bitcoin needs to be treated as a mature asset in 2026. My horizon is 2030, and what I want to observe by then is precisely whether this process will continue advancing.

In this context, regulatory evolution seems especially important to me. The potential approval of the CLARITY Act in the United States and the possibility that other countries adopt similar frameworks could reduce one of the biggest barriers to institutional entry: legal uncertainty. I do not believe that U.S. legislation will automatically produce global adoption of Bitcoin, but I believe that clear regulatory definition in one of the world's largest economies can serve as a precedent for other jurisdictions. Institutions can build products when they know the rules, investors can access those products when infrastructure exists, and as liquidity increases, the asset begins to acquire functions that would be much more difficult to develop in an environment of regulatory uncertainty. If this movement continues over the next few years, I believe we will reach 2030 with a Bitcoin market very different from the one we have today.

From this point onward, the discussion about Bitcoin changes in nature. The question is no longer merely “how much will it be worth?” but rather “what will it be possible to do with it?” One of the examples that interests me most is the possibility of using Bitcoin as collateral. A company that holds a large amount of BTC today can sell part of its position to obtain liquidity. In a more developed financial market, it could use that Bitcoin as collateral to raise capital without necessarily disposing of the asset. This does not mean that credit will automatically be cheap or that the structure will be risk-free, since Bitcoin's volatility requires LTV, or loan-to-value, mechanisms that determine how much an institution can lend relative to the value of the collateral, as well as margin calls and liquidation if the value of BTC falls significantly. But the possibility of incorporating the asset into credit infrastructure significantly changes its economic utility. Bitcoin would cease to be merely an asset purchased in the expectation of appreciation and could become part of balance sheets, financing structures, and credit operations. If this evolution occurs over the next four years, it will be one of the most important pieces of evidence that my thesis was correct.

## Why Bitcoin and not another cryptocurrency?

This question is fundamental because my thesis is not about cryptocurrencies in general. There are thousands of projects, some with interesting technologies and use cases that may be relevant, but interesting technology does not necessarily mean that the associated token will be a good asset. A network can be technically superior and still lose users, developers, liquidity, or relevance. It may depend excessively on a company, a foundation, or a specific team, or it may simply be replaced by a different technology a few years later. In the end, building good technology is not enough: it is necessary to earn trust, liquidity, and adoption and, above all, demonstrate that the network will remain relevant across different cycles.

Bitcoin interests me precisely because its main advantage is not being the newest or most sophisticated technology. It is resilience. Bitcoin has already gone through cycles of euphoria and collapse, attacks, forks, regulatory changes, crises of confidence, and countless predictions that it was dead. This does not guarantee that it will exist forever, but it demonstrates something that most other cryptocurrencies have not yet had time to demonstrate: the ability to remain relevant across different economic environments and different market cycles. For a thesis that I am willing to hold through 2030, I prefer to bet on an asset that has already passed this test several times rather than try to discover which will be the next winning altcoin.

Perhaps this is precisely one of the characteristics that attracts me most to Bitcoin. It is relatively boring. While the crypto market is constantly searching for the next innovation, Bitcoin continues to defend essentially the same proposition: a digital, decentralized monetary network with a limited supply. It may seem like little compared with projects that promise to revolutionize dozens of industries at once, but this simplicity also makes the thesis easier to understand and follow. I do not need to discover which technology will dominate in 2030. I only need to believe that the monetary property Bitcoin offers will continue to be desirable and that the network will remain relevant enough to preserve that property over time.

## The pyramid and bubble criticism

I also do not think it is possible to discuss Bitcoin without addressing one of the most recurring criticisms: the idea that it is a pyramid scheme or a new Tulip Mania. To me, these comparisons conflate different things. A pyramid scheme depends on an organized recruitment structure and the transfer of resources among participants, generally sustained by promises of returns. Bitcoin does not have a central company promising returns, nor an operator responsible for compensating earlier participants using money from new ones. This does not mean there is no speculation around the asset, nor that people cannot buy Bitcoin simply expecting to resell it at a higher price. But this behavior also exists in stocks, real estate, commodities, and virtually any asset traded in a market.

Likewise, I have no difficulty admitting that Bitcoin can form bubbles. This has already happened and will probably happen again. An asset can have real fundamentals and still trade far above or below a value that, in retrospect, would seem reasonable. The existence of speculation does not eliminate the existence of fundamentals. Bitcoin has a functioning network, a known monetary policy, a verifiable maximum supply, global liquidity, and growing financial infrastructure. We can discuss whether it is expensive or cheap, but simply calling it a pyramid does not answer the question I consider truly important: what function can this asset perform once its infrastructure is mature, and how far can institutional adoption take it?

## Artificial intelligence

The variable that interests me most for the next decade may not even be directly related to Bitcoin: artificial intelligence. We are entering a period of technological competition between the United States and China that will require extraordinary amounts of capital. The construction of datacenters, semiconductors, power grids, computing infrastructure, and artificial intelligence systems will require investments on a scale that is still difficult to quantify. Companies will need to raise capital to finance this expansion, governments will need to invest in strategic infrastructure, and investors will need to decide how to allocate resources in an economy increasingly dependent on computing, energy, and processing capacity. If AI truly becomes the main technological platform of the coming decades, we are talking about a transformation that could move trillions of dollars and profoundly alter how capital, labor, and productivity are organized in the global economy.

This is where I begin to see a connection with Bitcoin that may still be little discussed. I do not know exactly how artificial intelligence will transform the financial system, and I do not believe it is possible to predict this precisely. But I can imagine an economy in which AI agents directly participate in transactions, contract services, move capital, and interact with financial systems with increasingly less human intervention. A company could have autonomous agents negotiating computing resources, paying suppliers, or contracting services in different countries. An economy composed of billions of machines and digital agents may require forms of value transfer that are global, programmable, verifiable, and independent of the financial infrastructure of a single country. I am not saying that Bitcoin will necessarily be “the currency of AIs,” because that would be an extrapolation that my thesis does not need to make. Nor do I imagine Bitcoin as a solution for small everyday payments. The question that interests me concerns large movements of value: in an increasingly automated, global, and digital economy, what role will an asset that belongs to no company or government, can be transferred globally, and has a supply that cannot be expanded play? If companies, institutions, or digital agents need to move large amounts of capital across different jurisdictions, an asset with these characteristics could acquire a relevant function as an instrument for transferring and settling value, even if everyday payments continue to be made primarily through traditional currencies and financial infrastructures.

This question becomes even more interesting when we remember that the race for artificial intelligence will not only be a technological race, but also a race for capital. The United States and China have enormous strategic incentives to finance this infrastructure and not fall behind, and this could create a decade of extraordinary investment in technology, energy, and computing. I do not know what the monetary consequences of this process will be, but I consider it plausible that an economy requiring increasingly more capital will also increase the importance of assets capable of preserving value in an environment of monetary expansion and enormous investment. This is where Bitcoin's scarcity ceases to be merely an interesting characteristic and may begin to acquire much greater importance. If there is increasingly more capital in the world looking for scarce assets, the fact that there are only 21 million bitcoins could become much more relevant than it appears today.

Perhaps this connection between artificial intelligence and Bitcoin is completely wrong. But, to me, that is precisely one of the reasons to look toward 2030 rather than trying to evaluate the thesis solely based on what has happened so far. I am not trying to predict the price of Bitcoin four years from now. I am trying to imagine what functions this asset may perform after another four years of institutionalization, regulation, financial infrastructure development, and technological transformation. If, by then, Bitcoin continues to be merely a speculative asset used primarily to bet on its own appreciation, my thesis will be much weaker. If, on the other hand, it simultaneously becomes a store of value, an institutional asset, a form of collateral, and a permanent component of global financial infrastructure, then we will probably be looking at a very different reality from the one we have today. And perhaps it is precisely the combination of the expansion of the digital economy, the race for capital driven by AI, and Bitcoin's absolute scarcity that makes this latter possibility so interesting to me.

## 2030: a horizon of events, not a price target

That is why 2030 remains my horizon. Not because I have a specific price in mind, but because I want enough time to discover whether the thesis corresponds to reality. By then, I want to observe whether institutional adoption advances, whether financial infrastructure continues to develop, whether regulation reduces uncertainty, and whether Bitcoin begins to more clearly occupy the space of a store of value. Price will be a consequence of this process, not the starting point.

I expected more from Bitcoin this year and remain disappointed with part of the path it has taken. But this disappointment taught me to separate the performance of an asset from the validity of a thesis. The chart can be frustrating for years while the thesis continues to develop. That is why I do not want to reach 2030 with only an answer about how much one Bitcoin is worth. I want to discover what role it will have earned within the global financial system.

Until then, I do not need to convince anyone, nor do I need to get the maximum or minimum price right. I only need to observe whether reality continues moving in the direction I believe in. And, despite everything that has happened so far, I still believe it will.
