AI’s potential is framed as an emerging architectural decision-making layer rather than merely an evolution of machine learning or a mechanism for task/code replacement. Its significance depends on whether this layer can scale within real systems, where cost, load, observability, governance, security, maintenance, versioning, auditing, integration, and failure handling matter.

Initial assessment: LLMs appeared to be sophisticated ML that needed to prove real-world scalability. Vibe coding and broad code generation seemed limited by incomplete context, poor architecture, security, technical debt, hallucinations, observability gaps, and unpredictable costs. The decisive change was the emergence of an ecosystem—MCP, A2A, agents/sub-agents, memory, tools, local models, self-hosting, SDKs, provider abstraction—that makes AI composable within existing architectures. Scale therefore means not only processing volume but the ability to insert intelligence selectively into existing systems: deterministic code and traditional systems remain where predictability is preferable; humans remain where judgment/accountability are required; agents operate between them where problems are too ambiguous for fixed rules but need not require human intervention at every step.

Agents can interpret requests, gather context, choose tools, decompose tasks, delegate, evaluate results, and dynamically determine execution sequences. This differs from replacing existing systems: the existing architecture can remain while an intelligence layer decides how to use its capabilities. AI consequently occupies an intermediate position between deterministic code and human decision-making: capable of handling unanticipated ambiguity but constrained by supplied context, objectives, tools, permissions, and validation. The important architectural shift is from specifying all behavior in advance toward allowing some logic to be determined dynamically from context, objectives, and available capabilities.

AI-assisted architecture work demonstrates a distinction between delegating decisions and increasing decision-making capacity. AI can be used to explore alternatives, challenge assumptions, generate counterarguments, test architectures, identify weaknesses, and examine trade-offs before a decision exists. Its value therefore includes reducing the cost of hypothesis exploration and broadening the possibility space considered before decisions. Responsibility remains human because organizational constraints, policies, history, risks, and consequences may not be represented in model context. Expertise remains important because experienced architects identify relevant patterns, risks, questions, constraints, and trade-offs, and determine which AI-generated hypotheses matter. AI can execute or explore possibilities; it does not inherently determine which problem should be solved or all relevant constraints. Poor specifications can yield technically convincing solutions to the wrong problem. As AI becomes better at execution, specification, problem definition, constraints, context, and success criteria become more important.

Greater agent autonomy increases the need for explicit engineering. Requirements, business rules, acceptance criteria, architectural context, and organizational knowledge that humans previously supplied implicitly must become explicit, structured, and versioned because agents cannot reliably operate on knowledge absent from their context. Human organizations historically compensated for incomplete documentation through tacit knowledge held by developers, architects, analysts, and specialists; agentic systems expose these gaps because plausible inference is not equivalent to a business rule.

PDCA can function as a deterministic control structure around probabilistic agents: Plan receives requirements, context, and constraints and may invoke specialized agents for documentation, system analysis, dependencies, alternatives, and risks; Do executes an approved plan through specialized agents for code, tests, documentation, and security; Check independently validates requirements, tests, metrics, contracts, architecture, and consistency; Act determines whether to proceed, iterate, invoke an exception handler, or escalate to a human. The orchestration can remain deterministic while internal agent execution is probabilistic, preserving predictable process-level behavior.

OODA can structure an agent’s planning/reasoning cycle: Observe gathers current state and missing information; Orient relates it to requirements, business rules, architectural constraints, and knowledge; Decide selects strategy; Act executes or delegates. The cycle can iterate when new information emerges. Specialized sub-agents can handle requirements, security, architecture, code, testing, documentation, observability, and validation rather than creating one monolithic agent. A controlled architecture can therefore combine deterministic external workflow, probabilistic internal problem solving, explicit permissions, tool contracts, stopping conditions, validation, and human escalation. This transforms engineering processes into executable systems in which humans and agents participate together.

Agentic systems must be able to recognize insufficient context and obtain missing information rather than fabricate conclusions. Corporate knowledge is distributed across systems, documents, departments, specialists, and process owners. An agent may need to query systems, consult documents, delegate to another agent, or ask a human stakeholder. Human communication can therefore become another execution capability. An agent may interrupt execution, request missing information or approval, update its context, reassess, and continue. Organizational architecture consequently needs representations not only of systems and APIs but also responsibilities, knowledge sources, decision authorities, and mandatory human-approval points. Autonomy can mean executing what is automatable and involving humans precisely where their knowledge or authority is required, rather than eliminating human participation.

MCP can be understood more broadly than “OpenAPI for agents.” Traditional APIs expose predefined operations; an agent-oriented interface can expose capabilities through which an agent discovers how to investigate and solve a problem. An MCP environment could provide resources, context, specialized tools, and investigation mechanisms rather than merely exposing hundreds of endpoints. An agent can receive a problem without a predefined sequence, discover missing information, query capabilities, interpret results, select subsequent actions, validate progress, and iterate. This does not require unrestricted autonomy: deterministic workflows, permissions, contracts, human approvals, and policy controls can constrain the process. The distinction is operational: APIs expose functions; capabilities provide means for contextual problem solving; agents determine when/how capabilities are used; processes define boundaries and success criteria. Humans and agents could interact with the same capability layer, differing primarily in who drives the investigation.

The major underestimated risk is social engineering against agentic systems. When AI has access to documents, credentials, APIs, tools, and corporate systems, manipulated context can cause unauthorized actions rather than merely incorrect answers. Documents, webpages, messages, or other retrieved content can contain instructions intended to influence the agent. Language models may not reliably distinguish information from authoritative instruction unless provenance, authority, and scope are architecturally represented.

Multi-tenancy makes this critical. Tenant isolation cannot depend on the model correctly interpreting ownership. Search, tools, identifiers, memory, and retrieved context can accidentally cross tenant boundaries. Context availability is not authorization; registration of a tool is not authorization to execute it; knowledge of an identifier is not permission to access it; instructions embedded in content do not automatically have authority. Identity, tenant, context, capability, and authority must remain distinct and propagate through execution. Authorization must be enforced independently at architectural/data/tool layers rather than trusted to model reasoning. Least privilege should apply to agents and tools; sensitive operations should have independent policies, auditability, and potentially human approval. Automation can amplify failures because an authorization flaw or incorrect decision may be propagated repeatedly at scale.

The core security principle is that autonomy must be separated from authority. Agents can autonomously choose among permitted ways of solving a problem without acquiring permissions themselves. They can plan an operation without being authorized to execute every step. Model instructions can reinforce security but cannot be the final security boundary. Probabilistic intelligence can operate inside deterministic authorization, tenant, capability, and security boundaries.

AI may increase rather than decrease the importance of engineering. Historically, documentation, testing, architecture, requirements, and governance could be treated as overhead because implementation was expensive and speed pressured teams toward minimizing non-code work. These activities reduce future costs by preventing maintenance problems, regressions, architectural rigidity, incorrect implementation, and poor decisions. If AI makes code, tests, documentation, diagrams, analysis, and validation cheap, the bottleneck shifts from artifact production to determining which artifacts should exist, from which information, and under which constraints. Requirements become operational specifications for both humans and agents; acceptance criteria can drive verification; architecture documentation provides execution context; business rules need explicit representations. AI can help generate, update, cross-check, and validate these artifacts. A sprint can therefore become a controlled cycle of planning, implementation, verification, and learning rather than primarily a window for maximizing code output. Lower implementation cost makes problem definition, architecture, context, constraints, and validation comparatively more important.

AI-generated content creates a separate data-quality risk. As synthetic text, images, video, code, and other material proliferate, future models may increasingly train on outputs derived from previous models. Errors, biases, simplifications, and artificial patterns can propagate or amplify through recursive synthetic-data cycles. Synthetic data is not inherently harmful when deliberately generated, controlled, and validated; the problem is failing to distinguish human-originated data, deliberately curated synthetic data, and recycled model-generated content. As artificial information becomes cheaper and more abundant, trustworthy provenance, proprietary data, historical records, real-world experiments, and specialized human knowledge may become more strategically valuable. Data quantity does not imply knowledge quality.

AI’s technological importance and the economic valuation of AI companies are separate questions. A speculative bubble can coexist with a genuinely transformative technology. Companies can be overvalued, infrastructure costs underestimated, adoption speed exaggerated, and predictions about autonomous agents or labor transformation overstated while the underlying technology remains historically significant. The dot-com analogy illustrates that a market bubble can burst while the underlying technology persists and becomes infrastructure. The relevant post-bubble question is what technological capabilities continue to exist and generate durable value.

Determining whether AI represents a change of historical era may be impossible contemporaneously because historical eras are recognized retrospectively. Possible futures include a major technological transformation, a large speculative bubble, a transformative technology accompanied by incorrect predictions about timing and impacts, or combinations of these. Specific predictions about AGI, widespread worker replacement, and complete autonomy remain uncertain; demonstrations alone do not establish economic or social transformation. Nevertheless, AI has already progressed from a model/API-centric technology toward an architectural ecosystem with protocols, standards, agents, local models, integrations, and mechanisms for embedding intelligence into existing systems. Its emerging role is not merely productivity or code generation but contextual exploration and dynamic decision-making within controlled architectures.

Core trajectory: initial question = whether AI can scale; current question = how far an architectural decision-making layer can scale.
