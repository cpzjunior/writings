The introduction presents a personal and epistemic question about AI’s true potential, arguing that it may be too early to assess because society is attempting to understand a transformation while still inside it, without access to the future perspective needed to judge its historical scale. The author explains that their initial view of AI was deliberately pragmatic: large language models appeared to be sophisticated extensions of machine learning that would need to demonstrate their ability to move from impressive demonstrations and isolated experiments into scalable, reliable real-world architectures. As a solutions architect, the relevant questions were therefore operational—cost, load, observability, governance, failure modes, integration, security, maintenance, versioning, auditing, and behavior at large scale—leading initially to skepticism about approaches such as vibe coding, which seemed unlikely to survive enterprise constraints involving legacy systems, complex business rules, security, governance, and technical debt. The expected trajectory was that experimentation would produce impressive early results before hidden problems emerged and organizations discovered that engineering could not simply be replaced by code generation. The author emphasizes that this was not opposition to AI but an application of ordinary architectural criteria: a technology becomes strategically important when it can be incorporated into real systems without introducing greater problems than it solves. What changed this assessment was that AI itself evolved while it was being evaluated, eventually developing an ecosystem involving protocols such as MCP and A2A, agents, sub-agents, memory, tools, local models, self-hosting, SDKs, and model abstraction layers. AI increasingly appeared not merely as an API producing responses but as a composable architectural layer capable of interpreting context, selecting and coordinating existing capabilities, and dynamically determining parts of system behavior. This led to a revised understanding of “scale”: the important development was not simply greater processing capacity or better models, but the ability to incorporate intelligence into existing systems without rebuilding everything around AI. Traditional deterministic systems could continue handling predictable operations, humans could retain decisions requiring judgment and accountability, while agents could occupy an intermediate layer for tasks too ambiguous for fixed rules but too operational for constant human intervention. Agents could interpret requests, gather information, choose tools, decompose problems, delegate work, and evaluate results, effectively introducing dynamic decision-making into architectures that previously required behavior to be specified in advance. The author therefore concludes that the more consequential question is not whether AI can replace programmers or existing activities, but what becomes possible when a new layer of intelligence is placed over systems that already exist.

The “When AI Began to Scale” section explains that the author’s changing perception resulted less from models simply becoming more capable than from an emerging architecture around them. MCP, A2A, agents, sub-agents, memory, tools, local models, self-hosting, SDKs, and abstraction libraries created protocols, integration patterns, discovery mechanisms, context controls, compositional structures, and multiple execution strategies, making AI increasingly resemble a platform rather than an isolated tool. The relevant scaling therefore concerns composability: organizations can select models according to specific problems, run them locally, switch providers, connect tools, delegate to specialized agents, and insert intelligence at selected points in existing architectures. The author distinguishes a better model, which is a technological improvement, from a new way of composing systems, which is an architectural change. This makes it unnecessary to imagine organizations rebuilding themselves entirely around AI; instead, intelligence can be introduced selectively while deterministic code, traditional systems, and human judgment retain roles where they remain appropriate. Agents are particularly interesting because they can interpret requests, gather information, select tools, decompose problems, delegate to sub-agents, and evaluate results while operating on top of existing services rather than replacing them. This suggests that the major transformation may not be programmer replacement but the ability to determine parts of system logic dynamically according to context, objectives, and available capabilities. AI therefore occupies an intermediate position between conventional code and human decision-making: unlike traditional automation, it can handle situations not fully anticipated in advance, but unlike an unconstrained artificial human, it operates within the capabilities, objectives, context, and constraints established by the architecture. This realization caused the author to reconsider the original question of whether AI could replace existing work; the more important question became what new capabilities emerge when intelligence is added to the systems already built.

The “The Architect and AI” section describes how direct use of AI in the author’s daily architectural work changed their understanding of its value. Rather than asking AI what to do and blindly following the answer, the author uses it to explore problems, provide context, examine alternatives, challenge assumptions, generate counterarguments, compare architectures, expose weaknesses, investigate trade-offs, and deliberately argue against conclusions already reached. AI can therefore participate before a decision exists, reducing the cost of exploring hypotheses and alternatives rather than merely accelerating execution of an already chosen solution. This differs from conventional code generation, where productivity gains can be measured through reduced implementation time; reasoning assistance is harder to quantify because its value lies in expanding the number and breadth of possibilities that can be examined before a decision. The author emphasizes the distinction between delegating a decision and increasing a person’s capacity to make one: responsibility for architecture remains human because organizational constraints, internal policies, system history, risks, and consequences may not be available to the model, while AI provides a real-time reasoning counterpart. Experience therefore remains relevant not simply because experienced architects possess many answers, but because they recognize patterns, identify risks, know which questions matter, and understand hidden trade-offs. AI can generate hypotheses, alternatives, and questions rapidly, but humans must determine which are relevant and identify constraints or consequences the model missed. The author consequently rejects a simple replacement narrative for complex technical work, distinguishing execution of an instruction from deciding which instruction should exist. Agents can interpret context and choose among possibilities, but they still require objectives, constraints, context, and validation mechanisms, and they cannot independently determine the full problem they should solve. Poorly specified problems remain problematic because AI can generate highly convincing solutions to the wrong problem, making the traditional “garbage in, garbage out” principle particularly significant when the output appears authoritative. The increasing capability of AI therefore increases the importance of specification: cheaper code generation makes problem definition more important, and greater agent autonomy requires clearer objectives, boundaries, context, and success criteria. The architect’s role may consequently shift from knowing every answer toward structuring problems, defining constraints, evaluating alternatives, and designing systems in which humans and agents can collaborate. AI becomes more than a productivity tool when it participates in determining what should be built rather than merely producing the resulting artifacts.

The “Maybe We Need More Engineering, Not Less” section argues that greater agent autonomy may increase rather than diminish the need for explicit engineering discipline. Traditional systems can rely heavily on tacit organizational knowledge held by developers, architects, analysts, and operators, allowing undocumented assumptions and exceptions to be supplied by people during execution. Agentic systems cannot reliably access knowledge that remains implicit, so requirements, business rules, objectives, constraints, success criteria, and other previously informal knowledge need to become explicit, structured, and versioned. Requirements documentation therefore becomes part of the operational context used by agents rather than merely a reference for human developers. The author connects this to established engineering processes such as PDCA, interpreting them as control mechanisms for continuously planning, executing, checking, and correcting rather than as bureaucratic rituals. A possible agentic architecture could have a deterministic workflow coordinating Plan, Do, Check, and Act, while specialized agents and sub-agents perform probabilistic reasoning within each stage. A planning agent could consume requirements and architecture context, investigate dependencies, research alternatives, and identify risks; an execution agent could coordinate specialists responsible for code, tests, documentation, and security; a checking agent could independently verify implementation against requirements, tests, contracts, metrics, and architectural constraints; and a control agent could determine whether the process should finish, repeat, invoke a specialized exception path, or escalate to a human. OODA could operate inside planning, allowing agents to observe system state, orient themselves using requirements and constraints, decide on strategies, act or delegate, and repeat when new information changes the situation. The author stresses that the entire system need not become probabilistic: orchestration can remain deterministic while individual agents perform probabilistic tasks within controlled boundaries. This preserves predictability where required while allowing agents flexibility where traditional rules are insufficient. Sub-agents can specialize in requirements, security, architecture, code, testing, documentation, observability, and validation, creating computational decision hierarchies analogous in some respects to human organizational structures. The resulting architecture would transform requirements, architecture, development, testing, documentation, and validation from loosely connected human activities into executable processes involving both people and agents. The conclusion is that AI may not eliminate engineering processes but make them more explicit and executable, enabling humans and agents to participate in structured workflows without requiring either complete automation or human intervention at every step.

The “When AI Needs to Ask” section focuses on the difference between an AI that answers a sufficiently specified question and an agent responsible for achieving an objective. An autonomous agent will inevitably encounter cases where available context is insufficient, especially because corporate knowledge is distributed among systems, documents, departments, specialists, and process owners. Instead of converting missing information into an assumption, an agent could identify the dependency, locate the responsible stakeholder, request the required information or decision, update its execution context, and continue. Human communication thus becomes another tool available to the agent, alongside APIs, documents, and other agents. This is important because real-world processes are rarely completely specified at initiation; traditional systems handle unexpected conditions by interrupting and handing them to people, whereas an agent can dynamically discover which additional steps are necessary. The same mechanism reframes hallucination: sometimes the issue is not selecting the wrong answer from known possibilities but attempting to answer when available information cannot support a reliable conclusion. In such cases, greater generative capability is insufficient; the agent must recognize inadequate context, seek another source, request confirmation, or escalate to someone with authority. Agent execution therefore becomes potentially non-linear: a task can begin, encounter an unknown dependency, pause for information, receive a response, reassess the context, and continue. This requires architecture to represent not only systems and APIs but also organizational responsibilities, knowledge ownership, approval authorities, and situations requiring human participation. Autonomy consequently does not eliminate humans but allows their involvement to occur more precisely at points where knowledge or authority is genuinely required. The defining intelligence of such an agent is therefore not merely its ability to generate an answer but its ability to determine what next action is necessary to obtain sufficient information for a reliable decision, whether that means querying a system, consulting a document, delegating to another agent, or asking a person.

The “MCP Does Not Need to Be a New OpenAPI” section argues that MCP may be more significant than simply serving as a standardized API-like mechanism through which agents access tools and resources. OpenAPI primarily describes predefined operations exposed by a system, whereas an agent-oriented environment could expose capabilities through which an agent investigates what can be discovered and accomplished. The author connects this to PDCA: processes exist partly because humans cannot anticipate every future problem, so they establish structured methods for discovering solutions through planning, execution, verification, and learning. An agentic system can similarly receive a request without a predefined execution sequence, identify missing information and necessary capabilities, query tools, interpret results, determine subsequent actions, and continue until the defined objective is satisfied. This does not imply unrestricted autonomy; workflows can remain deterministic, permissions can remain explicit, tool contracts can remain clear, and sensitive actions can require human approval. The difference is that programmers no longer need to hard-code every possible sequence for using those capabilities. The author therefore distinguishes traditional APIs, which expose functions, from potential MCP environments that expose broader capabilities, including resources, context, specialized tools, and investigation mechanisms. Rather than requiring an agent to know every endpoint beforehand, such an environment could allow it to begin with a question, discover missing information, consult sources, interpret results, and dynamically determine the next capability to invoke. Humans and agents could potentially use the same capability layer, with the difference being who drives the investigation and decision-making process. MCP would therefore not need to answer user questions directly; it would need to provide the means by which an agent can investigate and discover answers. This suggests a broader architectural shift in which APIs provide operations, tools provide capabilities, agents decide when and how to use them, and processes establish the boundaries and criteria governing those decisions. The historical role of engineering processes has been to provide systematic methods for dealing with problems that cannot be fully predicted, and agentic systems may now allow part of that problem-solving process itself to be delegated to machines.

The “The Problem Few People Seem to Take Seriously” section identifies social engineering against agentic systems as an underestimated security problem. When AI merely generates answers, manipulated context may produce incorrect information; when agents possess tools, credentials, documents, APIs, and corporate-system access, manipulated context can potentially induce unauthorized actions. This creates an attack surface in which information itself can attempt to influence model behavior: documents, webpages, messages, or other external content may contain instructions designed to redirect an agent toward actions that should not be taken. The fundamental difficulty is that a language model may not reliably distinguish information from authoritative instructions unless the architecture explicitly establishes provenance, authority, and scope. Multi-tenant systems make this particularly consequential because tenant isolation cannot depend on the model correctly interpreting which data belongs to which customer. Search filters, tool authorization, identifiers, shared memory, and contextual information can all become paths through which one tenant’s information reaches another if authorization is not enforced independently of the model. The author therefore distinguishes context from authorization: availability of information to the model does not imply permission to use it, registration of a tool does not imply permission to execute it, and an instruction contained in a document does not automatically have system authority. Identity, tenant, context, capability, and authority must be treated as separate concepts and carried through the execution flow. Authorization should be enforced by architecture and data sources rather than by trusting model reasoning, and least privilege should apply to agents and tools just as it does to conventional services. Agents should have only the capabilities required for their tasks, sensitive operations should have independent policies, tool-level authorization should verify access, and higher-risk operations may require human approval. The risk is amplified by automation because an authorization flaw or unexpected tool capability can be repeatedly exploited at scale. Security therefore needs multiple independent layers covering identity, tenant context, data filtering, tool authorization, policy enforcement, provenance, auditing, and escalation. The central architectural principle is that autonomy and authority must be separated: an agent can autonomously choose how to solve a problem without being able to expand its own permissions or access resources beyond those authorized. The agent’s intelligence may be probabilistic, but tenant boundaries, authorization rules, and security controls cannot depend on probabilistic interpretation.

The “Maybe AI Will Force Us to Take Engineering More Seriously” section argues that AI could alter the traditional trade-off between development speed and engineering quality. Activities such as documentation, testing, architecture, requirements, and governance have sometimes been treated as overhead because they do not immediately produce software, even though they reduce future costs associated with maintenance, regressions, structural changes, incorrect requirements, and poor decisions. If agents can generate code, tests, documentation, diagrams, analyses, and other artifacts rapidly, the bottleneck may shift away from producing artifacts toward ensuring that the artifacts are correct, contextually appropriate, and consistent with requirements and constraints. Requirements could therefore become operational specifications for both humans and machines; acceptance criteria could become inputs to verification agents; architectural documentation could provide context for agents; and business rules could be represented explicitly rather than remaining distributed across people. AI could also help maintain these artifacts by generating documentation from code changes, comparing implementations against requirements, detecting inconsistencies, and checking whether code, tests, documentation, and acceptance criteria remain aligned. This could change the meaning of a sprint from a race to maximize deliverables into a controlled cycle of planning, implementation, validation, and learning, consistent with PDCA. If implementation becomes dramatically cheaper, producing code ceases to be the primary difficulty; understanding what should be built, defining constraints, selecting architecture, supplying adequate context, and verifying results become more important. Rather than making engineering less relevant, AI could increase its importance because it reduces the cost of performing the activities that disciplined engineering requires. The potential productivity gain is therefore not merely faster code generation but making it economically practical to perform requirements analysis, testing, documentation, architectural validation, and other quality practices consistently.

The “The Data Problem” section raises uncertainty about whether future AI systems will have access to sufficiently high-quality data as synthetic content increasingly dominates the information environment. As AI makes text, images, video, code, and other material cheaper to generate, future models may increasingly train on content produced by earlier models, potentially creating recursive cycles in which errors, biases, simplifications, and artificial patterns are reproduced and amplified. The author does not claim that synthetic data is inherently harmful; controlled synthetic data can be highly valuable when deliberately generated, validated, and curated, including for scenarios that are expensive or impossible to collect directly. The concern arises when distinctions between human-generated material, intentionally produced synthetic datasets, and content merely recycled from previous models disappear. This creates a potential paradox: the easier it becomes to manufacture information, the more strategically valuable trustworthy-origin information may become. Proprietary data, historical records, real-world experiments, specialized expertise, and human-produced content could therefore become important assets because they cannot be replaced as easily by recursively generated material. The central issue is consequently not data abundance but data provenance and quality: AI requires data to improve, but increasing the quantity of available content does not necessarily increase the amount of reliable knowledge.

The “What If All of This Is a Bubble?” section preserves skepticism about the economic and financial dimensions of AI while separating them from the technology’s potential significance. The author considers it possible that AI valuations are excessive, that infrastructure economics may be underestimated, that companies currently perceived as inevitable may disappear, and that predictions about autonomous agents, AGI, and labor-market transformation may prove substantially wrong. However, the author distinguishes the question of how transformative a technology is from the question of how much investors should pay for companies attempting to develop it. The dot-com era is presented as an analogy: the speculative bubble involved excessive valuations and failed companies, but the collapse of those businesses did not invalidate the underlying historical importance of the internet. A similar situation could occur with AI, in which capital is misallocated, expectations are exaggerated, implementation proceeds more slowly than anticipated, and many companies fail while the underlying technology nevertheless becomes deeply embedded in software architectures and infrastructure. Major technological transformations can coexist with speculative excess because enthusiasm produces unrealistic predictions and attracts capital to projects that later disappear, while useful components survive and become infrastructure after the market corrects itself. The author therefore regards the possibility of an AI bubble as compatible with the possibility of profound technological transformation. The more important question is not whether a bubble exists but what technologies, architectures, practices, and capabilities remain after speculative expectations and unsuccessful companies have been removed.

The “The Problem of Recognizing a Change of Era” section returns to the epistemic difficulty of judging historical transformation while living through it. Historical eras and ruptures are generally recognized retrospectively because participants lack knowledge of what follows, so people living through the decline of Antiquity or the period preceding the French Revolution could not know how later historians would classify their circumstances. The author suggests that the same limitation applies to AI: it may be impossible to determine in the present whether the current period marks the beginning of a new era, a technological bubble, or a combination in which transformative technology coexists with exaggerated market expectations and failed predictions. The author’s current position is therefore less definitive than their earlier skepticism. They remain skeptical of highly specific claims about AGI, widespread worker replacement, and fully autonomous systems because impressive demonstrations do not establish economic or social transformation. At the same time, their direct experience as a solutions architect has made it increasingly difficult to regard AI as merely more sophisticated machine learning: the technology has developed protocols, standards, agents, local models, integration mechanisms, and architectural patterns that allow it to be progressively embedded into existing systems. The author is also personally participating in this change by using AI repeatedly to reason about technical problems, explore alternatives, challenge decisions, and investigate solutions, while retaining responsibility for the decisions themselves. The starting question was whether AI could scale; the current question is how far this new decision-making layer can scale. The ultimate uncertainty remains whether this represents the beginning of a new era, a historical bubble, or both, but the author’s perception has already changed from asking whether AI is important to examining the magnitude of the transformation it may produce.
