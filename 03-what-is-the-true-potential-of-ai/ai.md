The author questions whether it is possible to understand AI’s true potential while still living through the transformation it may be causing. Their perception has changed substantially over the past two or three years: initially, they viewed large language models as an impressive but conventional evolution of machine learning that would need to demonstrate that they could move beyond controlled demonstrations and integrate sustainably into real-world systems. As a solutions architect, their primary concern was not whether AI could work in isolated cases but whether it could scale under real constraints such as cost, load, observability, governance, security, maintenance, versioning, auditing, legacy integration, and large user bases. This led to skepticism toward approaches such as vibe coding, which seemed useful for prototypes but difficult to reconcile with complex enterprise environments, where poor architecture, insufficient context, security issues, technical debt, hallucinations, and unpredictable costs could emerge after the initial enthusiasm. The author expected companies to discover that AI could not simply replace engineering, but emphasizes that this was not opposition to AI; it was an attempt to evaluate it according to normal enterprise-architecture criteria. What they did not anticipate was how rapidly the technology and its surrounding architecture would evolve, suggesting that perhaps “can AI scale?” was itself the wrong question.

## When AI Began to Scale

The author's perception changed when AI evolved from a model accessed through an API into an architectural layer supported by MCP, A2A, agents, sub-agents, memory, tools, local models, self-hosting, SDKs, and abstractions across providers and models. The significant development was not merely that models became more capable, but that AI became composable and could be embedded selectively into existing systems: deterministic code could continue handling predictable operations, humans could retain decisions requiring accountability or contextual judgment, and agents could operate between these extremes by interpreting requests, gathering information, choosing tools, decomposing problems, delegating work, and evaluating results. This led the author to reconsider the idea that AI's primary significance would be replacing programmers. Instead, AI could introduce a new decision-making layer between traditional code and human decisions, allowing part of system behavior to be determined dynamically from context, objectives, and available capabilities rather than being completely specified in advance. The architectural implication is therefore not necessarily that organizations must rebuild around AI, but that existing architectures can gain an additional layer capable of deciding how their existing capabilities should be used, making the author's original scale-oriented evaluation incomplete because the technology was changing the architecture in which “scale” itself should be understood.

## The Architect and AI

The author now uses AI repeatedly in their own architectural work, primarily as a reasoning partner rather than as an autonomous decision-maker or code generator. They use conversations with AI to explore alternatives, challenge assumptions, identify weaknesses, compare architectures, investigate trade-offs, generate counterarguments, and deliberately attempt to disprove conclusions, sometimes beginning without knowing what the best solution is. The benefit is therefore difficult to measure as simple productivity because the main effect may be expanding the number of hypotheses and alternatives that can be explored before a decision is made, while reducing the cost of obtaining second opinions and examining possibilities. The author distinguishes increasing a person's decision-making capacity from delegating responsibility for decisions: architectural responsibility remains with the human because organizational constraints, policies, system history, risks, and consequences may not be represented in the model's context. They argue that experience is less about possessing every answer than recognizing patterns, identifying risks, knowing which questions matter, and understanding hidden trade-offs, while AI can rapidly generate hypotheses and questions that experienced professionals must still evaluate. This leads to a distinction between executing an instruction and determining which instruction should exist: agents can interpret context and operate within a space of possibilities, but still require objectives, constraints, context, and validation. Poorly specified problems can therefore produce convincing but inappropriate solutions, making specification increasingly important as AI becomes more capable. The author consequently suggests that the architect's role may shift from knowing all answers toward structuring problems, defining constraints, evaluating alternatives, and designing systems in which humans and agents cooperate, meaning AI is increasingly entering the engineering process itself rather than merely producing engineering artifacts.

## Maybe We Need More Engineering, Not Less

The author argues that greater agent autonomy may increase rather than decrease the need for disciplined engineering because agents require explicit representations of information that humans can otherwise supply through tacit organizational knowledge. Requirements, business rules, constraints, success criteria, and architectural context become operational inputs that agents need in order to make reliable decisions, making documentation more important as organizational knowledge must become explicit, structured, and versioned rather than remaining distributed across people's memories, conversations, tickets, and meetings. The author connects this with established process frameworks such as PDCA and OODA, proposing that deterministic workflows could govern probabilistic agents: a Plan stage could use agents and sub-agents to understand requirements, investigate dependencies, and evaluate alternatives; Do could coordinate specialized agents for implementation, testing, documentation, and security; Check could independently validate the result against requirements and architectural criteria; and Act could determine whether to finish, repeat the cycle, trigger specialized handling, or escalate to a human. OODA could operate inside planning agents as an iterative mechanism for observing the problem, orienting against available context and constraints, deciding on a strategy, and acting or delegating, with additional investigation triggered when information is missing. The key architectural distinction is that the workflow can remain deterministic even when internal execution is probabilistic, preserving predictable control over when agents act, what tools and information they can access, which criteria they must satisfy, and when they must stop. Specialized sub-agents could handle requirements, security, architecture, code, testing, documentation, observability, and validation rather than creating one monolithic autonomous agent. The resulting model is not an elimination of engineering processes but their transformation into executable structures in which humans and agents participate together, potentially making engineering practices more explicit rather than less important.

## When AI Needs to Ask

The author distinguishes an AI that answers a sufficiently defined question from an agent responsible for achieving an objective, because the latter must identify missing information, decisions, and resources during execution. In real organizations, necessary knowledge may reside in documents, systems, departments, specialists, or process owners, so an agent may need to recognize that technical information is insufficient and request a business clarification from the appropriate stakeholder rather than infer an answer. This gives agents the ability to initiate interactions and treat human communication as another operational capability alongside querying systems, using tools, and delegating to other agents. Real-world processes are often incompletely specified at their beginning, and an agent can potentially respond to unforeseen conditions by discovering which additional steps or sources of information are required instead of simply stopping or guessing. This also reframes hallucination: sometimes the issue is not choosing incorrectly among known possibilities but recognizing that the available context cannot support a reliable conclusion, in which case the appropriate behavior is to seek another source, request confirmation, or escalate to someone with authority. Agent execution therefore becomes potentially nonlinear, with tasks being paused, dependencies investigated, answers obtained, context reassessed, and execution resumed. At the organizational level, this requires representing not only systems and APIs but also responsibilities, knowledge ownership, approval requirements, and sources of authoritative information. Autonomy consequently does not mean eliminating humans but placing human participation precisely where knowledge or authority is necessary: systems are queried when information is in systems, documents are consulted when information is documented, agents are delegated to when specialized computation is appropriate, and people are asked when their knowledge or authority is required. Intelligence in this model is therefore partly the ability to determine what the next necessary step is rather than simply generating an answer from existing context.

## MCP Does Not Need to Be a New OpenAPI

The author sees MCP as potentially more significant than simply becoming an “OpenAPI for agents,” because traditional APIs expose predefined operations while agent-oriented systems could expose capabilities that agents dynamically investigate and compose according to the problem at hand. The comparison with PDCA illustrates this distinction: rather than encoding every possible sequence of operations in advance, an agent could receive an unresolved request, determine what information it needs, query available resources, evaluate the results, execute appropriate actions, check whether the objective has been satisfied, and repeat the cycle when necessary. This does not imply unrestricted autonomy; workflows can remain deterministic, permissions can be explicit, tools can have contracts, and sensitive operations can require human approval. The proposed distinction is that an API primarily exposes functions, whereas an MCP environment could expose capabilities, context, resources, queries, investigation mechanisms, and specialized tools that give an agent the means to discover how a problem might be solved. Humans could potentially use the same surface manually, with the difference being who drives the investigation. In such an architecture, MCP would not need to know the answer to a user's question or encode every execution path; it would provide the capabilities through which an agent can discover that answer at runtime. This suggests a broader architectural model in which APIs expose operations, tools expose capabilities, agents decide when and how to use them, and processes define the limits and criteria governing those decisions, allowing part of the problem-solving process previously encoded explicitly by programmers to be delegated to machines while retaining architectural controls.

## The Problem Few People Seem to Take Seriously

The author identifies social engineering and context manipulation as a major security concern for agentic systems because an AI with access to tools, documents, credentials, APIs, and corporate systems can potentially be influenced into taking unauthorized actions rather than merely producing an incorrect answer. Language-model systems introduce an attack surface in which technically valid information can contain instructions intended to manipulate the agent, and the model may not reliably distinguish descriptive content from authoritative commands unless the architecture explicitly establishes provenance, authority, and scope. This becomes particularly important in multi-tenant systems, where tenant isolation cannot depend on the model correctly interpreting which information belongs to which customer; authorization boundaries must be enforced by the architecture and data sources. Potential failures include searches returning another tenant's documents, tools accepting identifiers without validating ownership, shared memory carrying information across authorization boundaries, or agents using information obtained earlier without preserving its security context. The author therefore emphasizes that context is not authorization and that identity, tenant, context, capability, and authority must remain distinct concepts throughout execution. Agents may choose tools and propose actions, but independent authorization layers must determine whether those operations are actually permitted, with least privilege, data-layer filtering, tool-level authorization, auditing, explicit policy controls, and human approval for higher-risk operations providing additional safeguards. Because automation can amplify the scale and speed of an authorization failure, security needs to assume that agents can make mistakes, context can be manipulated, and tools can be used unexpectedly. The central principle is that autonomy and authority must be separated: an agent may have autonomy over how it solves a problem without having authority over every resource or operation involved. Probabilistic intelligence can operate inside deterministic security boundaries, but tenant isolation, authorization, and security controls cannot depend solely on probabilistic model behavior.

## Maybe AI Will Force Us to Take Engineering More Seriously

The author argues that AI may alter the historical trade-off between delivery speed and engineering quality because activities such as documentation, testing, analysis, diagrams, and validation could become much cheaper to produce. Traditionally, teams have sometimes treated these activities as secondary costs because they delay immediate delivery, even though they reduce future maintenance, regression, architectural, requirements, and governance costs. If agents can generate such artifacts rapidly, the primary challenge shifts from producing artifacts to ensuring that the artifacts are correct, mutually consistent, and based on appropriate requirements and constraints. Requirements documents could become operational specifications for both humans and agents; acceptance criteria could become inputs for verification agents; architectural documentation could provide execution context; and business rules could be represented explicitly rather than remaining distributed among people. Agents could also continuously generate documentation from code changes, compare implementations against requirements, detect inconsistencies, and enforce consistency among code, tests, documentation, and acceptance criteria. This could change the meaning of a sprint from a period focused primarily on maximizing delivered code into a controlled cycle of planning, implementation, validation, and learning, consistent with the PDCA model discussed earlier. If implementation becomes extremely inexpensive, the scarce engineering capability becomes understanding the problem, defining constraints, selecting architecture, supplying context, and verifying that the result solves the intended problem. The author therefore suggests that AI could make rigorous engineering economically easier rather than obsolete, because documentation, testing, analysis, and validation would no longer need to compete as heavily with implementation speed.

## The Data Problem

The author expresses skepticism toward highly optimistic AI forecasts because future model development may face challenges concerning the provenance and quality of training data. As AI makes it increasingly cheap to generate text, images, video, code, and other material, future datasets may contain growing amounts of content generated by earlier AI systems, creating the possibility that errors, biases, simplifications, and artificial patterns could be reproduced and amplified across successive generations. The author does not claim that synthetic data is inherently harmful: deliberately generated and carefully validated synthetic data can provide valuable examples, simulations, and controlled training material. The concern instead arises when distinctions between human-generated content, deliberately curated synthetic data, and content recursively produced or recycled by previous models become unclear. This creates a potential paradox in which increasing the ability to generate information makes information with trustworthy provenance more valuable. Proprietary datasets, historical records, real-world experiments, specialized knowledge, and human-produced material may therefore become strategic assets because they cannot be replaced as easily by another generation of synthetic content. The central issue is consequently not the quantity of available data but whether that data is sufficiently reliable and deserving of being treated as knowledge.

## What If All of This Is a Bubble?

The author remains skeptical of extreme AI predictions and acknowledges the possibility that parts of the AI market constitute a large speculative bubble, with some valuations potentially disconnected from economic capacity and some ambitious claims about autonomous agents, AGI, and labor-market transformation potentially failing to materialize. However, the author separates the technological question from the investment question: the market can overvalue companies attempting to develop a technology while the underlying technology itself remains historically significant. The dot-com episode is presented as an analogy in which speculative excesses, failed companies, and unrealistic predictions coexisted with the internet's genuine long-term transformation. Similar dynamics could apply to AI if infrastructure costs are underestimated, adoption proceeds more slowly than expected, companies fail, or current expectations prove excessive without implying that models, agents, or AI-enabled systems are irrelevant. Major technological transformations can generate exaggerated predictions and excessive capital allocation before market corrections eliminate weaker projects and leave successful technologies as infrastructure. The author therefore treats the possibility of an AI bubble as compatible with the possibility of a profound technological transformation and focuses less on whether a bubble exists than on what technological capabilities and infrastructure remain after speculative expectations and business failures have been filtered out.

## The Problem of Recognizing a Change of Era

The author concludes that it may be intrinsically difficult to determine whether AI represents a historical change of era while the transformation is occurring, because historical periods and ruptures are generally recognized retrospectively by people who possess knowledge of what followed. Current possibilities include AI ultimately being remembered as the beginning of a new era, as a major technological bubble, or as a genuinely transformative technology whose market expectations, timing, impacts, and apparent winners were nevertheless substantially wrong. The author therefore remains skeptical of specific predictions concerning AGI, widespread worker replacement, and completely autonomous systems, emphasizing that impressive demonstrations do not by themselves establish economic or social transformation. At the same time, their direct experience has made it impossible to view AI simply as more sophisticated machine learning: technologies such as protocols, agents, local models, integrations, and supporting architectures are making AI increasingly incorporable into existing systems, while the author already uses AI routinely to analyze technical problems, explore alternatives, challenge decisions, and improve problem-solving without treating it as the final authority. The author's perspective has consequently shifted from asking whether AI can scale to asking how far it can scale, while leaving unresolved whether the current period represents the beginning of a new era, a historical overreaction that will later be remembered as a bubble, or some combination in which genuine technological transformation coexists with substantial speculative excess.
