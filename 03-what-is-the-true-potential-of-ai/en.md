# What Is the True Potential of AI?

_The great transformation brought by AI may not lie in replacing what we already do, but in introducing a new layer of decision-making into the systems and processes we build._

**Summary:** I started out seeing AI as an evolution of machine learning that would need to prove it could scale in the real world, but my perception changed when it began to form a new architectural layer, capable of embedding intelligence into the systems we had already built. To me, its greatest potential does not lie simply in replacing programmers or automating tasks, but in enabling agents to interpret context, explore possibilities, and decide how to use existing capabilities within controlled processes. This makes engineering, specification, governance, and security even more important, because autonomy cannot mean authority. We still do not know whether we are facing a new era or a bubble, but I can no longer look at AI merely as a more sophisticated technology: what matters now is discovering how far this new decision-making layer can scale.

---

There is a question I have been asking myself more and more over the past few months: what is the true potential of artificial intelligence? And, more importantly, do we even have the conditions to answer that question now? The more I think about it, the less convinced I become that we can. My impression is that it is still too early for us to understand what is happening. Perhaps we are trying to measure a historical transformation while we are still inside it, using conceptual tools that were built before this transformation began. And perhaps the greatest difficulty is precisely that: when we are living through an important change, we do not have access to the future that would allow us to understand the true scale of what is happening in the present.

This is a particularly personal reflection for me because my perception of AI has changed considerably over the past two or three years. I did not begin this journey believing that we were facing a new technological revolution. Quite the opposite. My first impression was much more pragmatic and, in retrospect, perhaps even excessively conservative. I saw large language models as a fairly sophisticated evolution of machine learning. An impressive, useful, and probably important technology, but still a technology. Something that would need to prove it could move beyond controlled demonstrations, interesting experiments, and isolated use cases and become sustainably integrated into the architecture of the systems we build in the real world.

This distinction made a lot of sense to me because of the way I view technology professionally. As a solutions architect, I am not particularly interested in whether a technology can produce an impressive demonstration. What interests me is whether it can survive the constraints of the real world. What is the cost? How does it behave under load? How is it observed? How is it governed? What happens when it fails? How is it integrated with existing systems? How do we deal with security, maintenance, versioning, auditing, and evolution? And, above all, how does all of this behave when we stop having a prototype and start having thousands or millions of users depending on that architecture?

My question about AI, therefore, was never simply “does this work?” The question was “does this scale?” And, at first, my answer was no.

I looked at so-called vibe coding with considerable skepticism. The idea of simply talking to an AI and producing entire applications seemed interesting for prototypes, experiments, and small projects, but I had difficulty imagining it working in environments where there are non-functional requirements, legacy systems, complex business rules, security, governance, observability, and decades of accumulated decisions. It seemed more like one of those moments when a new technology encounters a narrative attractive enough to generate a wave of enthusiasm before encountering the constraints of reality.

I imagined a relatively predictable cycle. Companies would experiment with the technology, teams would produce enormous amounts of code, the initial results would seem extraordinary and, a few months later, the problems that normally remain hidden in demonstrations would begin to appear. Code that was difficult to maintain, insufficient context, poor architectural decisions, excessive dependence on the model, security problems, unpredictable costs, lack of observability, hallucinations, and technical debt that would eventually come due. At some point, I imagined, executives would realize that it was not possible to simply replace engineering with automatic code generation.

This was not a position against AI. Quite the opposite. I was trying to evaluate it the way I would evaluate any other technology that could enter an enterprise architecture. A technology does not become important because it works well in a demonstration. It becomes important when we can incorporate it into real systems without the problems it introduces being greater than the problems it solves.

What I did not expect was for the technology itself to keep changing so rapidly while I was making this assessment.

And perhaps that was the first sign that I was asking the wrong question.

## When AI Began to Scale

What changed my perception was not simply the fact that the models got better. It was realizing that, around them, an architecture was emerging. MCP, A2A, agents, sub-agents, memory, tools, local models, self-hosting, SDKs, and libraries capable of abstracting different models and providers began to form an infrastructure layer that simply did not exist when I made my initial assessment. AI stopped being merely an API that receives a prompt and returns a response and began to occupy a space within the architecture of the systems themselves. For someone who works with solutions architecture, this difference is difficult to ignore. When I start seeing protocols, integration patterns, discovery mechanisms, context control, component composition, and different execution strategies emerging around a technology, it stops looking like just a tool and starts looking like a platform.

And perhaps “scale” is not the most precise word if we think only in terms of processing volume. What began to scale was the ability to incorporate AI into real systems. It began to become composable. We can choose different models for different problems, run them locally when it makes sense, switch providers, connect tools, delegate tasks to specialized agents, and insert intelligence at specific points in an existing architecture. This is very different from simply having a better model. A more intelligent model is a technological evolution. A new way of composing systems is an architectural change.

This difference also explains why my perception of AI's potential began to change. I no longer needed to imagine a company rebuilding its entire architecture around artificial intelligence. Quite the opposite. The most interesting possibility was precisely to add intelligence where it made sense. Deterministic code continues to be used where clear rules solve the problem better. Traditional systems continue to execute transactions, integrations, and processes that require predictability. Humans continue to make decisions when judgment, accountability, or contextual knowledge are necessary. But between these extremes lies an enormous amount of work that is not predictable enough to be automated by simple rules and, at the same time, not complex enough to justify human intervention at every step.

This is where agents begin to become interesting. They can interpret a request, gather information, choose tools, decompose a problem, delegate parts of the task to sub-agents, and evaluate the results before continuing. They do not need to replace the existing system. They can be added to it. An agent can sit between a human request and a set of existing services, using those services dynamically to reach a solution. The previous architecture continues to exist; what changes is that there is now a new layer capable of deciding how to use it.

That is why, the more I observe this evolution, the less convincing the narrative becomes that the great AI question is simply replacing programmers. Perhaps the more important change is something else. For decades, we built systems in which behavior had to be specified in advance in code. Now we are beginning to build systems in which part of the logic can be determined dynamically by artificial intelligence, based on context, objectives, and available tools.

AI begins to occupy an intermediate position between code and human decision-making. It is not simply traditional automation, because it can interpret situations that were not completely anticipated. Nor is it simply an artificial human, because it operates within the constraints and capabilities we define for it. It is a new decision-making layer within the architecture.

And perhaps it was precisely at that moment that I began to realize I had been evaluating the technology incorrectly. I was trying to determine whether AI could replace what we already did. What was beginning to happen was different: we were discovering what becomes possible when we place a new layer of intelligence on top of everything we have already built.

## The Architect and AI

Today I use artificial intelligence several times a day in my own work. This may be one of the things that has changed my perception of the technology the most, because I stopped observing it only from the outside and began incorporating it into my own work process. I do not use it to ask what I should do and blindly follow the answer. Most of the time, I do exactly the opposite. I present a problem, explain the context I consider relevant, explore alternatives, question assumptions, ask for counterarguments, compare architectures, discuss trade-offs, try to find weaknesses in a proposal, and use the conversation to discover things I might not have considered on my own.

In some cases, I start a discussion without even knowing exactly what the best solution is. This is perhaps the most interesting point. AI is not merely helping me execute a decision I have already made. It participates in exploring the space of possibilities before the decision exists. I can present an architecture and ask it to try to destroy it. I can propose a solution and ask what the arguments against it are. I can explain a constraint and ask for alternatives that preserve a particular requirement. I can present two seemingly equivalent options and explore the trade-offs of each. I can even reach a conclusion and then ask the AI to take the opposing position and try to prove me wrong.

This process is very different from simply asking an AI to write code. In that case, productivity is relatively easy to measure: before, it took me a few hours to produce a certain amount of code; now it might take a few minutes. But when I use AI as a reasoning partner, the gain is harder to quantify. Perhaps the greatest benefit is not producing things faster, but being able to think about a problem more broadly. The cost of exploring a hypothesis drops drastically. The cost of asking for a second opinion practically disappears. The cost of comparing alternatives also decreases. And this changes the very number of possibilities I can consider before making a decision.

There is a huge difference between delegating a decision and increasing someone's capacity to make a decision. I am not handing responsibility for the architecture over to AI. I am using AI to increase my ability to analyze the architecture. The decision remains mine, including because I am the one who knows certain organizational constraints, internal policies, system history, risks involved, and consequences that may not be present in the context provided to the model. But now I have a tool capable of participating in the discussion practically in real time.

This also changes the relationship between experience and AI. I do not think an experienced architect is simply someone who knows many answers. Experience, to a large extent, means recognizing patterns, identifying risks, knowing which questions need to be asked, and understanding the trade-offs hidden behind an apparently simple decision. AI can be extremely useful precisely because it can generate hypotheses, alternatives, and questions at a speed that a human alone would hardly be able to keep up with. But someone still needs to know which of those hypotheses are relevant, which constraints were ignored, and which consequences are not being considered.

That is why I have difficulty with the idea that AI will simply replace humans in the most complex technical jobs. There is a fundamental difference between executing an instruction and deciding which instruction should exist. A traditional program executes predefined logic. An agent can interpret context and make decisions within a space of possibilities that was not completely specified in advance. But that still does not mean it knows which problem it should be solving or that it can determine on its own what all the relevant constraints of that problem are.

This is precisely why I like to think of AI as an intermediate layer between code and human decision-making. It can handle a degree of ambiguity that would be unfeasible for conventional automation, but it still needs context, objectives, constraints, and validation mechanisms to produce reliable results.

This characteristic also explains why I do not believe that simply putting an AI inside a company will immediately transform its productivity. The model can be extremely capable and still produce a completely inappropriate solution if given a poorly defined problem. If the context is wrong, the answer can be wrong. If the requirements are incomplete, the solution can be technically excellent at solving the wrong problem. If the constraints are unknown, the agent can choose an alternative that looks locally optimal and is terrible for the system as a whole.

This is something an architect should recognize immediately, because it is not a problem unique to AI. It is a classic engineering problem. Garbage in, garbage out still exists. The difference is that we now have a machine capable of producing an extremely convincing answer even when the input specification is poor.

And perhaps this is one of the things that interests me most about this transformation. The better AI becomes at executing, the more important it becomes to know how to specify. The cheaper it becomes to produce code, the more important it becomes to know how to define the problem. The more autonomy we give agents, the more we need to clearly establish their limits, objectives, context, and success criteria.

Perhaps AI is not reducing the importance of the architect. Perhaps it is changing what we expect from one.

The architect may stop being someone who needs to know all the answers and become someone who can correctly structure the problem, define the constraints, evaluate alternatives, and build a system in which humans and agents can work together. Artificial intelligence can dramatically increase the capacity for exploration, but we still need someone who knows which space should be explored.

And perhaps that is one of the reasons why, after starting to use AI in this way, it became increasingly difficult for me to treat it merely as a productivity tool. A tool that writes code is useful. A tool that participates in the process by which I decide what should be built is something else.

In this second scenario, AI is not merely producing artifacts. It is entering the engineering process itself.

## Maybe We Need More Engineering, Not Less

There is a recurring idea that increasingly autonomous agents will eventually make traditional development processes obsolete. I suspect the opposite may happen. The more autonomy we give an AI, the more we need to structure what used to remain implicit. A traditional system can hide an enormous amount of knowledge inside the code and the people who operate it. An agentic system needs to make this knowledge explicit, because an agent does not know what has not been placed in its context.

A requirements document, for example, takes on even greater importance in an agent-based architecture. It stops being merely a reference for the development team and begins to function as part of the context required for the AI itself to make decisions. An agent needs to know the objective it must achieve, the constraints it must respect, the business rules involved, the criteria that determine whether the result is correct, and the limits within which it can operate. If this information exists only in people's heads or is distributed fragmentarily across conversations, meetings, tickets, and messages, there is no reliable representation of the problem that can be used by the agent. The more autonomy we give AI, the more important it becomes to transform tacit knowledge into explicit, structured, and versioned knowledge.

For decades, we were able to compensate for poor specifications because human beings functioned as a kind of runtime for the organization's tacit knowledge. A developer knew that a certain table could not be changed that way because someone had explained it six months earlier. An architect knew why a particular integration existed because they had participated in the original project. An analyst knew about a business exception that had never been documented. The system worked because people continuously filled in the gaps left by the documentation.

When we put agents into this process, these gaps begin to appear in a different way. AI may infer what should probably happen, but a plausible inference is not necessarily a business rule. And the greater the agent's autonomy, the more dangerous it becomes to depend on that inference. The more autonomous AI becomes, the less we can depend on knowledge that exists only in people's heads.

This does not mean we need to abandon the processes we have developed so far. Perhaps we can finally use them in the way they were designed. PDCA, for example, can be understood not as a bureaucratic methodology, but as a control mechanism for a process that needs to continuously plan, execute, check, and correct. The arrival of agents does not eliminate this cycle. It allows us to imagine that different stages can be executed or assisted by specialized agents.

We can imagine, for example, a deterministic workflow in which the first step is an agent responsible for Plan. It receives the requirements document, the architectural context, and the constraints of the problem and produces an execution plan. But this agent does not need to work alone. It can use specialized sub-agents to analyze documentation, consult existing systems, evaluate dependencies, research architectural alternatives, or identify risks. The result is not simply a model response, but a plan produced within a defined process and subject to validation criteria.

The second step would be Do. An execution agent receives the approved plan and coordinates its implementation. Again, it can use sub-agents for specific tasks. One can modify code, another can generate tests, another can update documentation, and another can perform security checks. The main agent does not necessarily need to know how to perform all these tasks directly. It needs to know how to decompose the work and coordinate the available capabilities.

Then we have Check. Here, an agent with a completely different responsibility comes in. Its objective is not to produce the solution, but to verify that what was produced meets what was specified. It can compare the result with the requirements, run tests, analyze metrics, verify contracts, assess whether the architecture was respected, and look for inconsistencies. It is important that this stage not simply be a continuation of the execution agent, because there is value precisely in separating who produces from who verifies.

Finally, we have Act. An agent analyzes the verification results and determines what to do next. If everything is within the defined criteria, the process can proceed. If there are deviations, the flow can return to planning with new information. If the problem falls within a known set of exceptions, a specialized sub-agent can be triggered. If the situation falls outside the defined boundaries, the process can be stopped and handed over to a human.

The important point here is that we are not necessarily talking about four completely autonomous agents freely talking to one another. We can have a deterministic workflow controlling the process, with agents executing specific tasks within each stage. The orchestration can be deterministic even if the internal execution is probabilistic. This distinction seems fundamental to me for building systems that are truly governable.

OODA can enter an even more interesting layer, especially within the agent responsible for planning. Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. The agent observes the current state of the problem, gathers context, identifies what has changed, and determines what information is available. It then orients itself by relating that information to requirements, business rules, architectural constraints, and available knowledge. It then decides which strategy should be used and, finally, executes or delegates the necessary actions.

This can happen in cycles. During observation, the planning agent may discover that certain information is unavailable. Instead of simply inventing an answer, it can trigger an investigation sub-agent. That sub-agent can consult documentation, systems, repositories, or other sources of context. When the information returns, the agent reorients itself and decides again. The process stops being a simple linear sequence of prompts and becomes a controlled cycle of perception, interpretation, decision, and action.

And here an architecture emerges that I find particularly interesting. We can have an external deterministic flow responsible for ensuring that the correct stages happen in the correct order, while within each stage we can have agents and sub-agents using probabilistic reasoning to solve problems that would be difficult to express as traditional rules. The workflow defines the process. The agents solve the problems within that process.

This preserves something I consider essential in enterprise systems: predictability at the level where it is needed. We do not need to accept that the entire system is a probabilistic black box simply because some of its parts use artificial intelligence. We can clearly define when the agent may act, which tools it may use, which information it may access, which criteria it must meet, and in which situations it must stop execution.

The very idea of sub-agents takes on another meaning in this context. An agent does not need to be a monolithic entity that knows how to do everything. We can build specialists for requirements analysis, security, architecture, code, testing, documentation, observability, or validation. The agent responsible for a given stage coordinates these specialists as needed. This creates a decision hierarchy similar to what we already find in human organizations, but with computational capabilities being dynamically invoked.

At the limit, we can imagine a request entering this system as an unresolved problem. The workflow identifies that it belongs to a certain class of request. The planning agent uses OODA to understand the problem and builds a plan. Sub-agents help gather context and evaluate alternatives. The plan proceeds to execution. The execution agent coordinates other specialists. The result goes through independent validation. The control agent evaluates the results and decides whether the cycle ends, needs to be repeated, or requires a human to take over.

This is not the end of engineering processes. It is their transformation into executable systems.

Perhaps this is one of the most interesting changes AI can bring. For a long time, processes such as requirements, architecture, development, testing, documentation, and validation existed primarily as human activities connected by tools. Now we can begin transforming these processes into structures that humans and agents can execute together.

Perhaps the future is not an organization without processes. Perhaps it is an organization in which processes are so well structured that humans and agents can participate in them without having to choose between complete automation and human intervention at every stage.

And, in this scenario, AI does not eliminate engineering. It makes engineering even more explicit.

## When AI Needs to Ask

There is an important difference between an AI that answers questions and an agent responsible for executing an objective. In the first case, the interaction begins with a request sufficiently defined by the user. In the second, the agent itself needs to determine what information, decisions, and resources are required to complete the task. This means that, at some point, it will inevitably encounter situations in which the available context is not sufficient. The question, then, is not only how to make the model reason better about what it knows, but how to allow it to obtain what it does not yet know.

In corporate processes, much of the knowledge required for a decision is not necessarily contained in documents or systems. It may be distributed among different departments, specialists, and owners of specific processes. An agent analyzing an architectural change, for example, may have access to technical documentation and data from the systems involved, but still need to confirm a business rule with the person responsible for a particular product. Instead of turning this absence into an assumption, the agent could identify the dependency, locate the appropriate stakeholder, and request the necessary information. After receiving the answer, the execution context would be updated and the process could continue.

This introduces an important characteristic into agent architecture: the ability to initiate interactions. The agent stops being merely a component that receives external stimuli and starts actively participating in the process. It can query systems, execute tools, delegate activities, and also request information or decisions from people. Human communication becomes another tool available for executing the objective, rather than merely the mechanism through which the user initiates a conversation with the AI.

This change is particularly relevant because real-world processes are rarely completely specified at the moment they begin. Traditional systems deal with this through forms, validations, and previously coded business rules. When an unforeseen condition appears, the process is normally interrupted and handed over to a person. An agent can handle this situation more dynamically, identifying which dependency has emerged and seeking the elements necessary to resolve it. The difference is not merely in automating a step that used to be human, but in allowing the system itself to discover which additional steps are necessary.

This also changes the way we think about hallucination. In certain situations, the problem is not that the model chose the wrong answer among several known possibilities. The problem is that none of the available information allows a reliable conclusion to be reached. In that case, increasing generation capability does not solve the issue. The agent needs to recognize that the context is insufficient and change strategy. Instead of producing a conclusion, it can seek an additional source, request confirmation, or escalate a decision to whoever has authority over it.

The result is an architecture in which execution is not necessarily linear. An agent can start a task, discover a dependency, temporarily interrupt its execution, request information, receive the response, reassess the context, and then continue. This behavior brings agentic systems closer to the way complex processes actually work, in which decisions often depend on information that only becomes relevant during execution itself.

There is also an important organizational consequence. If agents can interact directly with stakeholders, it will be necessary to represent within the architecture not only systems and APIs, but also responsibilities and sources of knowledge. The agent needs to know, for example, who is responsible for a given process, which area can validate a particular decision, and in which situations human approval is mandatory. This creates a kind of coordination layer between people, systems, and agents, in which knowledge about the organization becomes as important as knowledge about its systems.

In this model, autonomy does not mean eliminating human participation. It means using human participation more precisely. The agent executes what it can automate, seeks information wherever it is available, and involves a person only when their participation is necessary. Instead of placing the human at the beginning or end of every operation, the human appears within the flow precisely at the point where their knowledge or authority is needed.

This may be one of the most important differences between an AI that simply responds and an AI that actually participates in a process. The agent does not need to have all the answers available in advance. It needs to be capable of driving the process until it finds the answers required. When the information is in a system, it queries the system. When it is in a document, it consults the document. When it is with another agent, it delegates. When it is with a person, it asks. Intelligence, in this case, is not only the ability to generate an answer, but the ability to determine the next step required for a decision to be made based on sufficient information.

## MCP Does Not Need to Be a New OpenAPI

This same logic changes the way I see protocols such as MCP. It is easy to think of it as a kind of OpenAPI for agents, a standardized way of exposing tools and resources so that a model can use them. This comparison is useful for understanding the concept, but it may be too limited to represent where this could go. A traditional API describes capabilities that were previously defined by whoever built the system. It essentially answers the question: what operations does this system offer? An agent-oriented environment can answer a different question: what can be discovered, investigated, and accomplished using the resources this agent has access to?

This brings us back to the previous chapter and to the reason processes such as PDCA exist. Humans spent centuries creating processes because they realized they did not need to know all possible situations in advance to establish a consistent way of dealing with problems. PDCA is interesting precisely because of this. It does not directly answer a specific question. It defines a structure for discovering the answer. Faced with a problem, we can plan, execute, check the result, and act on what we learn. If the result is unsatisfactory, the cycle begins again with more information. It is a way of transforming an unknown problem into a sequence of investigation, action, and learning.

An agentic system can do exactly that.

Imagine a request that does not directly correspond to any operation available in an API. In a traditional architecture, someone would need to know the systems involved, discover which endpoints exist, determine the order of the calls, interpret the results, and manually construct the response. In an agent-oriented architecture, we can provide the system with access to the necessary capabilities and allow the investigation process to be built dynamically. The agent receives the problem, identifies what it needs to discover, queries the available tools, gathers information, evaluates the results, decides which steps should be executed next, and continues until it reaches the criteria defined for that request.

The point is not to allow AI to do anything without control. Quite the opposite. The agent needs to operate within a well-defined process and boundaries. The workflow can remain deterministic. The agent can have specific permissions. Each tool can have clear contracts. Some actions can require human approval. What changes is that we do not need to program in advance all the possible sequences for using those tools.

This is where I see an important difference between a traditional API and an MCP server designed for agents. An API normally exposes functions. An MCP can expose capabilities. The difference may seem semantic, but it can be quite profound. A function tells the consumer what it can call. A capability can provide the agent with the means to discover how to solve a particular problem.

This also allows us to imagine an MCP that is not simply a copy of an OpenAPI. Instead of exposing hundreds of endpoints directly, it could provide query resources, context, specialized tools, and investigation mechanisms. The agent would not necessarily need to know in advance which operation to call. It could start with a question, discover which information is missing, consult a source, interpret the result, and decide which capability to use next.

In this model, a human could also use the same surface. The difference would simply be who is driving the investigation. A professional could query the available resources and manually decide which steps to execute. An agent could do the same thing automatically, following the process defined by the architecture. This creates a kind of common layer between humans and machines, in which both can interact with the same system capabilities, each using its own decision-making mechanism.

And this is where the idea of PDCA becomes even more interesting. We can imagine an agent responsible for a request receiving a problem without a predefined execution sequence. It begins with planning, identifies which information it needs to obtain and which actions will probably be necessary. It then executes the available actions through the tools exposed by MCP. Next, it checks the results and compares the current state with the objective defined in the requirements. If the result is not sufficient, the process itself determines that a new iteration is necessary. The agent returns to planning with a richer context and continues the investigation.

In this sense, MCP does not need to know how to answer the question the user asked. It needs to provide the agent with the means to discover the answer.

This is an important paradigm shift. In traditional systems, we try to anticipate possible paths and turn them into code. In an agentic system, we can define capabilities, limits, contracts, and processes, leaving the concrete sequence of use to be determined at runtime based on context.

Perhaps this is one of the reasons protocols such as MCP are potentially more important than they appear. The value is not merely in allowing a model to call a tool. The value is in creating an interface between an intelligence capable of making contextual decisions and a set of computational capabilities that previously had to be explicitly chained by a programmer.

This also helps explain why the boundary between API, tool, agent, and system is becoming less clear. An API provides operations. A tool provides a capability. An agent decides when and how to use those capabilities. A process determines which limits and criteria should govern that decision. And when all these elements are combined, we begin to have something that looks less like a traditional application and more like a system capable of investigating and solving problems.

Perhaps that is precisely why we have created processes throughout the history of engineering. Not to predict every problem that would arise, but to create a systematic way of dealing with problems we do not yet know.

The difference now is that we can begin delegating part of this process to a machine.

## The Problem Few People Seem to Take Seriously

There is, however, a problem that I consider particularly underestimated in this evolution: social engineering applied to agentic systems. When an AI merely answers a question, manipulating its context can result in a wrong answer. When that same AI gains tools, access to documents, credentials, APIs, and corporate systems, the situation changes completely. The problem is no longer merely the possibility that the AI may be wrong; it becomes the possibility that someone can induce the AI to do something it should not do.

This is a different kind of problem from traditional vulnerabilities. In a conventional system, we normally think about malformed inputs, authentication failures, privilege escalation, improper code execution, or exploitation of some condition not anticipated by the developer. In a system based on language models, there is an additional attack surface: the context itself used to make decisions. Information can be technically valid and still have been constructed to influence the agent's behavior. A document can contain an instruction that appears to be part of the content but was written to manipulate the model. A page consulted by an agent can attempt to direct it toward a particular action. A message received from another system can contain instructions that should not be treated as commands.

This is particularly dangerous because the agent cannot necessarily distinguish, on its own, between information and instruction. For a human, there is a relatively intuitive difference between reading a document that describes a procedure and receiving an order to execute it. For a language model, both can appear as information within the same context. If the architecture does not clearly establish the origin, authority, and scope of each piece of information, we are delegating to the model itself a security decision that it may not be able to make reliably.

The problem becomes even more serious when we enter multi-tenant environments. A traditional application may have very strict mechanisms to ensure that a user from one tenant can never query data belonging to another. This isolation needs to continue to exist when we introduce agents. We cannot simply trust the model to correctly interpret that a particular document, record, or tool belongs to client A and cannot be used to answer a request from client B. If there is an authorization boundary between tenants, it needs to be enforced by the architecture and by the data sources themselves, not merely described in a prompt.

Imagine an agentic system used by different customers of the same platform. An agent receives a request, queries a knowledge base, calls some tools, and assembles a response. If the context available to that agent contains information from multiple tenants, a fundamental question immediately arises: who authorized the agent to see each piece of that information? And more importantly, who guarantees that information belonging to one tenant will not be used to produce a response intended for another?

This problem can appear in ways that are much less obvious than a simple database leak. A search without the appropriate filter can return documents from another customer. A tool can receive a valid identifier but fail to validate which tenant it belongs to. An agent can use information obtained in a previous step to answer a later request without correctly preserving the authorization context. Shared memory can store information from different users and allow an agent to use it in a situation for which it was not authorized. Even an apparently harmless tool can become a vector for unauthorized access if its authorization is based only on what the agent claims it is doing.

This reinforces a distinction I consider fundamental: context is not authorization. The fact that information is available to the model does not mean it may be used. The fact that a tool is registered in the system does not mean the agent can execute it in that context. The fact that an agent knows a particular identifier does not mean it has permission to query that resource. And the fact that an instruction appears inside a document does not mean it has authority over the system's behavior.

We therefore need to treat identity, tenant, context, capability, and authority as distinct concepts. The system needs to know who is making the request, on behalf of which tenant, which resources that identity can access, which tools can be used in that context, and which actions can be executed. This information needs to follow the execution flow. We cannot authenticate the user at the entry point, hand a huge context to the agent, and expect it to preserve all security boundaries on its own throughout execution.

This also changes how we should think about permissions. The agent can decide which tool to use, but it should not be able to create a permission it does not have. It can identify that it needs to query a particular resource, but the authorization layer must verify that the resource belongs to the correct tenant and that the identity has access. It can propose an operation, but execution needs to pass through a policy independent of the model. The agent can be responsible for the operational decision, but the architecture remains responsible for authorization.

The principle of least privilege becomes even more important in this scenario. An agent responsible for querying information should have only the permissions necessary for querying. An agent capable of preparing an operation should not automatically be able to execute it. An agent working for a particular tenant should operate within a context explicitly associated with that tenant. And tools that allow access to sensitive data or operations should validate their own authorization rather than trusting that the agent has already performed that verification correctly.

There is also a propagation problem. A mistake made by a human may affect a limited amount of data or a limited number of operations. An agent can repeat an incorrect decision at a much larger scale. If there is an authorization flaw in a tool used by an agentic system, the model may discover that capability and use it repeatedly. Automation increases system efficiency, but it also increases the speed at which a failure can propagate.

For this reason, security in agentic systems needs to be designed in layers. Identity must be verifiable. The tenant must follow the request. Data access must be filtered at the data layer itself. Tools must enforce authorization independently of the model. Sensitive actions must have specific policies. Context needs to have a known origin and scope. Operations need to be auditable. And higher-risk actions may require human approval or another control mechanism before being executed.

Social engineering makes all of this even more relevant. If someone can insert a malicious instruction into a document, page, message, or any other source the agent consults, that information may attempt to alter the system's behavior. The attack does not necessarily need to exploit a traditional vulnerability. It can try to convince the agent to query a different tool, reveal information, ignore a restriction, or execute an operation for which it should not have authorization.

That is why I consider it dangerous to put security inside the model itself. We can instruct an agent never to access data from another tenant, never to reveal confidential information, or never to execute certain operations. These instructions are useful, but they cannot be the last line of defense. If a security boundary matters, it needs to be enforced by a layer that does not depend on the probabilistic interpretation of the model.

The architecture needs to assume that the agent can make mistakes. It needs to assume that context can be manipulated. It needs to assume that a tool can be used unexpectedly. It needs to assume that someone will try to push the system beyond its limits. And, above all, it needs to ensure that a model error does not automatically become a security breach.

This leads us to a distinction that may be essential for the future of agentic systems: autonomy is not authority. An agent can have autonomy to decide how to solve a problem without having the authority to access every resource necessary to solve it. It can choose among available tools without being able to change its own permissions. It can plan a complex operation without being authorized to execute every step of that operation.

Ultimately, we want agents to be capable of making decisions that we cannot completely anticipate. But we need to ensure that those decisions occur within boundaries that we can anticipate. The solution is not to eliminate autonomy. It is to separate autonomy from authority.

Intelligence can be probabilistic. The tenant cannot be. Authorization cannot be. Security boundaries cannot be.

## Maybe AI Will Force Us to Take Engineering More Seriously

There is another consequence that I find particularly interesting for software engineering. For a long time, speed and quality were treated as though they were necessarily in conflict. At certain points in development, activities that did not directly produce software ended up receiving less attention because they seemed to delay delivery. Documentation could be updated later. Some tests could wait until the next stage. An architectural decision could be refined once the system was more mature. Requirements could be adjusted during implementation. Agile processes, which were born precisely as an attempt to bring development closer to value generation, were often interpreted in an excessively operational way, as though the primary objective of a sprint were simply to produce the greatest possible number of deliverables before it ended.

I do not think this is necessarily a problem with any particular methodology. It is a much older tendency in engineering: when the cost of doing something is high, we are encouraged to reduce the work required to produce it. The problem is that some activities that appear secondary exist precisely to reduce future costs. Documentation reduces maintenance costs. Tests reduce the cost of regressions. Architecture reduces the cost of structural changes. Requirements reduce the cost of building the wrong thing. Governance reduces the cost of poor decisions. When these elements are treated merely as overhead, we are saving in the present and creating a debt that will be paid later.

The arrival of AI may change this equation in a rather interesting way. If agents can produce code, tests, documentation, diagrams, analyses, and other artifacts in a fraction of the time a human would take, perhaps the problem will no longer be the cost of producing these artifacts. The problem will become ensuring that we are producing the right artifacts, based on the right information, within the right constraints.

This puts requirements back at the center of architecture. If an agent needs to receive enough context to implement a solution, the requirements document stops being merely a reference for developers. It becomes an operational specification for humans and machines. Acceptance criteria stop being merely a way to determine whether a story can be considered complete. They can become criteria used by verification agents. Architectural documentation stops being merely reference material and begins to provide context for agents that need to make decisions about that system. Business rules stop being knowledge distributed among people and need to have an explicit representation that can be consulted during execution.

In this scenario, documentation no longer competes directly with speed. AI itself can help produce, update, and validate it. An agent can generate documentation from changes in the code. Another can compare the implementation with the requirements. Another can identify inconsistencies between documentation and observed behavior. We can even imagine a process in which a change is not considered complete until code, tests, documentation, and acceptance criteria are consistent with one another.

This also changes the meaning of a sprint. If the cost of implementing a feature decreases drastically, perhaps we can finally separate speed from haste. A sprint can once again be a controlled cycle of planning, implementation, validation, and learning, in which the objective is to deliver verifiable value rather than simply maximize the amount of code produced. AI can accelerate each of these stages, but it does not eliminate the need for them to exist.

And this is particularly interesting when we connect this discussion to PDCA. An agent-assisted development system can plan a change, execute it, verify whether the result meets the requirements, and act on the deviations found. The process can be repeated until the defined criteria are satisfied or until the system identifies that the decision needs to be escalated to a human. The sprint stops being merely a production window and becomes a unit of learning and delivery within a much more structured process.

The major change is that the marginal cost of implementation may no longer be the primary bottleneck in software engineering. If producing code becomes extremely cheap, writing code stops being the hardest part of the job. The problem becomes understanding what should be built, correctly defining the constraints, choosing an appropriate architecture, providing sufficient context to the agents, and verifying that the result actually solves the original problem. We can generate ten different implementations in a few minutes, but we still need to know which one should exist.

This can increase, rather than decrease, the importance of engineering. For years, part of the discussion around agility was about removing processes that seemed to hinder speed. Now AI itself can make documentation, testing, analysis, and validation cheap enough that they no longer need to compete with implementation speed. If an AI can produce these artifacts almost as quickly as it produces code, we no longer need to choose between delivering quickly and doing proper engineering.

The true productivity gain from AI may not simply be producing software faster. It may be making it economically viable to consistently do all the things we know good engineering should do.

## The Data Problem

There is another issue that makes me skeptical of some of the most optimistic predictions about the future of AI: the quality of the data that will be available to future models. The cheaper it becomes to produce text, images, videos, code, and other content, the greater the amount of material produced by artificial intelligence systems is likely to become. If an increasing share of available content is generated by previous models, future models may, to some extent, be trained on synthetic data derived from other AIs. This creates an interesting risk: errors, biases, simplifications, and artificial patterns from one generation may be reproduced by the next and, eventually, amplified in a data cycle increasingly distant from original content.

This does not mean that training models on synthetic data is necessarily bad. Synthetic data can be extremely useful when it is produced and validated in a controlled manner. Models can generate examples that would be expensive or impossible to obtain directly, simulated environments can produce enormous quantities of data, and curation processes can select high-quality information. The problem arises when we stop distinguishing between data produced by humans, deliberately generated synthetic data, and content simply recycled by previous models.

Perhaps this is one of the paradoxes of AI's evolution. The better we become at producing information artificially, the more valuable information with a trustworthy origin becomes. Proprietary data, historical records, real-world experiments, specialized knowledge, and content produced by people may become strategic resources precisely because they cannot easily be replaced by another generation of synthetic content.

AI needs data to evolve, but an abundance of data does not guarantee quality. At some point, the more important question may cease to be how much content we can produce and become how much content actually deserves to be used as knowledge.

## What If All of This Is a Bubble?

As much as my perception has changed, I still find it difficult to accept some of the more extreme predictions about artificial intelligence. Perhaps there is a gigantic bubble around the technology. Perhaps some valuations are completely disconnected from the real economic capacity of the products being built. Perhaps companies that seem inevitable today will disappear over the next few years, just as happened in practically every major wave of technological innovation. Perhaps some of the most ambitious promises about autonomous agents, artificial general intelligence, and the complete transformation of the labor market will never materialize.

And this does not necessarily contradict the possibility that we are facing a profound technological transformation. These are two different questions. One is how much a technology can transform society. The other is how much we are willing to pay today for the companies trying to build it. The market can be completely wrong about the value of certain companies and, at the same time, be right about the historical importance of the technology those companies helped develop.

The history of the internet demonstrates this quite clearly. The dot-com bubble burst, countless companies disappeared, investors lost enormous amounts of money, and many of the predictions made during that period proved absurdly optimistic. But it would be difficult to argue today that the internet was merely a farce because part of the market was in a bubble. The speculation was wrong in many cases. The technology was not.

Perhaps something similar is happening now. We may be overestimating the value of certain companies, underestimating infrastructure costs, exaggerating the speed at which AI will be incorporated into the market, or creating expectations that the technology will not be able to meet. None of this necessarily means that the models, agents, and systems being built are irrelevant.

There is, in fact, a curious characteristic of major technological transformations: they often seem exaggerated precisely when they begin to happen. Enthusiasm produces absurd predictions, too much capital is directed toward bad projects, and an enormous number of companies try to capture the same opportunity. When reality finally imposes its limits, much of it disappears. But the elements that actually work remain and eventually become infrastructure.

It is therefore entirely possible that a significant portion of the AI market is in a bubble. This does not strike me as a particularly frightening conclusion. Bubbles burst. Companies fail. Valuations fall. Investors reassess their expectations. What interests me more is discovering what remains afterward.

If AI continues to evolve, become incorporated into software architectures, reduce the cost of certain activities, and increase human capacity to solve problems, it matters little whether some of the companies dominating the headlines today will exist ten years from now. The technology can outlive the businesses built around it.

A technology can be revolutionary and simultaneously be involved in a speculative bubble. One does not invalidate the other.

Perhaps the true test of AI is not discovering whether we are in a bubble. It is discovering what will continue to exist when it bursts.

## The Problem of Recognizing a Change of Era

There is one final question that interests me even more than all the others. Romans, Byzantines, and French people lived through moments that we now classify as the ends or ruptures of eras, but none of them could know that they were living through a historical transition. A Roman could not know that they were at the end of Antiquity. A French person in 1788 could not know that, a few years later, historians would treat that period as a rupture. Eras are retrospective constructions. We can only clearly see a historical change because we know what came afterward.

Perhaps we are trying to do something impossible with AI: determine now whether we are living through a change of era without possessing the future knowledge necessary to recognize it. It may be that, twenty years from now, we will look back at this period and consider that everything began here. It may be that we see only a gigantic technological bubble. It may also be that we discover that the technology was deeply transformative, but that almost all predictions about its speed, impacts, and winners were wrong. These possibilities are not necessarily mutually exclusive. A historical transformation can happen while the market exaggerates its scope, companies fail, and specific predictions prove wrong.

My position today is much less definitive than it was a few years ago. I remain skeptical of very specific predictions about AGI, widespread worker replacement, and completely autonomous systems. An impressive model demonstration is not enough to prove an economic or social transformation. At the same time, I can no longer look at AI and see merely more sophisticated machine learning. As a solutions architect, I have seen a technology that initially seemed unable to scale begin to acquire protocols, standards, agents, local models, integration mechanisms, and an architecture capable of progressively incorporating it into the systems we have already built. And, in a way, I myself am already part of this transformation. Today I use an AI several times a day to think about technical problems, explore alternatives, question decisions, and find the best possible solution to that problem at that moment. Not as an authority that decides for me, but as a collaborator that has expanded the way I work.

I started by looking at AI and asking whether it could scale. Today I am trying to discover how far it can scale. Perhaps it is still too early to know whether we are witnessing the beginning of a new era, a bubble that will be remembered as a historical exaggeration, or some combination of the two. What has already changed, at least for me, is that I stopped asking whether AI is important and started trying to understand the scale of the change it can produce.
