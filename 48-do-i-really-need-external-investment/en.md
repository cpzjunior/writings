# Do I Really Need External Investment?

_Bootstrapping, external capital, and the cost of building a company that I may not want to run._

**Summary:** When I think about entrepreneurship, I see the choice between bootstrapping and external investment less as a financial decision and more as a decision about the kind of company I want to build. Capital can accelerate execution and help take advantage of opportunities, but it also brings dilution, expectations, and pressure for growth that I may not want. On the other hand, not raising capital also has a cost, because I may lose speed and ground to competitors. Building a company also does not necessarily mean running it forever: I can sell it and move on to another project. In the end, before deciding how much money to raise, I need to understand what future I want to finance and whether I am willing to build the company that this capital will require.

---

When I think about entrepreneurship, one of the first decisions that comes up is how to finance the company I am trying to build. How much money should I put in myself? When should I look for investors? How long should I try to grow using the business's own revenue? And is there a point when raising capital stops being an option and becomes a necessity?

In the startup ecosystem, the answer often seems to have already been given. Find an opportunity, validate the idea, raise a round, hire people, accelerate the product, and pursue the next round. There is almost a natural sequence between finding a good opportunity and looking for money to turn it into a larger company.

But when I stop to think about this decision from the perspective of the person building the company, the question seems less obvious. Money can allow a company to hire earlier, develop faster, enter new markets, and take advantage of an opportunity that may not exist a few years from now. It can also buy time, and time may be exactly the resource a company lacks to survive.

At the same time, putting more money into a company does not necessarily mean that it will grow at the same rate. There are markets that still need to mature, products that need to be discovered by consumers, and hypotheses that need time to be tested. The main constraint on a company is not always cash.

And there is an even more personal dimension to this decision. The money that finances a company does not exist in isolation. It comes with choices about pace, size, risk, control, and the future. Depending on where the money comes from, other people become part of the company's story and, in some way, part of the story of the person who created it.

That is how I began to see the choice between bootstrapping and external investment less as a purely financial decision and more as a decision about what kind of company I am willing to build.

Because perhaps the hardest question is not how much money I can raise, but what happens after I raise it.

## The Type of Investment Changes the Relationship with the Company

When the money is mine, the decisions are mine. That has enormous value, but it also has a cost. With bootstrapping, the financial risk is much more concentrated on the founder. The capital invested is my own, the opportunity cost is my own, and if the company fails, the loss may represent years of accumulated wealth. Depending on the company's stage, we are not just talking about losing the money put into the business, but losing the ability to do other things with that money.

That does not mean bootstrapping is an easy option. Quite the opposite. The need to generate revenue, pay salaries, maintain healthy cash flow, and finance the next step can create enormous pressure. Autonomy exists, but it comes with the responsibility of sustaining the company with resources that could be used elsewhere in life.

External capital changes this equation. Part of the financial risk becomes shared. The company gains resources to hire, develop, test, sell, and survive longer without immediately depending on its own cash generation.

But there is not just one form of external capital. An angel investor, a venture capital fund, a strategic investor, or a debt structure can put money into the same company and still establish completely different relationships with it. The investment horizon may change, as may the expected return, the degree of participation in decisions, the governance structure, and the pressure for growth.

That is why the cost of a fundraising round is not just the percentage of the company I give up. It is also the conditions of the relationship I begin to build with the people who put money into it. An investor can be an excellent partner. They can bring experience, connections, customers, hiring capabilities, and even help the company make better decisions. But for that to happen, there is an important condition: interests need to be reasonably aligned.

Before raising capital, I can say that I want to grow. After raising it, depending on the type of investment and the expectations assumed, that can begin to turn into an obligation to grow.

The difference may seem small in the wording, but it is enormous in practice. When I choose external capital, I am not just choosing where the money to finance the company will come from. I am also choosing with whom I will share the risk, which decisions will no longer be exclusively mine, and, to some extent, what future I will need to build for that investment to make sense.

## Capital Injection Does Not Create a Market

There is another premise that seems dangerous to me: the idea that a good opportunity should be accelerated as soon as it appears. Not always. Some ideas need time to mature. Technology needs to become cheaper, the experience needs to improve, the infrastructure needs to exist, or people simply need to get used to a new way of doing something.

I believe in technology. But I also believe that technology adoption has its own pace. Augmented reality is a good example. Google tried to bring smart glasses to market more than a decade ago with Google Glass. The technology was impressive, but that was not enough to turn glasses into a consumer category at that time.

Today, Meta is taking a much more promising approach with smart glasses. The technology is more mature, the experience is more natural, and the proposition is closer to something people can incorporate into their daily lives. Even so, it may be that mass adoption will only really happen a few years from now. That does not mean Meta is failing. On the contrary. It may be precisely an example of much better execution in a market that is still maturing.

And that is where I see an important distinction: there is a difference between execution speed and adoption speed. A capital injection can greatly increase the first. It does not necessarily increase the second.

If a company's bottleneck is capacity, money can be transformative. If I have more customers than I can serve, hiring more people solves the problem. If I have demand and cannot produce enough, a capital injection can unlock production. If there is a short competitive window, money may be exactly what allows me to occupy it before another company does.

But if the bottleneck is discovering whether demand exists, hiring more people does not replace that discovery. It may even make the problem worse.

A company can raise capital, hire dozens of people, increase its marketing budget, and build an entire structure before having sufficient evidence that the market actually wants what it is offering. In that case, the capital injection is not accelerating a validated company. It is accelerating a hypothesis.

That is why, before asking how much money I need to put into the company, perhaps I should ask what bottleneck I am trying to solve. If the bottleneck is capital, then I need to find a way to finance that growth. If the bottleneck is learning, perhaps I need something else.

## The Risk of Arriving Late

The problem is that not raising capital is also a decision. A company may need capital precisely because someone else is competing for the same space. The market may be maturing, but that does not mean it will wait.

A competitor can raise money, hire the best people, invest in distribution, close strategic customers, and build a position that later becomes difficult to recover. In certain markets, network effects, scale, or a technological window can make speed a decisive competitive advantage.

In this context, not raising capital is also a bet. I may preserve a larger share of the company and, a few years later, discover that those 100% are worth much less because another company occupied the space I could not defend.

This may be one of the biggest misconceptions in the discussion around bootstrapping. Autonomy does not mean an absence of risk. Sometimes, it means taking on alone a risk that could have been shared.

External capital may be exactly what allows a company to survive long enough to find its market, or to grow quickly enough not to be eliminated by a competitor.

External capital has a cost. But not having capital also has one.

## Not Every Company Needs to Interest an Investor

There is another trap in this discussion: treating the ability to attract investment as a measure of the quality of the company.

A company can be excellent and not be interesting to a venture capital fund. It can have good customers, healthy margins, positive cash flow, and generate excellent income for its owners without having the scale potential required by a particular investment thesis.

This happens because “good company,” “scalable company,” and “company suitable for a particular type of capital” are different things. The investor is looking for a particular return profile. The founder may be looking for something else.

A company can be large enough to completely change the lives of its owners without being large enough to make sense within a fund's strategy. And there is nothing wrong with that. The problem begins when the founder starts using investor interest as a measure of the company's own success.

If nobody wants to invest, that may be a signal that something in the business needs to be reconsidered. But it may also simply mean that there is no alignment between that business and that type of capital.

In the end, perhaps the question is not “Why does nobody want to invest in my company?” but “Do I really need someone to want to invest in my company?”

## Not All Growth Is the Same

This leads me to a distinction I consider increasingly important: ambition for the business is not necessarily ambition for the size of the business. I can want to build an excellent company without wanting to build the largest company possible. I can want good customers, a healthy operation, good professionals, profit, autonomy, and the ability to make decisions without turning the company into a permanent growth machine. That does not mean a lack of ambition.

There is a very common archetype in entrepreneurship in which the founder must constantly pursue the next target, the next round, the next market, the next hire, and the next multiple. For some people, that environment is exactly what they are looking for. For others, it is not.

Not every CEO needs to be obsessed with targets. I may want to build a large company, a highly profitable company, a company that gives me autonomy, or a company that can exist without depending on me. These things can coincide, but they are not the same thing.

The maximum size a company can reach is not necessarily the size I should want it to reach. This matters because external investment can change precisely this equation. A business that could grow sustainably to a certain size may need to pursue a much larger outcome after receiving capital, because the return expected on that investment requires a significantly larger company.

The problem is not dilution itself. If I own 100% of a small company and, after a round, I own 60% of a much larger company, my stake may be worth much more. The problem is diluting for growth that does not create enough value or accepting, in exchange for that growth, a trajectory I did not want to follow.

Perhaps the point is that, by accepting capital, I am not just defining how much of the company I will own in the future. I am also defining which results will need to be achieved for that ownership stake to make sense.

## Building a Company Does Not Mean Running It Forever

There is another question that goes beyond financing. The more a company grows, the more it stops being a direct extension of its founder. In the beginning, the company depends on me for almost everything. I know the customers, the product, the problems, the decisions, and the people. The company and the founder almost overlap.

Over time, that changes. Employees, partners, investors, larger customers, processes, and responsibilities come in. Decisions begin to have consequences for many more people. The company begins to acquire an existence of its own.

I like to think about this the way it happens with a child. At first, they depend on you for practically everything. Then they grow, develop their own relationships, their own needs, and their own identity. At some point, the responsibility of the father or mother stops being to control every decision and becomes accepting that this person has a life of their own. Something similar happens with a company.

Building a company and remaining responsible for it forever are different decisions. Bill Gates left the position of CEO of Microsoft. Jeff Bezos left the position of CEO of Amazon. Warren Buffett built a relationship with Berkshire Hathaway that has long extended beyond the day-to-day operation of the business. In all these cases, the relationship with the company changed over time without that meaning they stopped being part of the history they helped build.

At some point, I may stop being the person who runs the operation. The company may be sold, management may pass to someone else, or I may remain a shareholder without continuing as CEO. None of these changes needs to mean failure. It may simply mean that the company grew beyond the relationship I had with it at the beginning.

And there is a possibility I find particularly interesting: building a company, selling it after reaching significant scale, and then starting another one that is much smaller. From the outside, this may look like regression. But it may be exactly the opposite. After building and running a large company, I may discover what size of company actually makes sense for my life.

## What Future Am I Financing?

The question of external investment cannot be separated from the question of what kind of company I want to build. If there is an opportunity that requires speed, capital may be essential. If competitors are advancing and the company needs to gain scale to survive, preserving autonomy may be too costly a decision. In other cases, the business may be able to grow organically, need time to mature, or simply not require a larger structure. None of these choices is necessarily better.

What seems dangerous to me is treating fundraising as a natural step before knowing what company I am trying to build. Capital does not come alone. It brings expectations, responsibilities, and a particular growth logic. Bootstrapping also has its price: it preserves autonomy, but concentrates risk and can limit the speed needed to compete.

That is why, before deciding how much the company can be worth, how much money I can raise, or what percentage I am willing to give up, I need to understand what future I am financing. Do I really want to build the company this investment will require? And, at the same time, can I build the company I want without this investment?

The answer to these two questions should determine how I finance the company, not the other way around.
