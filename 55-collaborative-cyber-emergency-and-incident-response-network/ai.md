The proposal introduces a collaborative cyber emergency and incident response network designed to reduce the time required to communicate critical cyber incidents to potentially affected organizations, shifting from manual processes that take hours or minutes to automated notifications delivered within seconds. Its central premise is that cyber threats increasingly extend beyond individual organizations due to interconnected systems, shared infrastructure, suppliers, technology providers, and operational dependencies. Consequently, cybersecurity and incident response must be addressed not only at the organizational level but also across entire business ecosystems.

Organizations have invested heavily in cybersecurity capabilities, including Security Operations Centers, monitoring tools, specialized teams, business continuity plans, and incident response procedures. However, communication with external organizations that may be affected by an incident remains heavily dependent on individual contacts, emails, phone calls, and manual coordination. When a shared technology provider, cloud infrastructure, software vendor, payment platform, telecommunications operator, or identity service is compromised, potentially exposed organizations may remain unaware of the situation while independently investigating their exposure. This communication gap creates an opportunity for threats to propagate across interconnected environments.

The proposed solution is an institutional emergency activation mechanism, comparable to a Panic Button, through which an organization can immediately notify other participants about a cyber emergency with potential third-party impact. Once activated, the network would identify organizations with relevant relationships or dependencies and distribute structured notifications containing essential information, such as incident classification, severity, affected providers, and potential exposure. Recipients could then assess their individual risk and initiate appropriate procedures, including enhanced monitoring, credential reviews, integration restrictions, preventive service suspension, or dedicated investigations. Additional information could be shared progressively according to confidentiality requirements, authorization levels, and need-to-know principles. The network would facilitate communication rather than centralize incident management or make operational decisions on behalf of participating organizations.

The primary objective is not to resolve cyber incidents within seconds, but to eliminate delays between incident identification and notification of potentially affected organizations. Emergency communication should become a standardized operational capability supported by predefined activation procedures, participant identification, severity classification, and automated distribution. Effectiveness could be measured through notification delivery time, the accuracy of affected-participant identification, acknowledgment time, and the time required for recipients to initiate their internal response procedures. Rapid notification would allow organizations to investigate potential exposure before independently detecting the threat or receiving conventional communications.

Supply chain security represents a natural starting point because organizations increasingly share technology vendors, software components, infrastructure services, APIs, and other critical resources. A compromise affecting one component can create risks across multiple organizations. A collaborative network could maintain predefined dependency relationships between participants, enabling targeted notifications based on an incident's scope and potential impact. Rather than relying on a single centralized structure, the proposal considers a federated architecture comprising dedicated networks for different industries or ecosystems, each with its own participation rules, governance, incident classifications, and information-sharing policies. Interoperability mechanisms would allow controlled communication between ecosystems whenever shared dependencies exist.

Although the financial sector offers a practical starting point due to its technological interdependence, operational criticality, and continuity requirements, the concept could extend to other industries. In the crypto asset ecosystem, for example, incidents involving exchanges, custodians, liquidity providers, trading platforms, custody services, or blockchain bridges could affect multiple participants. Rapid notifications would enable organizations to review permissions, strengthen monitoring, and implement preventive controls. Similar applications exist in healthcare, energy, telecommunications, government, and other sectors dependent on shared infrastructure. The underlying model is industry-independent, provided that each ecosystem can establish appropriate governance and interoperability arrangements.

Building this infrastructure collectively introduces significant technical, organizational, legal, and regulatory challenges. The network must establish institutional trust, strong authentication, reliable participant identification, standardized incident classification, confidentiality controls, availability, auditability, and accountability. It must prevent unauthorized or fraudulent notifications, including those resulting from compromised credentials, and avoid exposing sensitive business relationships or triggering unnecessary service disruptions. A centralized platform could become a high-value attack target, making federated architecture, strong institutional identities, digitally signed messages, redundancy, and comprehensive auditing important design considerations. Information sharing must also comply with confidentiality obligations, banking secrecy, data protection requirements, and contractual restrictions. Clear boundaries must separate notification responsibilities from each organization's autonomous operational decisions.

In Brazil, the Central Bank of Brazil and Febraban could play important roles in coordinating the initiative. The Central Bank could contribute institutional coordination, regulatory perspectives, communication standards, interoperability requirements, and security guidelines for authorized financial institutions. Febraban could mobilize financial institutions, technology providers, and specialists, leveraging its experience in sector-wide technology and security initiatives. Organizations such as CERT.br and CTIR Gov, alongside technology companies, infrastructure providers, and representatives of other industries, could also contribute. Existing Brazilian and international information-sharing and incident coordination initiatives should be assessed and leveraged rather than duplicated. The proposed network could complement these structures by introducing a standardized emergency activation and rapid operational notification capability.

The initiative is intended as a collaborative proposal rather than a finalized architecture, governance framework, commercial product, or implementation specification. Its development should involve institutions and specialists with practical knowledge of cybersecurity, infrastructure, risk management, regulation, and operational constraints. One possible initial step would be a controlled pilot involving a limited number of financial institutions and strategic technology providers. This pilot could validate activation procedures, authentication mechanisms, dependency mapping, participant identification, and notification delivery times before expanding participation and interoperability.

The broader objective is to establish collective cyber emergency response as a shared capability rather than an isolated organizational responsibility. A single company should not be expected to independently develop and operate infrastructure serving multiple institutions and industries. Instead, participating organizations should jointly define the standards, trust mechanisms, governance structures, and communication processes required to disseminate critical information rapidly and reliably. The initiative should begin by evaluating existing capabilities, identifying gaps, and developing a practical collaboration model.

The proposal ultimately addresses a growing structural challenge: as organizations become increasingly interconnected, the impact of cyber incidents extends beyond individual infrastructure boundaries, making collective awareness and coordinated response essential. Improving prevention and detection remains important, but the next evolution in cybersecurity should also include mechanisms that allow organizations to communicate and respond collectively when incidents occur. If threats are shared across ecosystems, the capabilities required to respond to them should also be shared. The initial document is intended to stimulate discussion and gather contributions, criticism, and alternative approaches from information security professionals, solution architects, infrastructure specialists, risk managers, financial institutions, regulators, and technology providers.
