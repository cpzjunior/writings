The central thesis is that a startup can maintain multiple core activities without sacrificing focus if it concentrates most resources on a primary economic activity while deliberately allocating a smaller share to experimentation and alternative opportunities. Focus is defined as clarity about resource priorities rather than exclusive commitment to a single activity. Diversification can therefore serve not only risk reduction but experimentation, organizational learning, and discovery of future opportunities.

A four-and-a-half-year experience founding and volunteering as president of Se Doar, a social marketing NGO with a free platform connecting NGOs, institutions, social projects, donors, companies, and volunteers, exposed a structural sustainability problem. The organization depended heavily on volunteers because its revenue was limited and unpredictable, making permanent hires risky. Although volunteers could be highly committed, their availability was constrained by their other responsibilities, including the founder's own paid career. By March 2024, the founder could no longer sustain the additional workload and, without a successor willing to assume the presidency, the NGO ceased activities. Its mission and model had not necessarily become irrelevant; the structure failed because it depended on an amount of personal time that was no longer available. This led to the broader question of whether every organizational activity must independently sustain itself financially in order to exist.

An alternative model is a company with one economically sustainable core activity supporting other activities that have different purposes, such as revenue generation, intellectual-property development, knowledge production, social impact, or experimentation. The activities should coexist for a reason, and the primary activity must be sufficiently strong to sustain the overall structure.

Focus and concentration are distinct. A small company has limited resources and therefore needs prioritization, but prioritization does not logically require directing every resource toward one activity. A company can dedicate most people, capital, and time to the activity that sustains it while reserving a smaller portion for other strategic opportunities. This creates a portfolio of bets with different risk levels, objectives, and return horizons. The central management problem becomes deciding which activity receives the greatest allocation and how much capacity can safely be reserved for others.

A hypothetical company structure would contain Corporate, Entertainment, and Social divisions. Corporate would be the economic core, developing management products, automation, and tailored corporate solutions and generating most revenue and predictability. Entertainment would develop games, animation, and digital experiences, reflecting a longstanding interest but operating in a highly uncertain market where individual projects can consume substantial time and capital without finding an audience. Social would use technology and knowledge to support NGOs, institutes, foundations, and community projects while providing professionals in training with real-world development opportunities. Social initiatives would not need immediate monetization to justify their existence because the company could deliberately allocate capacity to social objectives.

The specific three-division configuration is personal rather than universal. Other combinations could be valid if their activities have a coherent reason to coexist, such as education/software/media or industry/technology/research. The general principle is that multiple activities can coexist if there is a clear priority and rationale for integration. Corporate would initially be the center of gravity, while Entertainment and Social would receive smaller allocations. Those allocations could change as evidence accumulates: a secondary activity could expand, remain small, disappear, or become dominant. Resource balance should emerge from organizational learning rather than being imposed permanently.

Diversification requires disciplined asymmetry rather than equal allocation. An initial allocation resembling 80/20 is proposed: roughly 80% of effort toward Corporate and 20% distributed between Entertainment and Social. Corporate would fund salaries, infrastructure, and experimentation, while the remaining capacity would deliberately allow projects such as games, social initiatives, or emerging technologies to develop without requiring immediate financial self-sufficiency. The 80/20 split is not permanent; resource allocation should change when an activity demonstrates greater potential.

The principal risk is resource dilution: attempting several activities without giving any enough capacity to succeed. This risk does not necessarily invalidate diversification; it means allocation must have a clear rationale. A company can explicitly identify one activity as the priority while reserving limited capacity for strategic alternatives. The relevant failure condition is not the existence of multiple activities but the absence of any sufficiently strong activity capable of sustaining the organization.

Keeping multiple activities inside one company can create value beyond financial diversification because resources can circulate among them. Financial capital, technology, intellectual property, knowledge, infrastructure, and people can move across divisions. A professional moving from Corporate to Entertainment could transfer expertise in software architecture, product management, user experience, and customer needs. Someone moving in the opposite direction could bring knowledge of interaction, engagement, design, and game development. Social projects could expose professionals to problems and contexts unavailable in corporate work, while technologies and practices developed elsewhere could be reused for nonprofit problems.

This requires a culture of non-linear career mobility rather than merely a formal mobility policy. Employees should not be permanently identified with one division; they should be able to move among environments when opportunities and interests align. The organization consequently accumulates not only financial capital but also knowledge, intellectual property, experience, and diverse problem-solving repertoires. Cross-division circulation increases the possibility of unexpected connections generating new products, technologies, and solutions.

Diversification therefore becomes an innovation strategy when activities exchange capabilities rather than merely exchange money. A technology created for Entertainment might improve a Corporate product; experience from a Social project might reveal a new product opportunity; a corporate tool might reduce the cost or time of an entertainment project. Professionals themselves transport knowledge across contexts, allowing techniques and perspectives from one industry to influence another.

Multiple divisions can function as experimentation environments. Each has different users, constraints, and problems, but knowledge does not need to remain confined to its originating context. The value of diversification consequently has at least two levels: reducing dependence on one revenue source and expanding the organization's capacity to learn and discover new opportunities. The second is considered the more strategically interesting benefit.

For startups specifically, the rationale differs from that of established companies. Established companies have operating histories, capital, and more information for allocating resources, and can separate businesses into subsidiaries or holding structures when appropriate. Startups are still testing assumptions about products, markets, demand, and revenue models. Maintaining several activities can preserve strategic options while confidence in any one direction remains limited, provided investment levels reflect different degrees of confidence.

A multi-activity structure can also change the meaning of a pivot. Instead of completely abandoning one hypothesis and starting another, a startup can explore multiple hypotheses simultaneously at unequal investment levels. One activity may gain traction, another may remain small, and another may fail. Resources can then be shifted toward demonstrated potential while reducing or shutting down weaker activities. Knowledge, technology, infrastructure, intellectual property, and experience accumulated by a failed activity may still retain value elsewhere, so a pivot need not discard everything previously built.

The reserved experimental allocation can therefore be understood as an option-preservation mechanism. If experimental bets fail, the economic core remains intact. If one succeeds, its resource allocation can gradually increase and potentially transform it into a new core business. The distinction is between intentional diversification—deliberately allocating a limited portion of resources to alternative possibilities—and lack of focus, where resources are distributed without clear priorities.

Large diversified companies demonstrate that multiple activities can be organized through different forms of integration, although their scale makes them imperfect analogies for startups. Nintendo combines hardware, software, digital services, and intellectual property; Microsoft operates across productivity, cloud, advertising, devices, and gaming; Amazon expanded from e-commerce into businesses including cloud computing, advertising, and entertainment. These examples do not establish a universal model or imply that an early-stage startup should replicate them. They demonstrate instead that multiple activities can coexist under different organizational architectures and that their relationships may involve financing, shared technology, intellectual property, distribution, knowledge, or platform effects.

Integration has limits. Multiple activities should not automatically be placed inside one operating structure. If divisions have unrelated customers, technologies, processes, and capabilities, coordination costs can exceed synergies. Excessive integration can cause unnecessary standardization, inefficient personnel allocation, and competition for resources that would be better concentrated within individual businesses. Activities may therefore belong to the same economic group without sharing one operating organization. The key test is whether there is a substantive advantage to keeping them together, such as shared people, technology, knowledge, intellectual property, infrastructure, or distribution. “Diversification” by itself is insufficient justification.

The broader conclusion is that a company does not inherently need a single core activity to have focus. The relevant conditions are a clear center of gravity, explicit resource priorities, controlled investment in secondary activities, a rationale for organizational integration, and mechanisms for transferring people and knowledge across activities. In the proposed structure, Corporate provides economic sustainability and predictability, while Entertainment and Social preserve experimentation and alternative purposes. Their relative importance can evolve according to what the organization discovers.

The underlying principle is not that Corporate, Entertainment, and Social are universally appropriate activities. It is that a company can maintain a primary path while deliberately keeping other possibilities open. If it understands why those possibilities exist, how much it is willing to invest in them, what can be learned from them, and when resources should be reallocated, multiple activities can constitute a deliberate mechanism for discovering what the company can become rather than merely a distraction from focus.
