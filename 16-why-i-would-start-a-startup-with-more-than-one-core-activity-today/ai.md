The introduction explains the author’s motivation for considering a startup with multiple core activities, rooted in four and a half years running Se Doar, a social marketing NGO alongside a technology career. Although the NGO’s mission and model remained relevant, its dependence on volunteer availability and limited, unpredictable funding made sustained operations difficult; when the author could no longer maintain a second workday and no successor was available, the organization closed in March 2024. This experience led to a broader question about whether every activity must independently sustain itself financially to be viable. The proposed alternative is an organization in which one economically sustainable core activity supports other activities that may pursue revenue, intellectual property, knowledge, social impact, or experimentation, provided there is a coherent reason to keep them together and the primary activity is strong enough to support the structure.

The section “Focus Does Not Mean Doing Only One Thing” challenges the common startup premise that focus requires choosing exactly one core activity. While concentration is often necessary because startups have limited resources, the author distinguishes focus from exclusivity: focus means knowing where to place most resources, not necessarily placing all resources in one activity. A company could devote most of its people, capital, and time to its economically sustaining activity while reserving a smaller capacity for activities with different objectives and risk or return horizons. The resulting organization could maintain several deliberate bets rather than optimizing exclusively for one product or market. The central management problem would therefore be deciding which activity receives priority and how much capacity can safely be allocated elsewhere without weakening the core.

The section “One Company, Multiple Core Activities” describes the organizational structure the author would personally choose: Corporate, Entertainment, and Social. Corporate would be the economic foundation, developing management products, automation, and customized corporate solutions while generating most revenue and providing the predictability required to support the rest of the organization. Entertainment would pursue games, animation, and digital experiences, reflecting a longstanding personal interest but also accepting the high uncertainty of that industry without making the entire company financially dependent on individual releases. Social would use technology and knowledge to support NGOs, institutes, foundations, and community projects while providing practical development opportunities for professionals in training; its activities would not necessarily need to justify themselves through revenue. The author emphasizes that these three areas are personal choices rather than a universal model: other companies could combine education, software, media, industry, technology, research, or other activities. The relevant principle is whether there is a reason for the activities to coexist and sufficient clarity about resource priorities. Corporate would initially be the center of gravity, while the secondary activities could gain or lose resources over time depending on what the organization discovers.

The section “Diversification Requires Focus” argues that multiple activities would require disciplined rather than equal resource allocation. The proposed initial structure is approximately 80% of resources devoted to Corporate and 20% divided between Entertainment and Social, making Corporate the predictable economic foundation while reserving deliberate capacity for experimentation. This asymmetry distinguishes the model from indiscriminate diversification: secondary projects are not expected to receive equal investment, and their resource allocation can change as evidence emerges about their potential. The author recognizes the risk that spreading limited startup resources across too many initiatives could prevent any of them from reaching sufficient scale, but argues that the problem is not the existence of multiple activities itself; it is allocating resources without a clear rationale or without maintaining a sufficiently strong core. Focus can therefore coexist with several activities as long as priorities remain explicit and the organization retains a clear center of gravity.

The section “Circulation of Resources and People” argues that the value of multiple activities extends beyond financial diversification because money, technology, intellectual property, knowledge, and people can circulate among them. A professional moving from Corporate to Entertainment could transfer skills in software architecture, product management, user experience, and customer understanding, while someone moving in the opposite direction could bring insights from game development concerning interaction, design, engagement, and experience. Social projects could expose professionals to problems and contexts unavailable in corporate products, while technologies and practices developed elsewhere could subsequently be applied to nonprofit work. The divisions would therefore function not as isolated businesses but as connected environments within a common structure. Achieving this would require a culture that supports non-linear careers and allows people to move between divisions according to interest and opportunity. Over time, the company could accumulate not only financial capital but also knowledge, intellectual property, experience, and diverse repertoires, with circulation among activities potentially creating unexpected combinations and opportunities.

The section “Diversification as an Innovation Strategy” develops the argument beyond risk reduction. Financially, having Corporate fund an Entertainment project is merely diversification, but the author sees greater value when activities exchange capabilities and each can increase the capabilities of the others. Technologies created for Entertainment might improve Corporate products; Social projects might expose unmet needs that inspire new products; corporate tools could reduce the cost or time required for entertainment projects; and solutions developed for clients could be adapted for nonprofits. People themselves transmit these lessons as they move across contexts, allowing techniques or perspectives developed in one industry to produce different applications elsewhere. Multiple activities therefore become experimentation environments within the same organization, each exposing the company to different users, constraints, and problems while allowing knowledge to circulate. The deeper justification for diversification is consequently not merely reducing dependence on one revenue source but expanding the organization’s capacity to learn, experiment, combine capabilities, and discover new opportunities.

The section “For a Startup, Experimentation Can Be Part of the Strategy” distinguishes this approach from diversification in an established company. Mature organizations already possess capital, operating histories, and more information about where resources can be allocated, making structures such as subsidiaries, holding companies, or focused business units viable. A startup, by contrast, is still testing its assumptions about products, markets, demand, and revenue models, so maintaining several activities with different investment levels could preserve options while evidence is still limited. This also changes the concept of pivoting: instead of completely abandoning one hypothesis and starting again, a company could explore several hypotheses simultaneously, increase investment in those showing traction, and reduce or eliminate those that fail. Resources accumulated in one activity—including people, technology, knowledge, and infrastructure—may retain value elsewhere. The proposed 80/20 allocation therefore represents not simply a diversification mechanism but an intentional discovery budget: the secondary 20% creates room to identify potential future core businesses without threatening the economic foundation. The author distinguishes this from lack of focus by defining diversification as a deliberate decision to allocate a bounded portion of resources to exploring alternatives.

The section “What Other Companies Can Teach Us” notes that companies operating across multiple activities are not a new phenomenon, citing Nintendo, Microsoft, and Amazon as examples of organizations combining businesses such as hardware, software, digital services, intellectual property, productivity, cloud computing, advertising, devices, gaming, commerce, and entertainment. These companies are explicitly not presented as models to replicate because their scale, structures, and markets differ substantially from those of an early-stage startup. Instead, they demonstrate that multiple activities can be integrated through different mechanisms: one division can finance another, activities can share technology, intellectual property, distribution, or knowledge, and one business can create a platform that enables others. There is therefore no single architecture for a multi-activity organization; the important requirement is a rationale for coexistence. For startups, the potential value is especially connected to discovery: activities can be tested at different levels of investment, expanded when they show traction, reduced or discontinued when they do not, while knowledge and capabilities accumulated during experimentation can remain useful elsewhere.

The section “When Integration Does Not Make Sense” establishes a limit to the thesis by arguing that multiple activities should not automatically be housed within the same operating organization. If divisions have substantially different customers, technologies, processes, and capabilities, coordination costs may exceed the benefits of integration. Shared staffing could become artificial, technologies could be standardized unnecessarily, and competing resource demands could weaken otherwise independent businesses. In such cases, separation may be preferable even if the activities remain under the same economic group, since common ownership does not require a common operating structure. The relevant question is whether there is a concrete advantage to keeping the activities together; diversification alone is insufficient. Shared people, technology, knowledge, intellectual property, infrastructure, or distribution can provide such a rationale. The thesis is therefore not that companies should do several things, but that they need not restrict themselves to one activity when the benefits of integration justify the additional complexity.

The conclusion, “Focus Does Not Mean Having a Single Direction,” brings together the author’s experience with Se Doar, professional experience in technology, and interest in games. The author does not want to rely on a second workday to pursue meaningful projects and therefore imagines a company where one activity provides economic sustainability while other activities both learn from and support one another. Corporate would initially provide focus and predictability, while Entertainment and Social would receive smaller allocations that preserve experimentation without undermining sustainability; these proportions could change as evidence accumulates, including through the growth, reduction, or termination of individual activities. The specific Corporate/Entertainment/Social configuration is explicitly not presented as universal; other combinations could work if they have a coherent rationale and clear priorities. The broader argument is that a company does not necessarily need a single core activity to remain focused: focus can mean knowing the priority, the resource allocation, the reasons secondary activities exist, and what can be learned from them, while deliberately keeping alternative possibilities open.
