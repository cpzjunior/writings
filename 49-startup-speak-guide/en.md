# A Guide to Startup Speak

_A practical guide to the main terms in the startup ecosystem, with definitions, concrete examples, and related terms, organized by levels of complexity._

**Summary:** I like the idea of treating startup vocabulary as a foreign language: at first, you only need to learn the essential words to understand what is being said; then, the relationships between those terms allow you to follow conversations about product, market, growth, money, and ownership. So I organized the main concepts of Startup Speak into levels of complexity, prioritizing the vocabulary needed to recognize the terms and understand how they connect, without trying to create a definitive dictionary. The idea is not to turn anyone into a specialist, but to provide enough context for these words to stop looking like a sequence of incomprehensible acronyms. You don't need to speak Startup Speak fluently. You just need to stop thinking they're speaking another language.

---

I'm a fan of Código Fonte TV's Programmer's Dictionary. The idea always seemed very good to me because programming has its own language. People coming from outside encounter terms that, to those already in the field, seem obvious, but can turn a simple conversation into a sequence of incomprehensible words.

Something similar happens in the startup world. There is its own vocabulary, made up of acronyms, English expressions, and concepts that, for people who work in this environment, eventually become part of everyday language. For those just arriving, however, following a conversation can be harder than it should be.

The purpose of this guide is to treat this vocabulary as a foreign language. Imagine that you have just arrived in a distant country. On your first day, you don't need to discuss politics or negotiate a contract. You need to understand the basics so you can get by. Here, the logic is the same. First, you learn the simplest words and, as you progress, you start to understand conversations about product, market, growth, economics, capital, and ownership.

The inspiration comes from the Programmer's Dictionary, but this article does not intend to reproduce the same format. A dictionary works well as a reference, with independent terms organized for lookup. In a single article, that structure would lose some of its meaning. The concepts are connected and make more sense when they appear within the conversations in which they are used.

For that reason, the content is organized into learning levels, following a natural progression. The order is not alphabetical, and the levels do not exist merely to separate easy words from difficult ones. The idea is to build vocabulary gradually, so that each stage helps you understand the next.

And this is, deliberately, a practical guide. The startup world has far more terms than could possibly be covered in a single article. It would be easy to turn the text into an endless catalog of acronyms, metrics, financial instruments, and expressions specific to certain areas. The proposal here is different: to select the vocabulary most useful to someone who wants to start following these conversations and understand how the main concepts relate to one another. Anything left out can be learned later, with better context for fitting each new term into place.

Like any professional language, this vocabulary also has its dialects. Some terms are quite objective; others vary depending on context. The definitions presented here are practical references, not universal rules. The goal is not to create a definitive reference or turn anyone into a specialist, but to provide enough vocabulary to recognize the concepts, follow the conversations, and keep learning.

You don't need to speak Startup Speak fluently. This guide only needs to make sure that, when someone starts talking, you know what they're talking about.

## N1. First Words

Every language begins with basic vocabulary. In Startup Speak, some of the first words are startup, founder, co-founder, customer, user, B2B, B2C, SaaS, and marketplace.

A startup is not simply a small or newly created company. The term usually refers to an organization seeking to build a repeatable and scalable business model in a context of uncertainty. A company can be new without being a startup, just as it can be small without aiming to scale rapidly. What matters is the nature of the business and the pursuit of a model that can grow significantly without requiring a proportional increase in the structure needed to operate it.

A founder is someone who founded the company. A co-founder is one of the founders when more than one person holds that position. The distinction is simple, but these terms appear frequently because the company's history and corporate structure are directly tied to who founded it.

Customer and user are also not necessarily the same person. A customer is the person who buys or pays for the product; a user is the person who actually uses it. In many businesses, the two figures coincide. In others, they do not. In a corporate solution, for example, a company may be the customer while its employees are the users. This distinction is especially important for understanding how the product is sold and used.

B2B, business to business, is the model in which a company sells to other companies. B2C, business to consumer, is when it sells directly to the end consumer. The difference goes far beyond the acronym: it changes the sales dynamics, decision-making process, sales cycle, and the way the product reaches the market.

SaaS, software as a service, is the model in which software is offered as a service, usually through a subscription or recurring fee. A marketplace, on the other hand, is a platform that connects supply and demand, allowing different participants to transact with one another. They are very different models, but both appear frequently in conversations about digital businesses.

With this basic vocabulary, a sentence like “it's a B2B SaaS startup founded by two co-founders, with companies as customers and employees of those companies as users” no longer sounds like a sequence of disconnected words. You don't master the language yet, but you can already understand what they're talking about.

To see if you're following: a newly created company, with a single founder, that sells subscription software directly to end consumers, is it necessarily a startup? And, in that case, who is the customer and who is the user?

## N2. Starting to Talk

After learning the first words, it's time to follow what the startup people are talking about. Now it isn't enough to know the meaning of each term in isolation. You need to understand what the company is trying to build, for whom, and in which market. This is where problem, solution, value proposition, business model, ICP, TAM, SAM, SOM, MVP, validation, PMF, moat, pivot, and traction appear.

The conversation usually starts with a problem worth solving. The solution is the way found to solve it, while the value proposition explains why that solution is relevant to the customer and why they should choose it. The business model, in turn, describes how the company creates, delivers, and captures value, including who pays, what they pay for, and how the operation generates revenue. These concepts form the foundation of conversations about product and business.

To understand who actually has this problem, we use ICP, Ideal Customer Profile, the profile of the ideal customer for the business. It doesn't simply mean who could buy, but who has the greatest fit with the problem the company solves, the greatest potential value, and, in many contexts, the best conditions to be acquired and served efficiently. The clearer the ICP, the more precise product, marketing, and sales decisions tend to be.

Once the customer is defined, another important question arises: how many people or companies could buy this solution? That's where TAM, SAM, and SOM come in. TAM, Total Addressable Market, represents the total market that could be served by the solution in a broad scenario. SAM, Serviceable Available Market, is the portion of that market that the company can serve considering its product, region, segment, or other constraints. SOM, Serviceable Obtainable Market, is the portion the company realistically expects to capture. Together, the three concepts help size the market potential and put the opportunity into perspective.

With a problem identified, a customer defined, and an estimated market opportunity, the company needs to discover whether the solution actually works. That's where MVP, Minimum Viable Product, comes in. An MVP is the smallest version or experiment capable of testing a relevant hypothesis and generating real learning. It isn't necessarily a bad or incomplete version of the product. The idea is to reduce the cost and time required to discover whether a given hypothesis deserves to be developed.

This learning is what we call validation. Validating a hypothesis means obtaining evidence that supports or weakens it. Saying that people “liked the idea” can be a signal, but it is usually weak evidence. Validation becomes stronger when market behavior confirms what the company believed, whether through usage, payment, retention, recurring behavior, or another relevant behavior.

When the solution demonstrates that there is consistent demand and that the product can meet a relevant need in a given market, another important term begins to appear: PMF, Product-Market Fit. It isn't a certificate that the company receives at a particular moment, but a condition of fit between product and market, perceived through evidence of real and sustained demand.

Even after finding PMF, an important question remains: what prevents another company from doing something similar? That's where moat, or defensible competitive advantage, comes in. The term comes from the idea of a moat around a castle and represents what makes it difficult for competitors to enter or for customers to be lost. It can come from network effects, technology, data, brand, switching costs, scale, distribution, or other mechanisms that make the business harder to replicate.

Validation doesn't always confirm the initial hypotheses. When learning shows that some fundamental assumption was wrong, a pivot may emerge. Pivoting doesn't simply mean changing a feature. It means making a significant change in product or business strategy based on what was learned, while preserving what still makes sense. A company can change its audience, monetization model, product, or distribution method without necessarily abandoning all of its previous work.

As the product finds a market and the company begins to move forward, another term emerges: traction. It is evidence that the business is gaining ground. It can appear in revenue, number of customers, users, growth, retention, or other relevant metrics. Traction is not synonymous with revenue, nor necessarily with PMF. A company can show growth or other signs of traction before finding solid product-market fit. What matters is demonstrating some consistent movement toward a business that works.

With this story to tell, the pitch appears, the presentation used to explain the business briefly and in a structured way. The pitch deck is the material that organizes this narrative. In a conversation with investors, for example, it typically connects the problem, solution, market, business model, traction, team, and capital needs. The content varies depending on the context, but the function is similar: to allow someone to quickly understand what is being built, for whom, the size of the opportunity, and why it matters.

To see if you're following: imagine a company that has identified a problem, defined its ICP, and estimated a large market, but still doesn't know whether its solution actually works. Should it first invest in building the complete product or launch an MVP? And if the first tests show that customers don't behave as expected, what would need to happen to justify a pivot?

## N3. Everyday Life

After the first conversations, it's time to understand how people find, try, and continue using a product. If at the previous level the concern was discovering whether there is a problem, a solution, and a market, now the question becomes how people enter the product and what happens afterward. This is where acquisition, funnel, lead, conversion rate, activation, retention, churn, and cohort appear.

Acquisition is the process of bringing users or customers to the product or sales process. This can happen through paid media, content, referrals, sales, partners, organic search, or a variety of other channels. But bringing people to the product is only the beginning. After acquiring them, you need to understand how they move forward.

The funnel organizes precisely these stages. Depending on the business, a person might first learn about the company, become a lead, start a trial, activate the product, and finally become a customer. The funnel helps visualize how many people reach each stage and, most importantly, where they stop progressing.

A lead is a person or company that has shown some interest in the solution and entered the acquisition or sales process. What constitutes this interest varies depending on the business. It might be filling out a form, requesting a demo, leaving contact information, or taking another action that indicates intent. In some models, especially B2B, the concept is central; in others, such as certain B2C or PLG products, it may not exist as a formal stage.

That's where conversion rate comes in, the conversion rate between one stage and another. If one thousand people reach a page and one hundred take a given action, the conversion rate for that stage is 10%. Tracking these rates makes it possible to identify points of friction and understand the impact of changes throughout the funnel.

But progressing through the funnel does not necessarily mean perceiving value in the product. Activation is the moment when the user first experiences the core value of the solution. There is no universal definition for this event. Each product needs to determine which behavior represents a meaningful first experience of value. In a collaboration tool, for example, it might be creating the first project; in another product, it could be completing a particular operation.

After activation, the question changes: does the user keep using the product? That's retention. A company may acquire many users and activate them quickly, but still have a fragile business if those people stop using the product after a few days.

Churn represents precisely this loss. It can be measured in terms of customers or revenue, and the difference matters. Losing many small customers can have a different economic effect from losing a few large customers. That's why it is common to distinguish customer churn from revenue churn.

To understand whether retention is improving or worsening over time, we use the concept of cohort. A cohort is a group of users or customers that shares some relevant characteristic. A common use is to group people by the period in which they entered the product, forming January, February, March cohorts, and so on. Instead of looking only at the company's average retention, we can track the behavior of each group over time. This helps identify changes in customer behavior even when an aggregate metric appears stable.

This view makes it possible to connect acquisition and behavior. When someone says “our CAC increased, but retention of new cohorts improved,” they are saying that it became more expensive to acquire customers, but customers acquired more recently are staying longer. To know whether this trade-off is worthwhile, we still need to understand how much it costs to acquire that customer and how much value they generate over time.

To see if you're following: a company greatly increased acquisition and brought twice as many users to the product, but most abandon it before activation and new cohorts show increasingly lower retention. Is the problem necessarily acquisition? Which parts of the funnel would you investigate before concluding that the growth strategy is working?

## N4. Starting to Work

This is where Startup Speak begins to become closer to the language of management. It's no longer enough to know how many people reach the product and how long they remain. Now you need to understand whether growth makes economic sense: how much it costs to acquire a customer, how much value that customer generates, what the business's margin is, and how long it takes for the investment to pay back.

CAC, Customer Acquisition Cost, is the cost of acquiring customers. In a simplified view, it relates the expenses involved in marketing and sales to the number of new customers acquired during a given period. The exact calculation can vary depending on the scope adopted by the company, but the fundamental question is always the same: how much are we spending to acquire a customer?

This cost needs to be compared with the value the customer generates throughout the relationship. That's where LTV, Lifetime Value, comes in, representing the expected economic value of a customer during their time with the company. There are different methodologies for estimating it, and the quality of the estimate depends on the assumptions used. In unit economics analyses, it is especially important to consider the customer's economic contribution, not just gross revenue.

CAC and LTV therefore begin to make sense when analyzed together. If it costs a lot to acquire a customer who generates little value, there is a structural problem. If the acquisition cost is high, but the customer remains for a long time and generates enough margin to compensate for it, the situation is different. This relationship helps answer whether the company's growth is creating or destroying value.

This is precisely the logic of unit economics, the analysis of the economics of the business at the level of a unit, such as a customer, order, or transaction. Instead of looking only at the company as a whole, we analyze how much value a unit generates, how much it costs to acquire, and how much it costs to serve. CAC, LTV, margin, and payback are some of the pieces of this analysis.

Payback is the time required to recover CAC from the economic contribution generated by the customer. If the company spends R$1,000 to acquire a customer and recovers that investment in six months, the payback is six months, considering the assumptions used in the calculation. The longer the payback, the greater the need for capital to sustain growth tends to be.

To calculate this contribution properly, you also need to look at gross margin. It represents the portion of revenue that remains after the costs directly associated with delivering the product or service. A company can grow in revenue and still have poor economics if the margin on that revenue is insufficient.

In SaaS companies, this conversation takes on another dimension with MRR and ARR. MRR, Monthly Recurring Revenue, is monthly recurring revenue. ARR, Annual Recurring Revenue, is annualized recurring revenue. In a common simplification, ARR corresponds to twelve times MRR, although it is not necessarily equivalent to the accounting revenue recognized during the period.

When someone says “we're growing 10% per month in MRR,” they are talking about growth in the recurring revenue base. When they say “we reached R$12 million in ARR,” they are presenting an annualized view of that revenue. MRR and ARR help track business evolution, but they do not replace analysis of margin, CAC, LTV, or cash generation.

To see if you're following: a company is growing rapidly in MRR, but needs to spend increasingly more to acquire customers, has low margins, and takes a long time to recover that investment. Would you consider this growth healthy just by looking at revenue? What metrics would you analyze to understand whether each new customer is contributing to an economically sustainable business?

## N5. Talking About Growth

With product, metrics, and basic economics understood, we arrive at growth. Growth does not simply mean increasing the number of users. It is the discipline of finding and operating mechanisms capable of making relevant metrics grow consistently and in an economically sustainable way.

A company can grow in customers, revenue, active users, transactions, or other indicators. The central point is to understand which metric represents value for that business model and which mechanisms can influence it. To do this, the company needs to decide how it intends to reach the market and turn interest into customers.

GTM, Go-to-Market, is the strategy for entering and operating in the market. It involves defining the audience, positioning, distribution channels, sales approach, and the way the offering will be presented to the market. It is not just a launch campaign, but the structure that connects product, market, and acquisition.

This strategy also needs to answer a fundamental question: how will the company capture the value it delivers? Pricing deals precisely with decisions related to monetizing the offering. It is not just about setting a price, but deciding on the pricing model and unit of charge, plans, limits, discounts, and the relationship between price and perceived customer value.

The way the product is sold also influences the growth mechanism. In Sales-led Growth, the sales operation plays a central role in acquiring and converting customers. It is common for complex, higher-ticket products or those that require human interaction to demonstrate value, negotiate, and close contracts.

In Product-led Growth, or PLG, the product itself plays a central role in acquisition, activation, conversion, retention, and expansion. This does not necessarily mean a company without a sales team. It means that the product is one of the main growth engines and allows customers to experience and perceive value with less dependence on a traditional sales process.

Sales-led and Product-led are not closed boxes or necessarily mutually exclusive strategies. A company can start with founder-led sales, build a sales team, incorporate PLG elements, and change its strategy over time. Likewise, growth is not necessarily the goal of every business. Some companies may prioritize margin, cash generation, or slower growth without seeking accelerated scale or venture capital.

In the end, growth is not an isolated function. It is the consequence of the interaction between product, market, acquisition, conversion, retention, pricing, and business economics. Growing means finding a combination of these elements that allows the business to increase without destroying the value it generates.

To see if you're following: a company has a good product but cannot grow. Its pricing seems appropriate, but it still hasn't found an efficient way to reach customers. Would you start by increasing acquisition spending or first review its GTM? And, depending on how the product is sold and used, would a Sales-led or Product-led strategy make more sense?

## N6. Talking About Money

At some point, the conversation stops being just “is the business growing?” and becomes “how long can we finance this growth?” That's when burn rate and runway enter the vocabulary. After understanding how a company grows and whether the economics of that growth make sense, the next question is whether there is enough cash to sustain operations.

Burn rate represents the rate at which the company consumes net cash, usually tracked on a monthly basis. In a simplified view, it corresponds to how much cash the company is consuming to finance its operations. A company can have revenue and still have a burn if its expenses are greater than the cash generated by operations.

From burn and available cash, we can estimate runway, which represents how long the company can sustain operations given a certain level of consumption. If a company has R$3 million in cash and consumes R$500,000 per month, its simplified runway is six months. In practice, the calculation needs to consider expected changes in revenue, expenses, hiring, and other factors, because burn is not necessarily constant.

When someone says “we have eight months of runway,” therefore, they are saying that, under certain assumptions, there is approximately an eight-month horizon before the cash runs out. This information is important for deciding when the company needs to cut expenses, generate more cash, or seek additional capital.

A company does not necessarily need investors to finance its growth. Bootstrapping is building and financing the business primarily with the founders' own resources and the revenue generated by operations. This can reduce dependence on external capital and preserve a larger share of the founders' ownership, but it can also limit the speed of growth or require the company to reach positive cash generation sooner.

When operations do not generate enough cash or when founders choose to accelerate growth, fundraising may arise, the process of raising capital to finance the company. It involves preparing the fundraising narrative and materials, talking to potential investors, answering questions, undergoing due diligence, negotiating terms, and closing the transaction. Runway therefore also influences when a company needs to start raising money, since a round can take months to complete.

The capital raised can allow the company to hire people, develop the product, invest in acquisition, expand into new markets, or simply increase the time available to reach the next stage. But in many transactions, raising capital also means giving a share of the company to new investors. At this point, the conversation stops being just about how much money the company has and starts involving who puts that money in and what they receive in return.

To see if you're following: a company has R$3 million in cash, consumes R$500,000 per month, and is growing rapidly, but still does not generate enough cash to finance operations. Should it wait until the runway runs out before seeking investment? What factors would you consider when deciding between continuing to finance growth through the operation itself and starting a fundraising process?

## N7. Entering the World of Investors

When a startup decides to raise capital, a new question arises: who is willing to put money into this business and assume the risk of investing in it? This is when angel investors, venture capital, and the different stages of a round enter the picture.

An angel investor is an individual who invests their own capital, usually in early-stage companies. In addition to money, they can contribute experience, market knowledge, contacts, and guidance. The relationship with the founder can be quite close, especially in the early stages.

VC, short for venture capital, usually refers to funds specialized in investing in companies with high growth potential. The capital comes from an institutional structure, with investors in the fund itself and an investment thesis that guides where and how resources are allocated. Because this type of investment involves high risk, the fund seeks companies capable of generating significant returns to compensate for the losses inherent in the portfolio.

The investor profile tends to follow the company's stage. This is the context in which round names appear: Pre-seed, Seed, Series A, and later other series. These terms indicate financing stages, but they are not universal categories with rigid criteria. A Seed in one company or market can represent a very different situation from a Seed in another.

Generally speaking, a Pre-seed round is associated with companies that are still very early, often before a fully developed product or more robust market evidence exists. Seed is usually associated with companies that already have some level of validation and are building the product and business. A Series A tends to occur when there is a more consistent set of evidence around product, market, and growth capacity. The name of the round, however, does not explain the company's stage by itself. You need to look at the context, metrics, and fundraising objective.

Thus, the question that started with “how much money do we need?” evolves into “who can invest, at what stage, and why?” And once that investor is found, a new question arises: if someone put money into the company, what exactly did that person come to own?

To see if you're following: a startup is still validating its product, has little traction, and needs capital to continue experimenting. What type of investor and round stage would make sense in this context? And why would it be inappropriate to conclude that every company receiving VC investment is necessarily in a Series A?

## N8. Talking About Ownership

After understanding who invests in the company, the next question is what those people come to own. Equity is an ownership interest, meaning a portion of the company. When a founder owns 40% of a company, they hold an economic and ownership interest corresponding to that percentage, considering the structure and rights associated with it.

To visualize who owns what, we use the cap table, short for capitalization table. It represents the company's ownership structure and can include founders, investors, employees with equity or options, and other holders of rights related to the company. In practice, it is a map of how ownership in the business is distributed and, depending on how it is presented, may also account for interests that will be acquired or converted in the future.

At the beginning, this structure can be quite simple. If a company has four partners with 25% each, the cap table is straightforward. As investors enter, employee options are created, or new equity issuances occur, it becomes more complex.

A new issuance or conversion of equity can change the percentage held by existing shareholders. That's where dilution comes in. If a founder had 50% before a new issuance and has 40% afterward, their percentage ownership has been diluted.

This does not automatically mean that the founder lost economic value. If the company was worth R$10 million and their 50% stake was worth, approximately, R$5 million, dilution to 40% in a company that became worth R$20 million would mean a stake worth approximately R$8 million. The percentage fell, but the potential economic value increased.

For this reason, ownership should not be analyzed only in terms of percentage. You also need to consider the company's value, the rights associated with different classes of ownership, and the terms of the transaction. A smaller stake in a more valuable company can represent more value than a larger stake in a company worth less.

And the ownership structure doesn't involve only those who already hold equity. In growth companies, there may also be mechanisms for granting future ownership to employees and other professionals. One of them is the option pool, a portion of ownership reserved for future grants of options or other equity incentives. This is where concepts such as stock options, ESOP, vesting, and cliff come in, which we will see later.

To see if you're following: a founder owns 50% of a company valued at R$10 million. After a round, their ownership falls to 40%, but the company becomes valued at R$20 million. Were they necessarily harmed by the dilution? What would you need to analyze beyond the ownership percentage to answer?

## N9. Negotiating a Round

After understanding who participates in the ownership structure, we can understand how a round is structured. This is where valuation, pre-money, post-money, SAFE, and term sheet appear.

Valuation is the assessment of the company's value. In a startup, there isn't necessarily an objective value that can be directly observed in the market. The valuation depends on factors such as stage, growth, market, risk, traction, comparable companies, and, above all, the terms of the negotiation itself.

Pre-money is the company's valuation before the new investment. Post-money is the valuation considered after the new capital enters. In a simple example, if a company has a pre-money valuation of R$20 million and receives R$5 million in investment, the post-money would be R$25 million, assuming a simple structure in which the new capital is fully incorporated into the post-investment value.

These concepts help understand what percentage the new investor will receive in a round. In the example, the R$5 million invested represents 20% of the R$25 million post-money valuation. The investors would therefore own approximately 20% of the company after the transaction, assuming this simplified structure.

Not every fundraising, however, immediately defines the investor's ownership. SAFE, Simple Agreement for Future Equity, is an instrument through which the investor provides capital to the company in exchange for the right to receive equity in the future, according to certain events and conditions established in the instrument. Instead of immediately establishing a purchase of ownership at a determined valuation, the SAFE allows the conversion to happen later, normally tied to events defined in the contract.

A SAFE can establish mechanisms such as a valuation cap and discount, which influence the conversion terms. A valuation cap establishes a valuation limit used to determine the conversion of the investment, while a discount grants the investor a discount relative to the price per share defined in a future round, according to the instrument's rules. The important point at this level is to understand the central idea: the money enters now and the ownership can be defined later according to the established rules.

Before the definitive documents, there is often a term sheet. It summarizes the main proposed terms of the transaction, such as valuation, investment, rights, and other relevant conditions. It is not simply a sales presentation, but the document that consolidates the main points under negotiation and serves as the basis for formalizing the transaction.

When someone says “we're discussing the term sheet for the next round,” therefore, the conversation is no longer about a generic idea of raising money. The parties are negotiating the specific conditions under which the investment may happen. At this point, understanding only how much money comes in and what percentage will be given away is no longer enough. You also need to understand how ownership will be distributed among founders, investors, and employees over time.

To see if you're following: a company has a pre-money valuation of R$20 million and receives R$5 million in a round. What would the post-money valuation be and, in a simple structure, what percentage of the company would the new investor own? And if the investment were made through a SAFE, why might that ownership not be defined immediately?

## N10. Building Long-Term Ownership

Capital is not the only way to structure ownership. In growth companies, especially those that depend on key people, it is also common to discuss stock options, ESOP, vesting, and cliff.

A stock option is the right to acquire ownership in the company under certain conditions. It is not necessarily the same thing as already owning the shares. The option establishes a future right, subject to the rules of the plan and applicable conditions.

ESOP, Employee Stock Option Plan, is the plan that organizes the granting of options to purchase ownership to employees. The logic is to align part of employees' compensation or incentives with the value the company may create over the long term.

Vesting is the process through which that right is acquired progressively. Instead of someone receiving the entire stake on the first day, it can be earned over a certain period. This creates a link between continued employment, contribution, and economic ownership.

Cliff is the initial period during which no ownership is acquired. A common example is a four-year plan with a one-year cliff. In this case, the person does not acquire ownership during the first twelve months and, upon completing the first year, begins acquiring the first portion, followed by the rest of the vesting schedule. The specific terms vary depending on the plan.

There is also a word that can appear in different contexts and cause confusion: partnership. In certain organizations, especially investment banks, asset managers, and professional services firms, the term can refer to a structure in which certain executives are also owners or partners in the business. The logic is to align management and long-term economic ownership. It is not simply a commercial partnership between two companies.

With this, the ownership structure begins to become more complete. The cap table shows not only who the current shareholders are, but may need to account for investors, employee options, and rights that will be acquired over time. Ownership, therefore, is not necessarily static: it can be created, diluted, acquired progressively, and eventually transformed into value for its holders.

This is where exit comes in, an event involving liquidity or realization of value for investors and other shareholders. The best-known paths are an acquisition, a merger, or an IPO, although the specific form of exit and value realization depends on the company, market, and transaction structure.

For someone entering Startup Speak, this is the point where many of the previous words finally connect. The company was created by founders, identified a problem, and developed a solution. It built a product, sought PMF, acquired customers, tracked retention, CAC, and LTV, found ways to grow, raised capital, brought in investors, and distributed ownership. Throughout this process, the cap table changed, new rights were created, and different people gained exposure to the value of the business. The exit represents one of the ways in which this value can finally be realized.

This also explains why so many financial and ownership terms are part of the everyday startup vocabulary. Valuation, equity, dilution, cap table, vesting, and exit are not subjects separate from the business. They are different ways of describing how the value created by the company is financed, distributed, shared, and eventually realized.

To see if you're following: a startup received investment, granted stock options to employees, and, over time, completed new rounds that changed the cap table. Years later, the company is acquired. What concepts would you use to explain who participates in that value, how that ownership was built over time, and what can happen to it at the time of the exit?

## Answer Key for the End-of-Level Tests

If you've made it this far, you've already gone through the main blocks of Startup Speak. Before wrapping up the guide, it's worth checking whether you were able to connect the concepts presented throughout the levels.

### N1. First Words

Not necessarily. Being newly created and selling subscription software to consumers does not automatically make a company a startup. You need to consider the nature of the business and the pursuit of a repeatable and scalable model. In this case, if the company sells directly to end consumers, it is B2C. Whoever buys is the customer and, if the same person uses the product, customer and user coincide.

### N2. Starting to Talk

An MVP makes more sense than immediately building the complete product because it allows hypotheses to be tested with less cost and time. If the first tests show that customers do not behave as expected, the company needs to analyze the evidence to understand which fundamental hypothesis is wrong. If the discovery requires a significant change in product or business strategy, a pivot may make sense. The business model also needs to be revisited when the learning calls into question how the company intends to create, deliver, or capture value.

### N3. Everyday Life

Not necessarily. The problem may be further down the acquisition process. If many users reach the product but do not reach activation and new cohorts show increasingly lower retention, it is necessary to investigate the funnel and understand where the user stops perceiving value. Increasing acquisition may simply put more people into a process that already has activation and retention problems.

### N4. Starting to Work

No. MRR growth, by itself, is not enough to say that the business is healthy. You need to analyze the economics of that growth, including CAC, LTV, gross margin, and payback. If each new customer costs a lot, generates little margin, and takes a long time to recover the investment, growth may be consuming cash rather than creating value.

### N5. Talking About Growth

Before simply increasing acquisition spending, it would be necessary to understand the GTM problem. The company may have a good product and still not have found an efficient way to reach the customer. The choice between Sales-led and Product-led also depends on the nature of the product, ticket size, sales complexity, and how the customer perceives value. Growth is the consequence of the combination of these elements, not simply of spending more on acquisition.

### N6. Talking About Money

It is not advisable to wait until the runway runs out. Fundraising can take time and involve negotiation and due diligence, so the company needs to consider its cash horizon before reaching the limit. In addition to burn rate and runway, it would be necessary to assess growth, expense trends, cash-generation capacity, the need for new investments, and capital market conditions. It would also be necessary to consider whether the company can finance growth through its own operations, through bootstrapping, or whether it needs external capital. The decision is both financial and strategic.

### N7. Entering the World of Investors

A very early-stage company, with little traction and in the process of validating its product, may be more aligned with early-stage investors, such as angel investors, and a Pre-seed or Seed round, depending on the business's maturity. This is not a rigid rule. A Series A normally presupposes a more consistent set of evidence around product, market, and growth capacity. The name of the round alone does not determine the company's stage.

### N8. Talking About Ownership

Not necessarily. In the example, the ownership percentage fell from 50% to 40%, but its approximate economic value increased from R$5 million to R$8 million. To evaluate the effect of dilution, you need to look not only at the percentage, but also at the company's valuation, the rights associated with the ownership, and the terms of the transaction. In addition, the cap table may include an option pool and future ownership, which also need to be considered to understand how the ownership structure is distributed. Percentage dilution does not automatically mean destruction of value.

### N9. Negotiating a Round

The post-money valuation would be R$25 million, resulting from R$20 million in pre-money valuation plus R$5 million in investment. In a simple structure, the R$5 million represents 20% of the post-money valuation, so the new investor would own approximately 20% of the company after the round. In a SAFE, however, ownership may not be defined immediately. The investment enters first and converts into equity later, according to the events and conditions established in the instrument. Mechanisms such as valuation caps and discounts can influence the terms of that conversion.

### N10. Building Long-Term Ownership

You need to look at the cap table and the rights associated with each ownership interest. Investors may hold equity acquired in a round; employees may have stock options subject to vesting and cliff; an option pool may reserve ownership for future grants; new rounds may cause dilution; and an exit may allow different shareholders to realize the value of their ownership. Therefore, to understand who participates in the proceeds of an acquisition, it is not enough to look at who the current shareholders are. You need to understand how ownership was structured, acquired, and changed over the life of the company.

If you were able to answer these questions without going back to the definitions, you are probably no longer just translating words from Startup Speak. You can already follow the logic behind a conversation about a startup, from the problem and product through growth, capital, ownership, and value realization.

## What to Do Next

After going through these levels, a sentence like “we're fundraising, we have eight months of runway, and we're evaluating a SAFE” no longer sounds like an arbitrary sequence of English words. It describes a concrete situation: the company is raising capital, has a certain financial horizon, and is evaluating a structure for receiving investment.

That's when vocabulary begins to fulfill its function. Words stop being barriers and become shortcuts to concepts.

Startup Speak is not a collection of difficult terms. It is the language used to describe a particular way of building, operating, financing, and organizing companies. The better we understand the relationships between the words, the less we depend on memorizing definitions and the more we can follow the conversation.

And, as with any language, after the first words comes practice. You will still encounter terms you don't know, expressions that seem strange, and people talking too fast. That's part of it. The important thing is to recognize the context, understand the structure of the conversation, and figure out the meaning of whatever is missing.

You don't need to speak Startup Speak fluently. You just need to be able to follow the conversation, understand what is being discussed, and know where to look when a new word appears.
