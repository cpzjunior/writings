Introduction: AI is changing the economics of software development by substantially reducing the cost and time required to turn decisions into working software, raising questions about whether Agile practices and frameworks should change. While one view sees AI as a reason to rethink Agile and another sees Agile principles as increasingly important, a third possibility is that AI changes the conditions that historically made waterfall processes difficult to adapt. Waterfall and Agile have traditionally been contrasted as sequential planning versus short feedback cycles because long implementation times made late discovery of incorrect assumptions expensive. If AI reduces implementation from weeks or months to hours or days, detailed planning may coexist with rapid validation. This does not make waterfall and Agile equivalent or eliminate changing requirements, dependencies, or mistaken decisions; rather, it changes the cost of discovering and correcting them. The central question therefore becomes less “Agile or waterfall?” and more how much time separates a decision from the point at which reality can reveal that the decision was wrong.

The Historical Problem with Waterfall: The fundamental problem with waterfall is not planning, specification, or predictability themselves, since these remain useful or necessary in many contexts, but the long interval between making a decision and validating it. When systems take months to specify, build, and test, incorrect assumptions may remain hidden until substantial effort has been invested, increasing accumulated risk and the cost of correction. Agile principles emerged partly as a response by reducing cycle size, delivering working software earlier, obtaining customer feedback, and using that information to revise subsequent decisions. In this sense, agility is better understood as learning speed than execution speed: the objective is to reduce the time during which the organization must rely on unvalidated assumptions. AI becomes relevant because if technology substantially lowers the cost and time required to validate what has been planned, it may alter the economic conditions that historically favored incremental approaches.

What Does It Mean to Be Agile?: Agile should be distinguished from specific practices and frameworks such as Scrum, Kanban, XP, Planning Poker, story points, sprints, and dailies, because these are mechanisms for implementing broader principles rather than the principles themselves. A practice can become obsolete when technology provides a better way to achieve its intended outcome without making Agile itself obsolete. Likewise, long-term roadmaps, detailed specifications, estimates, and organizational planning are compatible with agility when they remain subject to revision rather than being treated as certainty or immutable commitments. The key question is how quickly the organization can discover whether its plans are working and adapt when evidence contradicts them. AI may reduce the cost of turning decisions into software enough to allow more detailed upfront planning while preserving short validation cycles, shifting the question from whether to plan before learning toward how quickly planning and learning can occur together.

AI Is Reducing the Cost of Turning Decisions into Software: Generative AI is reducing not only the cost of writing code but also the broader cost of transforming intentions into technical artifacts such as tests, documentation, queries, scripts, configurations, and prototypes. This can make human implementation capacity less of a bottleneck, but it does not automatically reduce the cost of discovering whether the underlying problem or solution is correct. Faster implementation can therefore produce incorrect answers faster if user needs and problem definitions remain unclear. In relatively stable, regulated, or compliance-heavy environments with predictable requirements, detailed specification can be valuable, and AI may allow those specifications to be converted into functional systems quickly enough to enable rapid validation. This creates the possibility of combining prescriptive planning with short feedback cycles: the distinction between waterfall and Agile does not disappear, but one historical source of their separation—the long delay between specification and observable software—can become substantially cheaper.

Agile Is Not an Absence of Planning: Agile does not mean starting development without direction or abandoning planning, processes, forecasts, priorities, capacity management, dependencies, or roadmaps. Its distinction lies in recognizing that plans are based on incomplete knowledge and must be revised as new information emerges. Estimates are similarly useful when treated as tools for managing uncertainty rather than mechanisms for creating false certainty or commitments. AI can change the mechanics of planning without eliminating its purpose: for example, it could analyze historical performance, dependencies, complexity, and prior implementations to produce forecasts instead of relying on manual estimation practices such as Planning Poker, while humans remain responsible for interpreting assumptions and making decisions. Similar changes could affect Scrum preparation, XP testing and refactoring, and Kanban flow analysis. As AI automates information production and routine verification, ceremonies may shift toward discussing decisions, risks, exceptions, and evidence rather than manually producing information. Testing may shift from repetitive verification toward exploration, while code review may shift from detecting implementation defects toward questioning whether the underlying solution should exist. Some Agile practices may therefore become unnecessary without making Agile principles unnecessary. If detailed specifications can be converted into functional software within hours and rapidly reviewed with users, a process traditionally associated with waterfall could achieve iteration cycles comparable to sprints without adopting the same planning model as Agile.

AIs Did Not Come to Replace Testers, but to Unlock Their True Potential: As AI accelerates software production, verification can become a bottleneck, but simply increasing manual testing would not fully exploit the new economics. AI is well suited to scalable, repeatable verification of known behaviors, including regression testing, test-case generation, repeated execution, and comparison of expected and observed results. Human testers provide a different capability: discovering failures that were not anticipated, especially unusual feature interactions, unexpected sequences, ambiguous user interpretations, and behaviors absent from the specification. AI can therefore expand automated coverage while allowing testers to spend more time on exploratory testing, hypothesis generation, and investigation of unknown failure modes. This changes testing from a discrete post-development phase into an activity distributed throughout development, where automation continuously checks formalized expectations while humans investigate whether those expectations were sufficient. At the extreme, AI tests what the organization already knows how to test, while testers concentrate on discovering what the organization does not yet know it should test; the former scales primarily with computing resources, while the latter depends more heavily on experience, curiosity, and the ability to formulate unexpected hypotheses.

The Backlog and Roadmap Become Organized Around ROI: If AI dramatically increases software production capacity, planning based primarily on how much a team can deliver becomes less useful because greater implementation capacity does not imply that more software should be built. Prioritization shifts toward determining which problems, risks, opportunities, or hypotheses deserve investment, including considerations such as affected users, cost reduction, revenue potential, risk reduction, and the value of validating an uncertain assumption. This does not imply that every initiative can or should have a precise ROI calculation, since technical debt, security, reliability, observability, and strategic capabilities can have indirect or difficult-to-quantify returns, but initiatives should still have an explicit rationale concerning the problem addressed, risk reduced, or opportunity pursued. AI also lowers the opportunity cost of experimenting with new ideas, creating a risk that organizations produce large amounts of software that is easy to build but difficult to justify. The resulting bottleneck may shift from execution capacity to prioritization capacity, making productivity less about maximizing output and more about selecting valuable work. The same principle applies to automation: lower implementation costs do not eliminate maintenance, infrastructure, observability, or evolution costs, so cheaper construction increases rather than removes the need for disciplined decisions about what not to build. The backlog can therefore evolve from a queue constrained primarily by team capacity into a portfolio of competing opportunities, risks, and hypotheses, while the roadmap increasingly addresses which problems deserve investment first.

Code Review Is Not Just About Fixing Code: As AI lowers the cost of producing technically correct software, code review may need to focus less exclusively on implementation defects and more on whether the engineering decisions behind the implementation are justified. AI can efficiently turn a poor architectural choice into sophisticated, tested, documented code, including unnecessary abstraction layers, dependencies, infrastructure, or complexity that may pass technical checks while still creating long-term maintenance costs. Because the cost of constructing complexity falls faster than the cost of living with complexity, reviewers increasingly need to ask not only whether code works but whether the architecture, dependencies, abstractions, scope, and overall solution should exist. This changes the relative importance of seniority: technical knowledge remains necessary for understanding consequences, but experience increasingly manifests in recognizing when sophisticated technologies or architectures are unnecessary. Review should also remain non-hierarchical, since simple questions from less experienced engineers can expose unjustified complexity and make assumptions explicit. In this model, code review becomes a collective decision-quality mechanism rather than merely a defect-detection mechanism, with engineering value shifting from producing correct code toward preventing technically sound but strategically or architecturally poor decisions from becoming systems.

Go-Live Is Also Part of Development: Agile and more prescriptive processes traditionally differ partly in how they treat discovery: Agile emphasizes frequent exposure of working software to customers, while waterfall-like processes place greater emphasis on validating requirements and acceptance criteria before production. Go-live, however, can be treated not as the final validation point but as the beginning of an assisted-production phase in which a functional system is exposed to a limited audience, observed under real conditions, and adjusted according to evidence. Production can reveal not only defects but incorrect interpretations of user behavior, excessive complexity, or product hypotheses that were wrong even when the implementation correctly followed its specification. AI can make this feedback loop cheaper by reducing the time required to transform detailed specifications into deployable systems, allowing organizations to reach real-world evidence earlier and adjust before expanding commitment. Under this model, a prescriptive process can retain substantial upfront planning while incorporating iterative learning after go-live; the first production release becomes the beginning of an evidence-gathering phase rather than necessarily the completion of the solution.

When Execution Stops Being the Main Bottleneck: AI can increasingly automate estimation, analysis, planning support, testing, documentation, metrics analysis, and other mechanical activities involved in turning decisions into software, but it does not eliminate the need to decide what should be built, which problem matters, what risks are acceptable, what complexity is justified, or how to respond when reality contradicts expectations. As execution becomes cheaper, judgment becomes relatively more important: cheaper development increases the need to choose valuable problems, cheaper testing increases the need to decide what deserves exploration, cheaper estimation increases the need to understand uncertainties outside the estimate, cheaper deployment increases the need to interpret real user behavior, and cheaper architectural implementation increases the need to recognize when simpler solutions are sufficient. This also reinforces the distinction between Agile principles and the practices historically used to implement them; if AI provides better mechanisms for solving the problems addressed by sprints, story points, Planning Poker, Scrum, XP, or Kanban practices, those practices need not be preserved merely because they are traditional. AI can make detailed specifications, long-term roadmaps, and prescriptive processes compatible with relatively rapid feedback, while production can become part of the learning cycle. The result is not the conversion of waterfall into Agile, but a less rigid boundary between the two because processes with substantial upfront planning may now be able to achieve learning cycles measured in days or weeks rather than months.

Conclusion: The central issue is the time required to discover that a decision was wrong. AI changes the cost structure of software development by potentially reducing the interval between planning, implementation, deployment, and evidence, which may allow organizations to plan in greater detail without necessarily sacrificing rapid learning. This does not eliminate the distinction between waterfall and Agile, nor does it make either model universally interchangeable with the other; instead, it expands the range of processes that can potentially operate with short feedback cycles. Planning can remain detailed if it is not confused with certainty, roadmaps can remain long-term if they are revisable, specifications can remain extensive if their consequences can be validated quickly, and go-live can remain significant without representing the end of learning. The underlying engineering problem remains unchanged: discovering as early as possible whether the organization is developing something worth developing.
