AI substantially reduces the cost and time of turning software decisions into functional artifacts, potentially weakening the historical economic distinction between waterfall and Agile. Waterfall is not inherently problematic because it plans, specifies, or seeks predictability; its central historical weakness is the long interval between a decision and meaningful validation. Agile reduces this interval through shorter build–feedback cycles. If AI can reduce implementation from weeks or months to hours or days, more prescriptive planning may coexist with rapid validation. This does not make waterfall and Agile equivalent; it changes the cost structure that previously made detailed up-front planning and rapid learning difficult to combine.

Agility is better understood as learning speed than execution speed or adherence to Scrum, Kanban, XP, sprints, story points, Planning Poker, or dailies. These practices are mechanisms for achieving broader goals and can become obsolete when technology provides better mechanisms. Planning remains necessary for budgets, capacity, dependencies, hiring, communication, regulatory constraints, roadmaps, and priorities. An agile approach does not reject planning; it rejects treating an initial plan as sufficiently certain to remain unchanged despite new information. Specifications can be detailed without being treated as final, estimates can guide decisions without becoming promises, and roadmaps can persist while their hypotheses are continuously revised. The key question is how quickly planning can be confronted with reality.

Generative AI lowers the cost of transforming intentions into code, tests, documentation, queries, scripts, configurations, prototypes, and other technical artifacts, but faster implementation does not imply better understanding of the underlying problem. It can accelerate reaching a wrong answer. This distinction makes AI particularly relevant to contexts with relatively stable requirements, strong compliance constraints, low volatility, and limited need for user experimentation: detailed business rules, integrations, regulatory requirements, and acceptance criteria can be specified in advance, while AI may allow the resulting system to become functional quickly enough for validation. Thus, extensive planning and short feedback cycles can coexist without requiring the two methodologies to become identical.

AI can alter individual Agile practices without invalidating their underlying purposes. Estimates could be generated from historical delivery data, analogous work, dependencies, affected components, complexity, and prior durations instead of manually produced through Planning Poker; the team would still need to examine assumptions and uncertainty. Scrum preparation and estimation can be automated, XP practices can be accelerated through AI-generated tests and refactoring assistance, and Kanban can use models to analyze flow, bottlenecks, WIP, waiting, and rework. Meetings can shift from producing information to discussing decisions, risks, assumptions, and exceptions. Reviews may become more valuable as implementation becomes cheaper because the risk of building unnecessary software increases. The principle is to preserve effective decision-making, feedback, and adaptation rather than preserving historical rituals.

Testing illustrates a changing division of labor. As AI increases implementation capacity, verification can become a bottleneck. Automated systems are well suited to predictable, repeatable verification: regression testing, large numbers of scenarios, generated test cases, repeated executions, and comparison of expected and observed behavior. Human testers provide different value by exploring situations that were not anticipated: unusual sequences, interactions between apparently independent behaviors, interruptions, unexpected user interpretations, and combinations absent from the specification. AI can therefore expand formalized test coverage while allowing testers to concentrate on exploratory discovery. Quality becomes a continuous activity throughout development rather than a stage after implementation: machines verify what is already known to test, while humans investigate what may not yet have been recognized as needing a test.

As AI expands delivery capacity, prioritization becomes more important. Traditional backlog management often begins with team capacity: determine how much can be delivered and select work that fits. If implementation capacity increases dramatically, the constraint shifts toward deciding which problems deserve investment. Backlogs and roadmaps can therefore be organized more around expected value, risk, affected users, cost reduction, revenue opportunities, or hypotheses requiring validation, rather than primarily around available sprint capacity. Not every investment has a precisely measurable ROI; technical debt, security, observability, reliability, and future-change preparation can have indirect returns. Nevertheless, initiatives should be justifiable in terms of the problem addressed, risk reduced, or opportunity pursued. When prototypes and features become cheap to produce, the organization can suffer from excessive production: execution capacity may exceed prioritization capacity. Greater ability to build therefore increases the importance of deciding what not to build.

The same economic principle applies to automation. Lower implementation cost does not make every automation worthwhile because maintenance, infrastructure, observability, and evolution remain costs. If AI makes software cheap to produce, productivity should not be equated with software volume. Otherwise, organizations can accelerate waste rather than value.

Code review also shifts from primarily detecting implementation defects toward evaluating engineering decisions. AI can efficiently turn poor decisions into technically sophisticated systems: unnecessary abstraction, excessive architecture, disproportionate infrastructure, or needless dependencies can all be implemented quickly while passing tests, linters, and static analysis. The cost of creating complexity falls, but the cost of living with that complexity does not necessarily fall proportionally. Future engineers still need to understand, debug, document, modify, and justify it. Therefore, code review must ask not only whether the implementation is correct but whether the underlying decision is justified.

This increases the relative importance of engineering judgment. Technical knowledge remains necessary for understanding consequences, but AI reduces the relative value of merely knowing how to use technologies because models can explain libraries and generate implementations. Experience increasingly manifests in recognizing when a technology, architecture, or abstraction should not be used. Code review should also remain non-hierarchical: junior engineers can expose unjustified complexity through simple questions such as why an abstraction or layer is necessary. Review becomes a collective mechanism for making decisions explicit, sharing knowledge, and preventing technically correct but strategically poor systems. AI can write the solution; engineering remains responsible for deciding whether the solution should exist.

Go-live can likewise become part of the learning cycle rather than its endpoint. Prescriptive processes traditionally emphasize sufficiently defined and validated requirements before production, while Agile emphasizes discovering through working software. Production does not have to represent final completion. A functional release can reach a limited user group, be closely monitored, and generate evidence about actual behavior. Users may reveal not only bugs but incorrect assumptions about interpretation, workflow complexity, or product hypotheses. A system can behave exactly according to its specification while the specification itself is wrong. AI can make this production-learning loop cheaper by rapidly converting detailed specifications into deployable implementations and enabling rapid subsequent adjustments. Thus, a process can remain prescriptive before release while treating the first production deployment as the beginning of assisted production rather than the end of discovery.

As execution becomes cheaper, engineering value shifts toward judgment. If development becomes cheap, selecting what to develop becomes more important. If testing becomes cheap, selecting what deserves exploration becomes more important. If estimation becomes cheap, identifying what the estimate fails to capture becomes more important. If production deployment becomes cheap, interpreting real user behavior becomes more important. If sophisticated architectures become cheap, recognizing when simplicity is sufficient becomes more important. AI reduces mechanical execution costs without eliminating the need to decide what problem matters, what risk is acceptable, what complexity is justified, and how to respond when reality contradicts expectations.

The distinction between Agile and its operational practices is therefore important. Agile does not inherently require sprints, story points, Planning Poker, or Scrum. These practices emerged to solve particular problems in particular contexts. If technology provides better mechanisms, preserving them for tradition is unnecessary. The enduring principles are rapid access to working software, meaningful interaction and collaboration, responsiveness to change, and continuous confrontation of decisions with reality.

Under these conditions, AI can make some prescriptive processes compatible with feedback cycles that previously required incremental development. A detailed specification, long-term roadmap, and substantial up-front planning can coexist with rapid functional implementation and validation. Production can become the start of an assisted learning phase. A process historically associated with waterfall might operate in days or weeks rather than months, potentially approaching sprint-scale feedback cycles. This does not transform waterfall into Agile; it expands the range of process designs capable of supporting rapid learning.

The fundamental criterion is therefore not simply how much planning occurs, whether a roadmap exists, or which methodology is named. Planning can remain detailed without being confused with certainty; roadmaps can remain long-term without becoming immutable commitments; specifications can remain extensive if their consequences can be validated quickly; and go-live can remain a major delivery event without being treated as the end of learning. The central measure of agility is the elapsed time between making a decision and discovering that the decision was wrong.

AI changes that interval by reducing the cost of converting decisions into observable software. If months can become days or weeks, the historical need to choose between detailed planning and rapid learning becomes weaker. A process can remain highly prescriptive in its initial definition while remaining agile in the practical sense that it can rapidly discover incorrect assumptions and correct them. The technology, tools, and economics of software production change, but the fundamental engineering problem remains: discover as early as possible whether the organization is developing something actually worth developing.
