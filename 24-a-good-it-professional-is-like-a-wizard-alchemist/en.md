# The Good IT Professional Is Like a Wizard-Alchemist

_Working with technology requires knowing how to apply what has already been established, reuse experiences, and produce new knowledge._

**Summary:** I see the good IT professional as a wizard-alchemist: someone who knows how to draw on established knowledge, reuse experiences, and recognize when that is not enough. When the answer is not in the grimoire, it is necessary to go to the workbench, formulate hypotheses, experiment, and transform uncertainty into knowledge. But discovering is not enough: what has been learned must be recorded and shared so that individual experience becomes collective knowledge. In the end, excellence in IT is not about choosing between applying and discovering, but knowing how to move between the two.

---

Whenever I start a new game, one of the first things I try to understand is its magic and alchemy systems. In Skyrim, Ultima Online, The Witcher, Minecraft, and other games in the RPG and sandbox genres, I quickly become interested in what types of magic exist, which spells can be learned, what properties the ingredients have, and which combinations can result in new potions.

Thinking about this, I realized that perhaps there is a reason why I am so attracted to these two systems. They represent two different ways of interacting with a world. Magic is associated with established knowledge. There is a formula, a spell, a technique that can be studied and learned. Once I know that formula, I can reproduce it whenever I need it. The wizard builds their repertoire by accumulating knowledge and learning how to use what has already been discovered.

Alchemy is different. It represents the practical exploration of what is not yet sufficiently understood. There are ingredients, properties, and possibilities, but it is necessary to investigate how they relate to one another. It is necessary to combine, test, observe, and record the results. The alchemist does not merely learn existing recipes; they can also discover new combinations, understand new relationships, and turn those discoveries into recipes that others can reproduce.

It is difficult to say which of the two systems attracts me more. In fact, I think it is precisely the combination that interests me. I like the feeling of mastering a formula I did not know before. But I also like being faced with something for which I do not yet have a formula. I enjoy both the moment when I learn a new spell and the moment when I discover a new combination of ingredients. Perhaps that is why these systems feel so natural to me. One represents accumulated knowledge; the other, the exploration of the unknown.

And thinking about this, I began to notice a curious similarity with the way I like to work with technology. A good IT professional needs to know how to consult the grimoire when the answer already exists. They need to know the recipes that have already been discovered. But when the books do not have the answer to the problem in front of them, they need to know how to go to the workbench.

## The Wizard and the Alchemist

Wizard and alchemist are different figures in games, but they are not opposites. Games usually separate them into distinct classes because they need to turn their activities into different mechanics: the wizard learns and casts spells, while the alchemist gathers ingredients and prepares potions. Outside this division, however, the two are very close. Both study, experiment, learn, accumulate knowledge, and can turn discoveries into systematized knowledge. Both seek to understand the rules of a world and discover how to use them. For this metaphor, therefore, the separation between the two classes is less important than what they have in common. Essentially, we are talking about two manifestations of the same archetype.

The wizard represents mastery of knowledge that has already been systematized. They study formulas, learn spells, understand their principles, and know in which situations to use them. Their power lies in transforming accumulated knowledge into action. When they encounter a known problem, they draw on the knowledge they already possess and apply an appropriate solution. The grimoire represents this organized knowledge: what has already been studied, recorded, and can be consulted when necessary.

The alchemist represents another dimension of this same archetype: the practical exploration of the properties and relationships between ingredients. They observe, combine, separate, test, and record. They may know the individual properties of their ingredients and still not know what will happen when they are combined. Their workbench represents the space where hypotheses can be transformed into experiments and experiments can produce new discoveries. Their alchemy book records not only recipes received from others, but also what has been discovered through their own experience.

This does not mean that the wizard does not experiment or that the alchemist does not have recipes. A wizard can research new spells, test variations of an enchantment, and investigate principles they do not yet fully understand. An alchemist can study existing recipes, consult books, and reproduce experiments performed by other people. Both move between established knowledge and discovery. The difference lies in the emphasis each archetype places on this process: the wizard better represents the mastery and application of existing knowledge; the alchemist, the practical exploration of what is not yet sufficiently understood.

## A World with Too Many Ingredients

The wizard-alchemist archetype could be associated with practically any profession that involves knowledge, experience, and problem-solving. A doctor consults established knowledge and also investigates cases that do not behave as expected. A scientist draws on existing knowledge and produces new results through experimentation. An engineer combines known principles with tests and prototypes. The metaphor, therefore, is not exclusive to IT. But there is a characteristic of working with technology that makes it fit particularly well: the quantity, diversity, and speed of transformation of the tools available.

Programming languages, frameworks, libraries, databases, operating systems, protocols, APIs, managed services, clouds, observability platforms, CI/CD tools, queues, brokers, artificial intelligence models, and countless other technologies can be combined in ways that no one can fully know. New tools emerge, existing technologies change their behavior, and new ways of combining them continuously appear. The space of possibilities grows faster than any individual's ability to master it completely.

There is no grimoire large enough. Even an extremely experienced professional cannot know every available technology in depth, much less every possible combination between them. The problem ceases to be merely learning more and becomes dealing with a space of possibilities larger than what any person can keep in their repertoire.

In this scenario, knowing technology is only part of the skill set. The professional needs to know how to find knowledge they do not possess, assess whether what they found is reliable, recognize when a known solution does not apply to the context, and realize when there is an uncertainty that needs to be investigated. Experience ceases to mean merely accumulating answers and begins to include the ability to navigate what we do not yet know.

The grimoire remains fundamental. Documentation, books, technical articles, architectural patterns, and professional experience make it possible to solve an enormous number of problems without reinventing solutions. The alchemy book adds another layer: experiences and recipes discovered by other professionals, recorded so they can be reproduced and put to use. But when faced with a problem for which neither offers an adequate answer, the workbench remains.

It is this combination that makes the archetype particularly appropriate for IT. Not because other professions do not investigate or experiment, but because few need to simultaneously deal with such an extensive, heterogeneous, and constantly changing repertoire of tools and so many possibilities for combining them. In technology, it is particularly difficult to depend only on what is already in the books.

The IT world has too many ingredients for any professional to know all the recipes. Therefore, the differentiator is not having the largest grimoire or the most complete alchemy book, but knowing when existing knowledge is sufficient, when a previous experience can be reused, and when it will be necessary to go to the workbench to discover something new.

## The Workbench

A world with too many ingredients inevitably produces problems for which the grimoire and the alchemy book have no ready answer. It is at this point that the professional needs to leave the books and go to the workbench. Experimentation is one of the alchemist's most distinctive characteristics, but it is not exclusive to them. A wizard can also research new spells, test variations of an enchantment, or investigate the effects of magic they do not yet fully understand. The difference, within our metaphor, is that alchemy more directly represents this experimental relationship with ingredients and their combinations.

Experimenting, however, does not mean trying things at random until something works. The alchemist works methodically. Before mixing the ingredients, they need to know what question is being asked; during the experiment, they need to control the conditions; afterward, they need to observe the result and understand what it means. A negative result is also knowledge: a hypothesis was discarded, a possibility was eliminated, or a property was better understood.

In technology, a proof of concept serves exactly this purpose. It should not be confused with a small version of the final product. Its purpose is to answer a specific question and reduce uncertainty. If I want to find out whether a particular database supports a specific workload, I do not need to build the entire platform that will eventually use that database. If I want to know whether a library can handle a particular integration, I do not need to develop the entire application around it. If I want to evaluate an architectural decision, I can build only the components necessary to observe the properties that interest me.

For this reason, a good proof of concept can be small, incomplete, inelegant, and even disposable. The code does not need to survive the experiment, because its primary product is knowledge. The goal is to discover whether a hypothesis is true, understand a limitation, compare alternatives, or eliminate a possibility that seemed promising.

This separation between experimentation and delivery is fundamental. Even in the world of magic, researching a new spell can be much more dangerous than testing a combination of ingredients at the workbench. An unknown enchantment can produce effects that are difficult to predict or reverse. Both the wizard and the alchemist therefore need an appropriate space to investigate what they do not yet master.

In technology, the equivalent is to clearly separate what is being used to learn from what will be delivered to users. An experimental solution can demonstrate that something is possible without demonstrating that it is safe, sustainable, observable, scalable, or suitable for delivery. The workbench exists to answer questions; the delivery environment requires a solution that has already gone through a different process of validation and preparation.

This is precisely where the wizard and the alchemist meet in the IT professional. The wizard brings accumulated knowledge to the workbench, helping formulate hypotheses and choose which experiments are worth pursuing. The alchemist puts that knowledge into contact with practice and discovers what was not yet sufficiently understood. The discovery can then be recorded in the grimoire or the alchemy book so that others can reproduce it, question it, and develop it further.

The workbench exists to discover. The delivery environment exists to deliver. The IT professional needs to know how to move between the two without confusing them.

## The Right Magic or Potion for the Problem at Hand

One of the most important characteristics of the wizard-alchemist is knowing that a magic spell or potion is not inherently good or bad. It is appropriate or inappropriate for a particular purpose. In Skyrim, a character with rockjoint will not be cured by a healing spell, no matter how powerful it is, nor by a stamina potion. The problem is not the quality of these solutions; they simply do not treat that condition.

The existence of a functional solution does not mean that it is the solution to the problem we have. This seems obvious in a game and frequently ceases to be obvious in technology. Faced with a slow system, we may immediately start experimenting with caching, parallelism, new machines, or database changes. Faced with an outage, we may increase servers, configure retries, or add failover mechanisms. Faced with a business problem, we may start discussing a new application. In all these cases, we are choosing a magic spell or potion before fully understanding the problem.

The wizard's knowledge is important precisely for diagnosis. It allows them to recognize patterns, understand the context, and formulate hypotheses based on what they already know. What exactly is the problem being presented? What behavior are we trying to explain? What are the constraints? What actually needs to be solved? Only after understanding these questions does it make sense to decide whether a known solution exists or whether experimentation will be necessary.

A professional may know dozens of technologies and still choose the wrong tool because they started with the solution instead of starting with the problem. The fact that a technology is interesting, modern, or technically capable of solving something does not mean that it is appropriate for what we have in front of us. Having a large grimoire and an alchemy book full of experiences and recipes does not mean that all of them are appropriate for every situation.

When the solution is known, the wizard can apply the appropriate magic. When there is a known recipe, the professional can prepare the appropriate potion. When neither provides a satisfactory answer, the alchemist can go to the workbench and experiment. The same professional needs to be capable of making this transition between established knowledge and discovery.

The wizard-alchemist does not begin by asking which spell they can cast or which potion they can prepare. They first seek to understand the problem at hand. Only then do they decide whether to consult the grimoire, turn to the alchemy book, or begin a new experiment.

## When the Grimoire Is Not Enough

There is another situation, different from one in which the knowledge simply does not exist. The grimoire may contain a correct, known, and demonstrably functional answer, but it may still not be the best solution to the problem at hand.

Established knowledge needs to be general enough to apply to different situations. A technique may work very well as a general principle and still produce an inferior result when applied to a specific context. The problem is not the technique, but the distance between the conditions for which it was established and the conditions we encounter in practice.

In technology, this happens frequently. An architecture may be considered a good practice and function correctly, but introduce unnecessary complexity into a particular system. An algorithm may be appropriate for most cases while a more efficient approach still exists for a specific set of data. A technology may be mature and reliable, but have costs or limitations that make another alternative more interesting in that context.

This is where the alchemist becomes important even when the wizard already knows an answer. Experimentation is not only useful for discovering what the grimoire does not know. It can also investigate whether, for that specific context, there is a better solution than the one recommended by established knowledge.

This does not mean abandoning the grimoire. On the contrary: established knowledge provides the starting point and makes it possible to understand what we are modifying. The experienced professional knows what the conventional solution is, why it works, and what its trade-offs are. From there, they can decide whether there is a sufficiently strong reason to experiment with an alternative.

## The Magical Society

A magical society only evolves when its wizards and alchemists exchange information. Knowledge does not belong only to the person who discovered it, nor does it need to remain trapped in the laboratory where it was produced. The wizard consults the grimoire, the alchemist records their experiments in the alchemy book, and both share what they have learned so that other people can start from a more advanced point.

An alchemist discovers a new combination. Another reproduces the experiment and finds a limitation. A third realizes that the ingredient can be combined with another one. A wizard recognizes that the discovery can be applied to a different problem. A fourth turns the knowledge into a tool. Someone documents everything in a wiki. Another person writes a technical article. Eventually, what began as an individual experiment ceases to belong to the person who conducted it and becomes part of collective knowledge.

This is how technology evolves. Documentation, wikis, technical articles, RFCs, talks, books, communities, open source projects, and discussions among professionals are mechanisms through which knowledge produced in different work environments is transmitted, questioned, and improved. They are, in a way, the library of the magical society: a space much larger than any individual grimoire or alchemy book.

Sharing knowledge does not simply mean teaching a recipe. It means allowing other people to reproduce what was discovered, question its assumptions, find its limitations, decompose the solution, and recombine it with other knowledge. An isolated discovery can solve a problem. A shared discovery can generate an entire family of new solutions.

This also changes the way we can think about professional excellence. A good professional should not measure their importance only by the number of things they can do alone or by the amount of knowledge they can keep exclusively in their own head. There is greater value in producing knowledge that remains useful even when they are not present.

The most complete wizard-alchemist is not the one who possesses the most secret grimoire or alchemy book. It is the one who helps the entire library grow.

## Applying, Experimenting, and Learning

Being a wizard-alchemist does not mean experimenting out of curiosity or constantly seeking new technologies. It means knowing how to move between established knowledge and discovery: understanding what already exists, recognizing when it is sufficient, and investigating when it is no longer enough.

This combination also explains why the metaphor brings together two figures that games usually treat as distinct classes. The wizard represents knowledge that can be learned and applied; the alchemist, the practical exploration of what is not yet sufficiently understood. In practice, however, the two processes feed each other. Accumulated knowledge guides investigation, and what is discovered can be incorporated into existing knowledge.

Perhaps this combination is what makes these systems so interesting. There is a different kind of satisfaction in learning a formula and discovering a new one. One expands what we know how to do; the other expands what we know.

In IT, this dynamic appears all the time. The professional needs to recognize patterns, consult established knowledge, reuse previous experiences, and, when that is not enough, formulate hypotheses and experiment. They also need to transform what they learn into knowledge that can be reproduced, shared, and used by other people.

In the end, the wizard-alchemist is not the one who chooses between the grimoire, the alchemy book, or the workbench. It is the one who understands how they complement one another. The grimoire makes it possible to start from the knowledge that already exists; the alchemy book preserves experiences that can be reused; the workbench makes it possible to produce what we do not yet know.

In technology, knowledge is not only what is already in the books. It is also what we learn by experimenting, recording, and sharing. Working well means knowing how to do both: make use of what has already been discovered and continue discovering.
