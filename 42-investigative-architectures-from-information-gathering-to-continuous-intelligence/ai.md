While writing “The Anti-Mage: A Literary Project,” the author began reflecting on investigation itself—following clues, forming hypotheses, connecting events, and allowing answers to generate new questions—and transferred this idea from fiction into product architecture. The proposed system begins with a business question rather than merely an entity to monitor and conducts an evolving investigation across news, databases, corporate information, social media, and other sources. It can discover related entities, open new investigative paths, resolve conflicting evidence, strengthen or weaken hypotheses, and eventually conclude that evidence is insufficient, placing the investigation into monitoring until new information appears. When it resumes, it retains its prior hypotheses, evidence, sources, and uncertainties rather than starting over. The same model could support investigations of suppliers, competitors, technologies, customers, markets, or strategic hypotheses, making the persistent investigation—not the search or agent—the fundamental unit of the system.

Monitoring differs from investigation because a signal does not by itself establish what happened, why it matters, or whether it should affect a decision. A SOC illustrates this distinction: it correlates logs, alerts, and other events, investigates their context, evaluates severity, and determines whether a response is warranted, because an alert is not necessarily an incident. The same logic can apply to public information, where individual complaints, posts, videos, or reports may become meaningful when correlated over time and context. Search consequently becomes part of an investigative loop rather than a terminal operation: a news story can lead to an investigation of a company, an acquisition can generate questions about investors or technology, and conflicting information can trigger a search for primary sources. As evidence changes, hypotheses can be discarded or prioritized and new questions can emerge, requiring the architecture to preserve context and determine the next step. This suggests replacing permanently running agents with persistent investigations that have state and receive processing capacity when appropriate.

An investigative architecture can be understood through an operating-system analogy: just as an operating system manages limited CPU capacity across processes by tracking state, priority, interruption, and resumption, an investigative system can manage persistent investigations according to their objectives, uncertainty, priority, and resource budgets. Different investigations may require very different treatment: a quiet supplier may need only periodic checking, a competitor announcing an acquisition may require deeper analysis, an emerging technology may remain low priority until a relevant signal appears, while rapidly developing complaints may require immediate attention. The next action also depends on investigative state, since newly discovered entities, conflicting evidence, corporate changes, or duplicated sources can each create different investigative needs. The scheduler therefore does not merely choose which search to execute; it chooses which investigation deserves attention and what action is most useful within it. Investigations can be interrupted, resumed, blocked while awaiting evidence, generate sub-investigations, or close individual lines of inquiry, allowing agentic capacity to be shared rather than permanently assigned to individual problems. Continuous automation consequently becomes the dynamic management of investigations rather than thousands of agents polling the Internet on fixed schedules, with the scheduler becoming the mechanism that turns persistent investigations into a continuously operating system.

Because investigation consumes resources—including API calls, searches, processing, storage, inference, agent time, and potentially paid data sources—the architecture must determine not only what to investigate but how much effort is justified. The proposed “ROI” pun reframes the question as whether an investigation is worthwhile: what is the probability that additional information will change the conclusion, what would the impact of such a change be, and what would obtaining that information cost? When evidence is already consistent, uncertainty is low, and further searches are unlikely to affect a decision, the system can reduce frequency, enter monitoring mode, or close the investigation; when a single additional fact could materially change an important decision, another investigative cycle may have high value. This supports an investigative budget that directs capacity toward areas with greater potential information gain while reducing effort where marginal returns have declined. The optimal strategy will not necessarily be known in advance, so heuristics can be adjusted according to observed evidence yield, cost, and stopping points. Intelligence therefore includes not only discovering more information but determining when additional investigation is unlikely to generate sufficient value.

An investigation needs structured memory rather than a collection of documents. It should preserve competing hypotheses, distinguish evidence from inference, and avoid prematurely selecting a narrative that merely confirms an initial assumption. Different explanations for a sequence of criticisms, for example, may include a genuine operational problem, an isolated issue receiving disproportionate attention, coordinated activity, or unrelated events being grouped together; the system should seek evidence capable of distinguishing among these possibilities. It must also account for source independence, since multiple outlets repeating the same underlying story do not constitute independent evidence, and conclusions should distinguish “no relevant signal was found within the searched source perimeter” from proof that something does not exist. Continuity requires preserving the investigation’s state: its question, hypotheses, evidence, sources, discarded possibilities, unresolved questions, and latest conclusion. That state must also incorporate organizational context because the significance of an entity or event depends on the organization’s relationships, policies, classifications, preferred sources, previous decisions, and other domain-specific knowledge. Human corrections—such as correcting an entity relationship, challenging an inference, rejecting a hypothesis, or identifying more relevant evidence—should persist beyond the individual investigation and influence future work. This does not necessarily require retraining the underlying model: the architecture itself can learn organizational knowledge, source usefulness, investigative patterns, and heuristics while the model remains unchanged. The system therefore learns not only about the external world but also from the organization and about how that organization investigates the world.

The autonomy envisioned here primarily concerns conducting investigations rather than independently making consequential business or legal decisions. The system may autonomously select sources, deepen hypotheses, create sub-investigations, adjust effort, seek additional evidence, and resume paused investigations, while decisions such as terminating a business relationship or rejecting a supplier may require human approval depending on their impact. Enterprise deployments therefore need configurable autonomy levels, from observation and alerting through recommendation with human approval and, where appropriate, tightly governed automation. Greater autonomy also requires explicit representation of uncertainty and an investigative trail capable of reconstructing the process behind a conclusion. Such a trail should record the original question, hypotheses, sources, evidence considered or rejected, organizational context, intermediate conclusions, reasons for subsequent searches, and the path to the final recommendation, while also retaining human corrections and later outcomes. Auditability is consequently not merely a compliance mechanism but part of intelligence quality: understanding what the system did, why it did it, and which evidence it relied upon becomes increasingly important as autonomous investigation influences decisions.

The proposed architecture should be understood as a recombination of capabilities that already exist in the market rather than as an entirely new technological category. Competitive and market intelligence platforms already provide capabilities including source aggregation, validation, AI-assisted search and analysis, knowledge management, and enterprise integration, while other solutions provide continuous monitoring and insight generation across competitors, markets, and strategic entities; Brazilian companies likewise operate in competitive intelligence, monitoring, market intelligence, reputation, and information analysis. The potentially distinctive proposition is therefore to organize these capabilities around investigation as the primary unit. Instead of beginning with an entity and asking what happened to it, the system begins with a question and determines which entities, sources, events, and relationships need investigation. Search becomes an operation inside an evolving investigative process rather than an isolated execution producing a report, while the report ceases to be the endpoint and the decision becomes a state that can be revisited when new evidence may alter it. The architecture could connect existing capabilities through persistent investigations that dynamically adjust depth, prioritize resources, incorporate organizational knowledge, and learn from feedback, while adaptation to the Brazilian context could involve public sources, corporate structures, regulation, market characteristics, and governance requirements as components of the system’s intelligence rather than merely localization.

Taken together, these ideas describe an architecture in which information serves as the input to a persistent investigation that formulates questions, tracks hypotheses, preserves context, chooses subsequent actions, and returns to the problem when new evidence appears. AI supplies investigative capabilities, while the surrounding architecture supplies continuity, memory, organizational context, resource management, and governance. The central design question consequently shifts from which model to use toward how the investigation process itself should be organized. Heuristics can initially determine behavior and later be refined through experience as the system learns which sources and investigative paths produce useful evidence and when additional work ceases to generate sufficient value. This represents a progression from information collection, to monitoring, to large-scale summarization and analysis, toward systems in which information continuously feeds an investigation. Priority, cost, quality, heuristics, and autonomy remain configurable design variables rather than parameters requiring a single universal solution. The proposed distinction between a research tool and continuous intelligence therefore rests on whether the system merely retrieves and summarizes information or can maintain investigations, learn from their accumulated evidence and feedback, and return to unresolved questions when the external world changes.
