Investigation as the primary unit of an intelligence architecture: a persistent process that formulates hypotheses, gathers and connects evidence, preserves context and uncertainty, determines next investigative actions, and resumes when new information can affect a decision. The architecture can support questions about suppliers, competitors, markets, technologies, customers, or strategic hypotheses. An investigation evolves as evidence changes: searches can uncover related entities, conflicts can trigger primary-source research, hypotheses can gain or lose support, lines can be abandoned or expanded, and insufficient evidence can lead to monitoring rather than a definitive recommendation.

Monitoring differs from investigation because a signal does not inherently establish meaning, relevance, causality, or required action. Security Operations Centers illustrate the model: alerts are correlated and contextualized, patterns are investigated, severity is assessed, and response follows when warranted. The same principle applies to public and business information: individual complaints, posts, videos, reports, or news items become meaningful through relationships, temporal patterns, source analysis, and competing explanations. Search therefore becomes recursive: its result can determine the next research action. The architectural object is not a continuously running search agent but a persistent investigation with state.

Persistent investigations can be managed similarly to concurrent operating-system processes. Each investigation has state, objective, priority, uncertainty, and resource budget; some are active, some wait for evidence, and some are effectively complete. A scheduler allocates finite agentic processing capacity among them. Urgency and required depth depend on context: a stable supplier may need only periodic checking, a recent competitor acquisition may require deeper analysis, an emerging technology may remain low priority until a relevant signal appears, while rapidly developing potentially consequential events may require immediate processing. The next action is state-dependent: investigate a newly discovered entity, locate a primary source for conflicting claims, examine controlling parties after a corporate change, or trace duplicated reporting to its origin. The scheduler therefore selects both which investigation deserves attention and the most useful action within it. Investigations can be interrupted, resumed, paused while awaiting evidence, spawn sub-investigations, or terminate individual lines of inquiry. Agentic capacity can consequently be shared across many persistent investigations rather than requiring one continuously operating agent per problem.

Because investigation consumes API calls, searches, processing, storage, inference, agent time, and potentially paid-source access, the architecture must decide not only what to investigate but how far to investigate. An investigative ROI heuristic can consider the probability that additional information changes the current conclusion, the impact of such a change, and the cost of obtaining the information. When evidence is consistent, uncertainty is low, and another search is unlikely to affect the decision, effort can be reduced, shifted to periodic monitoring, or stopped. When one additional fact could materially change an important decision, another investigation cycle may have high value. An investigative budget can therefore concentrate resources where expected information gain is high and reduce them where marginal returns have declined. The optimal strategy is not necessarily known in advance; heuristics can be adjusted from observed outcomes, including which investigative paths produce relevant evidence, their cost, and when additional investigation ceases to justify its resources. Intelligence therefore includes knowing when sufficient evidence has been obtained, not merely discovering more information.

The investigation must maintain competing hypotheses rather than prematurely committing to one narrative. For example, increasing criticism of a company could reflect a genuine operational problem, an isolated issue amplified by exposure, a coordinated campaign, or the aggregation of superficially similar events. Evidence and inference must remain distinct: a source-reported fact is evidence, while interpreting it as a specific organizational risk is an inference; a relationship between events does not by itself establish causation. Source independence must also be assessed because multiple outlets reproducing the same originating report do not constitute independent evidence. Conclusions should preserve epistemic limits: failure to find relevant information establishes only that no relevant signal was found within the searched source perimeter, not that the underlying phenomenon does not exist.

Continuity requires persistent investigative state rather than merely conversation history. The system should preserve the question being investigated, hypotheses raised, evidence collected, sources consulted, discarded hypotheses, unresolved questions, and previous conclusions. This state must be combined with organization-specific context because the same information can have different significance depending on criticality, substitutability, relationships, policies, and prior knowledge. Organizational context can include entities, relationships, definitions, policies, preferred sources, classifications, previous decisions, and human corrections. Human feedback can correct entity associations, challenge inferences, confirm relationships, discard hypotheses, or identify more relevant evidence. These corrections can persist beyond an individual investigation and influence future investigations. Architectural learning is therefore distinct from model retraining: the underlying model may remain unchanged while the architecture accumulates organizational knowledge, source preferences, investigative heuristics, and corrections. The architecture learns both about the external world and about how the organization investigates that world.

Autonomy should primarily concern execution of the investigation rather than unrestricted decision-making. The system may autonomously select sources, deepen hypotheses, create sub-investigations, reduce effort, seek additional evidence, or resume paused investigations. High-impact actions such as legal decisions, terminating business relationships, or rejecting suppliers require greater governance and potentially human approval. Different contexts can support different autonomy levels, from observation and alerting through recommendations requiring approval to narrowly controlled automated actions. Autonomous investigation also requires explicit uncertainty representation and reconstructable reasoning. An investigative trail should capture the original question, hypotheses, sources, evidence considered or discarded, organizational context, intermediate conclusions, reasons for subsequent searches, final recommendation, human corrections, and later outcomes. Auditability is therefore not only a compliance mechanism but a component of intelligence quality: greater autonomy increases the need to understand what the system did, why it did it, and what evidence supported it. Recommendations and subsequent outcomes can feed back into future learning.

The underlying technological components already exist as a market category. Competitive and Market Intelligence Platforms, monitoring systems, AI-assisted research, external-source analysis, source aggregation, validation, knowledge management, and enterprise integration are established capabilities. Brazilian providers also operate in competitive intelligence, monitoring, market intelligence, reputation, and information analysis. The proposed distinction is therefore architectural rather than based on individually novel components: reorganize these capabilities around investigation as the primary unit. Instead of starting with an entity and asking what happened to it, start with a question and dynamically discover the entities, sources, events, and relationships that must be investigated. Search becomes an evolving investigative operation rather than an isolated execution ending in a report; the report becomes an intermediate artifact rather than the endpoint; and a conclusion becomes a revisitable state that can change when new information appears.

A Brazilian implementation could require investigation strategies adapted to local public sources, corporate structures, regulatory environments, market characteristics, and governance requirements. Such adaptation would be part of the system's intelligence rather than merely a localization layer.

The resulting architecture represents a progression from information collection, to monitoring, to large-scale summarization and analysis, toward continuous intelligence. Information becomes input to a persistent process that formulates questions, tracks hypotheses, preserves context, chooses next actions, allocates resources, incorporates organizational knowledge and human feedback, and revisits conclusions when the external world changes. The central architectural question consequently shifts from which model to use toward how to organize the system's investigative process. Some behavior can begin with heuristics and improve through operational experience. Over time, the architecture can learn which sources and investigative paths work for particular problems and when further investigation ceases to create sufficient value. Continuous intelligence is thus differentiated from conventional research tooling not primarily by finding more information or producing better summaries, but by maintaining a persistent, adaptive, resource-aware, context-specific investigation capable of learning from its discoveries and returning to unresolved questions as circumstances change.
