Legacy systems persist not merely because of cost, risk, technical difficulty, or criticality, but because they accumulate business knowledge that organizations may no longer be able to reconstruct independently. Over decades, business rules, customer-specific exceptions, regulatory adaptations, discontinued products, integrations, operational behaviors, and undocumented decisions become embedded in code and data while developers, teams, vendors, documentation, and institutional context disappear. Consequently, a legacy system can become one of the few remaining pieces of evidence of how the business actually operates.

Legacy status is therefore defined less by technology or age than by accumulated dependencies, decisions, and knowledge that make safe modification or replacement difficult. The distinction between understanding implementation and understanding business meaning is fundamental: a developer may determine exactly what a routine does without knowing why it exists, whether its behavior remains necessary, or which external processes depend on it. Replacing such a system requires recovering and preserving the business knowledge that still matters, not merely reproducing observable code behavior.

AI can substantially accelerate legacy-system investigation by analyzing large codebases, identifying dependencies and critical modules, relating data structures, generating documentation, comparing behaviors, and proposing possible business rules. It can function as an investigative guide, reducing the manual effort required to navigate systems that may contain millions of lines of code. However, identifying what code does is not equivalent to discovering what that behavior means or why it exists.

AI-generated explanations can be technically precise yet semantically incorrect because the rationale for a rule may reside outside the available evidence: an old contract, meeting, organizational decision, operational dependency, or team knowledge that no longer exists. The danger is therefore not limited to incorrect generated code. AI can produce plausible explanations or modern implementations that appear equivalent to the legacy system while silently omitting undocumented business rules. Code equivalence does not guarantee business equivalence. AI may also incorrectly classify behavior as redundant or obsolete, infer feature semantics from common data patterns, or recommend removing apparently unused structures whose dependencies are indirect or poorly documented.

AI consequently creates a second-order risk: because reverse engineering becomes much faster, organizations may confuse rapid generation of documentation, diagrams, explanations, or implementations with genuine understanding. Before AI, the high cost of analyzing legacy systems forced organizations to recognize their uncertainty; AI can make that uncertainty less visible by producing convincing narratives from incomplete evidence. The critical distinction is between what AI directly established from evidence and what it merely inferred.

Reliable modernization therefore requires multiple evidence sources rather than code analysis alone. Relevant evidence can include source code, historical production data, logs, existing tests, integrations, operational processes, documentation, and remaining subject-matter experts. AI can serve as an interpretation and correlation layer across these sources: organizing evidence, identifying contradictions, proposing hypotheses, and highlighting unanswered questions. Its value is not necessarily in supplying definitive answers but in making the investigation more systematic and revealing which questions still require human validation.

When documentation and code are insufficient, controlled experimentation can provide additional evidence. A sufficiently representative pre-production copy can be deliberately modified to test hypotheses: changing field values, removing validations, altering conditions, and observing downstream effects. Such experiments can expose indirect dependencies and reveal whether apparently irrelevant rules or fields affect distant processes. This turns reverse engineering into an empirical process: formulate a hypothesis, change one variable, observe consequences, and update the system model based on evidence.

Controlled experimentation does not mean arbitrary modification of critical production systems. Its reliability depends on how accurately the experimental environment reproduces relevant production conditions, including external integrations, data volumes, timing, and rare behaviors. Effects may also be indirect or delayed. Nevertheless, the approach changes legacy analysis from passive code reading into active interrogation of system behavior.

AI can support this experimental approach by proposing behavioral hypotheses, identifying components to observe, suggesting controlled experiments, and comparing observed results with expected behavior. This can help construct a causal model of the system from empirical evidence. It does not eliminate uncertainty, but it can transform unknown business rationale into testable questions about dependencies and consequences.

Legacy modernization can therefore be treated as a systematic discovery process combining code analysis, data investigation, behavioral observation, controlled experimentation, human institutional knowledge, and AI-assisted evidence synthesis. The central problem remains epistemic: discovering how a system behaves is easier than proving that every business rule that matters has been discovered.

Before replacing a legacy system, the primary question should therefore be: what does this system know that the organization must continue to know? Business knowledge may be explicit in code, hidden in data, preserved through unusual behavior, encoded in integrations, or retained only in human memory. No tool can prove the existence of a rule that is no longer observable. If the organization cannot establish with sufficient confidence which knowledge must be preserved, the migration may not yet be ready—not because the old technology is irreplaceable, but because the organization has not demonstrated that it understands the knowledge embedded within the system.
