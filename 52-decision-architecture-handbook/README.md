# Decision Architecture Handbook

_A structured approach to understanding contexts, establishing objectives, assessing risks and consequences, and evolving decisions across different scenarios._

**Summary**: In this book, I propose an approach to understanding and structuring decisions regardless of the domain or method used. I start from the idea that, although Solution Architecture, Project Management, Entrepreneurship, and other disciplines have their own practices, they share fundamental elements that underpin the decision-making process: domain, initial context, objectives, risks and uncertainties, direct and indirect consequences, and evolution. By organizing these elements and their relationships, I seek to provide a thinking framework that helps assess choices, understand their impacts, and adapt decisions over time, without imposing a universal method or rigid sequence. Rather than teaching people how to make decisions, the aim is to clarify the structure behind decisions and provide a reference that can evolve and be applied to different contexts.

The work brings together reflections on how we structure decisions and proposes an approach to understanding the elements involved in this process, establishing relationships between them, and considering their effects over time.

The idea began with a more specific question: would it be possible to develop a methodological reference for Solution Architecture that played a role similar to that of the PMBOK in Project Management? I felt the lack of an approach that organized the discipline's knowledge and practices and provided a reference to guide the work of solution architects. My education in Project Management and Solution Architecture, both at the MBA level, contributed to this perception. In the former, I encountered methods and practices for organizing initiatives and managing resources, risks, and deliverables. In the latter, I found approaches to understanding business needs, evaluating technological alternatives, and structuring solutions.

My entrepreneurial experience, particularly in the startup environment, added another perspective. In this context, decisions often need to be made with limited resources, incomplete information, and conditions subject to change. It is not always possible to conduct an exhaustive analysis before taking action, and many choices need to be revisited as hypotheses are tested and new knowledge emerges. This experience reinforced the perception that making a decision is not simply a matter of selecting an alternative: it involves understanding the situation, recognizing what is not yet known, and considering how a choice may change the conditions under which subsequent decisions are made.

As I moved between these areas, I began to identify recurring questions. Managing a project, designing a solution, developing a business, or driving organizational transformation are very different activities, but they all require understanding a situation, recognizing its conditions, establishing objectives, evaluating alternatives, dealing with uncertainties, and considering consequences. The methods used vary depending on the discipline, but many of the elements underpinning decisions remain present.

This realization changed the direction of the work. Rather than developing a specific methodology for Solution Architecture and subsequently others for Project Management and Entrepreneurship, I began investigating the concepts that precede these disciplines and help structure their practices. The focus shifted from organizing a specific professional activity to understanding the elements common to decisions made in different contexts. This process gave rise to the proposal presented in the book.

## A Structure That Precedes Methods

The expression Decision Architecture already appears in different fields and contexts. Therefore, this is not a claim to have coined the term or created an entirely new field. My contribution lies in organizing an approach of my own, bringing together concepts and practices from different disciplines into an integrated structure for understanding, documenting, evaluating, and evolving decisions.

The starting point is recognizing that a decision has structural characteristics that can be analyzed independently of the method chosen to guide it. Before selecting a framework, applying a technique, or starting a work process, it is necessary to understand what is being decided, under what conditions, with which objectives, and amid which uncertainties. It is also important to consider the effects the choice may produce and how those effects may influence the future.

This perspective helps explain why established approaches can differ and still share relevant concepts. The PMBOK organizes decisions and activities required for project management. TOGAF structures the evolution between architectural states, relating the current situation, the future vision, the gaps, and the necessary transitions. arc42 documents the structure and decisions of a software architecture. Lean Startup uses hypotheses, experiments, and learning to guide the evolution of businesses and products. Architectural Decision Records, known as ADRs, preserve the context, reasoning, and consequences of technical choices.

These approaches are not interchangeable, nor do they need to be. Each was developed to address specific needs. What interests me is the convergence of concepts that can be understood beyond their original contexts. The analysis of constraints, alternatives, and consequences, for example, is relevant both in solution architecture and in business decisions. The relationship between the current situation and the desired state can help structure organizational transformation. Experimentation can guide choices in scenarios where sufficient evidence is not yet available.

The cross-disciplinary nature lies in the concepts, not in the frameworks. The guide's proposal is to explore this layer of understanding that precedes the choice of methods, offering a common language for recognizing the structure of a decision and identifying which practices make sense in each situation. This also helps avoid a recurring problem: choosing a tool or methodology before adequately understanding the problem one intends to solve.

## The Six Foundations

The guide organizes this perspective around six foundations: domain, initial context, objectives, risks and uncertainties, direct and indirect consequences, and evolution. They represent dimensions that help us understand different aspects of a decision and the relationships between them. They are not mandatory steps, an execution sequence, or a checklist that must be completed in full in every situation. They are analytical perspectives that can receive different levels of attention depending on the nature and complexity of the problem.

The domain identifies the field of knowledge, activity, or problem in which the decision is situated. This definition matters because each area has its own concepts, criteria, and practices. A decision concerning a payment platform, for example, may involve architecture, security, finance, product, operations, and regulation. Each perspective contributes to the analysis, but may also reveal different priorities and constraints. A technically suitable alternative may be financially unfeasible; an option that is attractive to the business may introduce operational or regulatory risks.

The initial context, in turn, describes the specific conditions under which the decision takes place. Although related, domain and context are not the same thing: the domain defines the reference field, while the context brings together the concrete circumstances of the situation. Available resources, constraints, information, assumptions, commitments, dependencies, and previous decisions can significantly change the viable alternatives. Two organizations may face the same problem in the same domain and require different solutions because their conditions differ. Previous experience can provide useful references, but its applicability depends on current circumstances.

Objectives express the results one intends to achieve, preserve, or avoid. They guide the choice and make it possible to assess whether an alternative is suitable. However, objectives often remain implicit or generic. A technology modernization initiative, for example, may aim to reduce costs, increase capacity, mitigate risks, or enable a business strategy. Although these objectives may coexist, they are not equivalent and can lead to very different decisions. Making them explicit helps identify priorities and recognize conflicts, such as the need to accelerate delivery without excessively increasing risks or reducing costs without compromising quality.

Risks and uncertainties introduce into the analysis what cannot be known or controlled precisely. Decisions are made with limited information, hypotheses that still need to be verified, and future events that may change expected outcomes. Recognizing these conditions does not mean trying to predict everything, but rather understanding which uncertainties are relevant, which assumptions underpin the choice, and how different scenarios may affect its feasibility. In some cases, the most appropriate response may be to seek more information; in others, to conduct an experiment, adopt a reversible alternative, or establish conditions for reassessing the decision.

The analysis of direct and indirect consequences broadens the scope of the choice. A decision may produce the expected result while simultaneously generating effects in other parts of a system, organization, or process. Adopting a technology, for example, may address an immediate need but create dependencies, require new skills, or increase maintenance costs. Considering only the direct effect tends to leave these relationships out of the analysis. Examining broader consequences makes it possible to better understand the trade-offs involved and the impacts that may emerge later.

Finally, evolution recognizes that decisions do not exist in isolation over time. A choice changes the context in which future decisions will be made, may create new possibilities, impose constraints, or require revisions. In dynamic environments, the decision does not necessarily end when an alternative is selected. Its implementation generates information, results, and conditions that may justify adjustments. This perspective brings decision-making closer to a continuous process of learning and adaptation, in which the ability to revisit choices is just as relevant as the ability to make them.

The six foundations influence one another. Understanding the domain may reveal new constraints in the context; analyzing these conditions may lead to a reassessment of objectives; identifying uncertainties may change how alternatives are evaluated; and the consequences of a choice may alter the scenario in which subsequent decisions will be made. Therefore, it makes little sense to treat them as independent compartments or impose a rigid sequence. The value of the structure lies precisely in making these relationships more visible.

## From Understanding to Application

An important concern during the development of the guide was avoiding the transformation of the proposal into yet another universal framework or a recipe for decision-making. The sheer number of available methods, techniques, and tools can already make the choice of how to approach a problem complex in itself. In certain situations, deciding how to decide ends up requiring as much effort as the original decision.

The intention is to provide a thinking framework that helps identify which aspects deserve attention, without prescribing a single way of working. A simple and reversible decision does not require the same level of analysis as a strategic choice with significant impact and limited reversibility. The depth of the investigation should be proportional to the complexity of the situation, the potential consequences, and the degree of uncertainty involved.

The guide also seeks to connect the foundations to concrete situations. For this reason, in addition to chapters dedicated to each dimension, it includes a section focused on application examples. The approach is inverted: instead of starting with the concepts, it starts with specific problems and contexts to identify which foundations help us understand them. This perspective is important because, in practice, we do not usually begin an analysis with an organized conceptual framework. We start with a need, a problem, or an opportunity and must determine what needs to be considered.

The proposal can be explored in Solution Architecture, Project Management, Entrepreneurship, User Experience, and other fields in which choices need to be developed and evaluated. It can also be useful in personal decisions, provided the foundations are adapted to the nature of the situation. The objective is not to apply the same procedures in all these scenarios, but to recognize recurring structures without disregarding the specific characteristics of each domain.
