The Decision Architecture Handbook proposes a cross-disciplinary approach to understanding, structuring, documenting, evaluating, and evolving decisions, regardless of the domain or methodology involved. It identifies six fundamental dimensions shared by different decision-making disciplines: domain, initial context, objectives, risks and uncertainties, direct and indirect consequences, and evolution. The framework provides a common conceptual foundation without prescribing a universal methodology, mandatory sequence, or rigid process.

The initiative originated from the search for a methodological reference for Solution Architecture comparable to the PMBOK in Project Management. Academic experience in Project Management and Solution Architecture highlighted the importance of structured practices for managing initiatives, resources, risks, deliverables, business needs, technological alternatives, and solution design. Entrepreneurial experience, particularly in startups, introduced the challenges of making decisions with limited resources, incomplete information, and constantly changing conditions. These experiences demonstrated that decisions involve understanding circumstances, identifying unknowns, evaluating impacts, and anticipating how current choices influence future decisions.

Comparing different disciplines revealed common decision-making patterns. Project management, solution design, entrepreneurship, and organizational transformation all require understanding situations, identifying constraints, establishing objectives, evaluating alternatives, managing uncertainty, and anticipating consequences. This realization shifted the focus from developing a specific methodology for Solution Architecture to identifying the conceptual elements that underpin decisions across different domains.

Decision Architecture is an expression already used in multiple fields. The book does not claim to have originated the term or established an entirely new discipline. Its contribution is an integrated structure that combines concepts from different methodologies to support decision understanding, documentation, evaluation, and evolution. The premise is that decisions have structural characteristics that can be analyzed independently of the methods or frameworks selected to guide them. Understanding what is being decided, under which conditions, toward which objectives, and amid which uncertainties provides the foundation for selecting appropriate methods.

Existing methodologies demonstrate how these concepts appear across different disciplines. PMBOK organizes project management practices and decisions; TOGAF structures architectural evolution through current and target states, gaps, and transitions; arc42 documents software architecture structures and decisions; Lean Startup uses hypotheses, experiments, and learning to guide business and product development; and Architectural Decision Records (ADRs) preserve the context, rationale, and consequences of technical choices. These approaches are complementary, not interchangeable. Their underlying concepts can be applied beyond their original contexts, providing a common language for understanding decisions before selecting specific methodologies or tools.

The framework is organized around six interconnected foundations. They represent analytical dimensions rather than mandatory steps, sequential phases, or a universal checklist. Their relevance and depth depend on the nature, complexity, and impact of each decision.

The domain identifies the field of knowledge, activity, or problem in which a decision is situated. It establishes the relevant concepts, criteria, and practices. Complex decisions may involve multiple domains with different priorities and constraints. For example, a payment platform decision may require architectural, security, financial, product, operational, and regulatory perspectives. An alternative may be technically appropriate but financially unfeasible, or commercially attractive while introducing operational and regulatory risks.

The initial context describes the specific circumstances surrounding a decision, including available resources, constraints, information, assumptions, commitments, dependencies, and previous decisions. It differs from the domain because it represents the actual conditions under which the decision must be made. Organizations facing the same problem within the same domain may require different solutions because their circumstances differ. Previous experiences can provide useful references, but their applicability depends on current conditions.

Objectives define the outcomes to achieve, preserve, or avoid. They establish priorities and provide criteria for evaluating alternatives. Making objectives explicit helps identify potential conflicts and trade-offs. Technology modernization, for example, may aim to reduce costs, increase capacity, mitigate risks, or enable business strategies. These objectives may coexist but lead to different decisions, such as accelerating delivery without excessively increasing risks or reducing costs without compromising quality.

Risks and uncertainties address the limitations of available information and the unpredictability of future conditions. Decisions frequently depend on incomplete evidence, assumptions, and unvalidated hypotheses. The objective is not to predict every possible outcome but to identify relevant uncertainties, understand the assumptions supporting a decision, and evaluate how alternative scenarios may affect its feasibility. Appropriate responses may include gathering information, conducting experiments, choosing reversible alternatives, or establishing conditions for reassessment.

Direct and indirect consequences expand the analysis beyond the immediate expected outcome. Decisions may solve an existing problem while creating dependencies, introducing operational requirements, demanding new skills, or increasing maintenance costs. Evaluating only direct effects can conceal important impacts across systems, processes, and organizations. Considering broader consequences makes trade-offs explicit and helps identify effects that may emerge over time.

Evolution recognizes that decisions change the conditions under which subsequent decisions will be made. Implementation generates information, results, constraints, and opportunities that may require adjustments. Decision-making should therefore be understood as an ongoing process of evaluation, learning, and adaptation rather than an activity completed when an alternative is selected. The ability to revisit decisions is as important as the ability to make them initially.

The six foundations influence one another and should not be treated as independent categories. Understanding the domain may reveal contextual constraints; analyzing those constraints may change objectives; identifying uncertainties may alter the evaluation of alternatives; and the consequences of a decision may transform the context of future choices. Their value lies in making these relationships explicit and providing a comprehensive understanding of the decision structure.

The approach deliberately avoids becoming another universal methodology or prescriptive decision-making framework. Instead, it provides a conceptual reference for identifying which aspects deserve attention without imposing a standardized process. Analytical depth should be proportional to the decision's complexity, potential consequences, uncertainty, and reversibility. A simple, reversible choice requires less investigation than a strategic decision involving substantial impact and limited opportunities for reversal.

The framework is connected to practical situations through application examples that begin with specific problems and contexts rather than abstract concepts. This reflects how decisions typically emerge in practice: from a need, problem, or opportunity, followed by the identification of relevant considerations. The approach can support Solution Architecture, Project Management, Entrepreneurship, User Experience, organizational transformation, and personal decisions. Its application requires adapting the foundations to each domain rather than imposing identical procedures across different scenarios.

The book represents an initial stage in the development of the proposal rather than a definitive model. Its concepts form a coherent foundation, but practical application, contributions from different professional fields, and reader feedback may reveal opportunities for clarification, refinement, and expansion.
