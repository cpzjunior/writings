The author reflects on Sales professionals who stood out because they often had prior experience in Operations or technical areas, which gave them practical knowledge of the product before they had to sell it. They also remained close to customers after closing, following their evolution and identifying new needs, which naturally created opportunities for expansion and referrals. The author argues that the important distinction is not the professional’s original function, but the combination of understanding how the product actually works and staying connected to the people who use it. This creates a difference between merely knowing a product and knowing how to sell it properly, as well as between winning a customer and building a lasting relationship.

Sales training can provide knowledge of features, differentiators, use cases, benefits, positioning, and negotiation arguments, but practical experience with the product reveals integrations, prerequisites, limitations, operational problems, and conditions that may not be apparent from a sales presentation. Sales must also understand the customer’s problem rather than simply search for places to fit the product, because a technically capable solution may still be inappropriate for a particular context. This changes the central commercial question from how to persuade the customer to buy toward whether the solution genuinely makes sense for that customer. When it does, Sales can act with greater confidence and authority; when it does not, declining the opportunity can preserve trust and avoid creating a poor customer relationship.

In technology sales, Sales frequently interacts with architects, engineers, security, infrastructure, data, operations, and other technical stakeholders who may not make the financial decision but can strongly influence it by assessing feasibility, identifying risks, and challenging assumptions. Sales does not need to replace these specialists or possess their depth of expertise, but should be able to understand the context, ask relevant questions, follow the technical reasoning, participate confidently, and recognize when deeper specialist involvement is required. There is a meaningful difference between repeatedly deferring questions to technical teams and being able to explain the general situation before bringing in the appropriate specialist. When Sales can contextualize an issue, acknowledge a specific limitation, and involve an architect or engineer to deepen the discussion, the specialist becomes an extension of a competent conversation rather than someone brought in to rescue it, reinforcing customer trust.

Trust is especially important in complex sales because purchasing decisions can involve significant financial commitments, processes, integrations, organizational changes, and risks that only become visible after implementation. Customers need confidence that the vendor understands their problem and that its promises can actually be delivered. This credibility comes partly from practical product knowledge: Salespeople who understand how the solution works, including its possibilities and limitations, demonstrate competence that cannot be created through a persuasive pitch alone. Knowing the product also makes it easier to acknowledge uncertainty or involve the appropriate specialist rather than improvising an answer simply to appear knowledgeable. Sales therefore does not need to know everything; it needs to demonstrate that it understands what it sells, recognizes its limits, and can guide the customer to the right expertise when necessary. Practical knowledge becomes one of the concrete foundations of commercial trust.

The idea that satisfaction is determined by the relationship between expectation and reality has direct implications for Sales because Sales strongly influences the expectations customers carry into the organization. If those expectations are based on superficial product knowledge, reality may fail to match them even without any deliberate misrepresentation; limitations, dependencies, or conditions may simply have been overlooked. A sale can therefore appear successful when the contract is signed and revenue is recognized but later create implementation problems, support demands, deteriorating relationships, and renewal risk when the customer discovers that the solution does not work as expected. Understanding the product in practice is consequently not merely a way to sell more effectively but a way to sell appropriately. Declining an opportunity when the product is unsuitable can sacrifice short-term revenue while preserving trust and leaving room for future business, whereas selling solely to meet a target can generate immediate revenue while destroying longer-term value. A properly executed sale begins with expectations that the organization can realistically fulfill.

Closing a deal is often treated as the end of Sales’ involvement, but the author argues that closing should instead mark the transition from a commercial stage into an ongoing customer relationship. Sales can retain commercial targets without treating the signed contract as the only measure of success; an important additional question is whether the customer actually received what they believed they were buying. Sales should not replace Customer Success, but it should not disappear after signing either. Maintaining some proximity allows Sales to understand whether value is being generated, what problems have appeared, what new needs have emerged, and how the relationship is developing, while preserving knowledge acquired during the sales process. The transition to Customer Success should therefore feel like continuity rather than a handoff between unrelated organizations: Sales brings knowledge of the original motivations, objectives, priorities, and expectations, while CS brings direct knowledge of the customer’s actual experience. Information must also flow in the opposite direction, because CS may identify new needs, changing usage patterns, or problems that Sales could not have observed during the original process. This continuity should not turn every customer interaction into an upsell attempt; the primary logic should remain understanding the customer and selling only when a genuine need exists that the company can address. When Sales and CS work this way, the relationship can develop through a cycle of need, sale, value delivery, satisfaction, trust, renewal, expansion, referrals, and new opportunities, allowing existing customers to generate further business over time without requiring constant acquisition of entirely new customers.

A satisfied customer can renew, expand usage, purchase additional products, present new requirements, or recommend the vendor to other organizations and professionals, making the ongoing relationship a source of future opportunities rather than merely a consequence of the original sale. Salespeople who remain close to customers can detect needs that were absent or unclear during the initial purchase, while customers may proactively return when a new problem arises or remember the vendor when another need appears. Referrals are particularly valuable because the prospective customer enters the relationship with an existing reference of trust, and the referring customer effectively lends some of their own reputation to the vendor. This generally reflects more than contractual fulfillment: it reflects the perception that the vendor solved a problem and can be trusted. The author therefore frames sales as something that can build, preserve, or destroy trust, with satisfaction and trust potentially leading to renewal, expansion, and referrals. The sales process consequently becomes a cycle rather than a series of isolated transactions, and the quality of a sale affects not only current revenue but also the opportunities that can emerge from it later.

Sales should still operate with targets, and the appropriate structure depends on the business model, sales cycle, product, and other variables, with new revenue necessarily remaining an important metric in many contexts. However, closing should not be the sole measure of sale quality. Two companies can generate the same amount of new revenue while creating very different underlying businesses: one may have customers who renew, expand, recommend, and remain satisfied, while another may experience churn, complaints, high support costs, and renewal difficulties. The distinction is therefore between the amount of revenue generated and the quality of the revenue being built. Revenue followed by churn has a different long-term significance from revenue followed by satisfaction, renewal, expansion, and referrals. This does not make Sales solely responsible for customer satisfaction, because implementation, product, support, and Customer Success can all determine whether a customer ultimately succeeds. Sales nevertheless has a specific responsibility to begin the relationship with a promise the organization can realistically fulfill. The quality of a sale should therefore be considered in terms of both its immediate commercial result and the relationship and opportunities it creates afterward.

The author returns to the initial observation that the most impressive Sales professionals were not necessarily distinguished by having originated in Operations or technical functions, but by having understood the reality of the product before having to persuade customers to buy it. That knowledge allowed them to speak with greater authority, understand customer problems more effectively, recognize unsuitable solutions, involve technical specialists when necessary, and establish expectations closer to what the company could actually deliver. Their continued proximity to customers after closing also allowed them to follow customer evolution and identify new needs. The author presents this as a distinction between simply selling and contributing to a sustainable business: understanding the product builds trust, understanding its limitations helps align expectations, aligned expectations increase the likelihood of satisfaction, satisfaction strengthens trust, and trust creates conditions for future conversations, sales, and referrals. Closing therefore changes the nature of the relationship rather than ending it: the contract converts an opportunity into a customer, while what follows determines whether that customer becomes a one-time revenue source or the beginning of a long-term relationship capable of generating value for both sides.
