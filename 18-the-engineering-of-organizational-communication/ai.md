Some time ago, I started thinking about how distance changes our perception of the environment. Living on a lower floor exposed me to constant street noise and a large amount of irrelevant information, while living on the top floor provides greater silence through distance but also reduces awareness of changes happening at street level. This became an analogy for organizational hierarchy: as leaders move farther from operations, filtering reduces noise and enables abstraction, but can also remove important signals. The solution is not to eliminate filtering or make leaders consume all operational information, but to design better filters that preserve relevant context. Organizational communication should therefore be treated as an engineering problem involving information paths, filtering, abstraction, timing, recipients, and feedback, with the objective of establishing an appropriate distance between decision-makers and the reality their decisions affect.

Communication should not be treated as a monotonic variable in which more information necessarily produces better decisions. Individuals naturally develop filters when exposed to excessive stimuli, and organizational professionals do the same when confronted with large numbers of messages, meetings, dashboards, and documents. These filters are rational adaptations to information overload, but they learn patterns rather than necessarily recognizing true importance, so relevant information can be discarded along with noise. Excess communication can therefore be more dangerous than visible silence because it creates the impression that people are informed while reducing their ability to distinguish meaningful signals. Too little communication produces information asymmetry, while too much produces noise, and both can lead to decisions based on incomplete representations of reality. Organizational communication should therefore be optimized not for the volume of information transmitted, but for the amount of relevant information that reaches the appropriate person at the appropriate time in a usable form.

Possessing information does not necessarily mean possessing knowledge or a shared understanding of reality. Organizations need to distinguish observed facts, interpretations, hypotheses, evidence, and contextual understanding: an outage is an observation, attributing it to a particular component is an interpretation requiring evidence, and predicting that changing the component will prevent recurrence is a hypothesis. If these levels are mixed, repeated interpretations can gradually acquire the appearance of facts and become embedded in organizational communication. Logs, metrics, incident records, documentation, and decision histories help preserve evidence and reconstruct reality, but contextual knowledge often remains concentrated in individuals who participated in decisions, incidents, customer interactions, or implementations. Organizational capability emerges when the organization records not only what was decided, but also the facts, evidence, context, alternatives, and subsequent learning behind those decisions. Communication therefore needs to support the construction, questioning, and preservation of a shared representation of reality rather than merely transporting information between people.

Hierarchy establishes authority, responsibility, and accountability, but it does not need to determine every path through which information travels. Vertical communication supports governance and coordination, while horizontal communication allows specialists, development, operations, support, and leadership to exchange information directly when necessary. Hierarchical layers naturally compress information, which is necessary for scale, but problems arise when compression removes context required for a decision or when hierarchy becomes a mandatory path for information and action. Each additional layer can introduce latency, interpretation, and context loss, particularly during crises where direct access to people closest to a problem may be necessary without replacing existing operational ownership. The objective is therefore not to eliminate hierarchy or make communication universally direct, but to allow information to follow the appropriate path through the structure, with the key question being what the shortest useful path is between required knowledge and the person capable of making the decision.

Executives need abstraction and distance to perform their roles effectively, but should not become completely dependent on information filtered through hierarchical layers. Periodically engaging directly with operations, support, recurring problems, manual processes, difficult systems, customer escalations, and undocumented tacit knowledge can reveal information that rarely appears in executive reports. Operational and support teams function as organizational sensors, often detecting architectural, process, automation, or customer problems before they become visible in aggregated indicators. Direct observation also allows leaders to evaluate the communication mechanisms themselves, identifying unused reports, excessive meetings, informal knowledge concentrations, or unnecessarily long decision paths. Leadership should be able to move from aggregated indicators to anomalies, processes, incidents, and eventually the people holding the relevant context, then return to the executive level and convert local observations into systemic decisions. This resembles observability in distributed systems: executives do not need continuous visibility into every internal state, but they need the ability to drill down when an aggregate signal warrants investigation, so hierarchical distance does not become informational distance.

When one professional becomes the person who knows everything about a system, process, or integration, what appears to be valuable specialization can represent an organizational single point of failure. Knowledge concentration develops gradually as someone repeatedly solves problems, accumulates historical context, participates in decisions, and becomes the default reference, creating a feedback loop in which greater expertise produces greater dependence and greater dependence produces more opportunities to accumulate expertise. This affects both the organization and the professional: absences become risky, vacations require planning, incidents continue reaching the same person, and delegation becomes difficult. A mature organization should preserve specialization while preventing it from becoming a monopoly on knowledge. The specialist can remain the primary reference while documenting, teaching, and distributing the knowledge so that the team's capability grows rather than its dependence on one individual. The objective is for expertise to create organizational value that persists beyond the specialist's physical availability.

Growing organizations share important properties with distributed systems: no individual has complete system state, knowledge is distributed across components, communication has latency, states can diverge, and decisions may need to be made using local knowledge. This makes decentralization partly an architectural response to distributed information: when the knowledge necessary for a decision resides locally, some authority should often be located near that knowledge. However, not every decision should be decentralized; decisions involving systemic risk, capital, strategy, compliance, or organization-wide consequences may require centralized context and authority. The relevant design question is therefore which decisions require global context and which can operate with local context, with autonomy proportional to knowledge, risk, and inter-unit contracts. Like distributed systems, organizations require governance mechanisms such as policies, standards, ownership, documentation, metrics, observability, escalation paths, protocols, and interfaces to preserve coherence across autonomous units. Excessive centralization creates a human bottleneck in which authority is concentrated at a point that cannot possess all relevant context, while uncoordinated distribution creates divergent states and hidden dependencies. Communication connects local autonomy with global coordination and therefore becomes part of the organization's architecture rather than merely an interpersonal capability.

A July 2026 example from Xbox illustrates how organizational structure can create communication latency. CEO Asha Sharma announced a restructuring and stated that some areas had as many as 14 management layers, which she associated with slower decisions, reduced clarity of accountability, and more difficult delivery; the proposal was to reduce management layers to no more than five and, where possible, three. The important issue is not the number itself but the transformation of information as it crosses organizational boundaries: successive summaries and interpretations can introduce delay and context loss, while decisions moving downward through multiple levels can acquire ambiguity or altered priorities. This can be understood as organizational latency analogous to communication latency in distributed systems. The problem is not inherently having managers or hierarchy, but requiring unnecessary intermediaries to transmit, interpret, or validate information and decisions. The proposed Xbox structure, involving makers, player-coaches, and people directly responsible for decisions and outcomes, follows the principle of reducing unnecessary complexity while retaining accountability. Hierarchy can continue to establish ownership, budgets, risk, and responsibility without becoming a mandatory sequence through which every piece of information or decision must pass. Organizational architecture should therefore distinguish governance structure from communication topology and avoid turning accumulated hierarchy into unnecessary latency.

Communication is not only a one-way transmission mechanism but a feedback loop connecting reality, observation, communication, decision, execution, new reality, and renewed observation. A decision has organizational value only if its effects can return as information to those capable of evaluating and changing subsequent decisions. If feedback is delayed or unreliable, the organization may continue acting according to a reality that has already changed. Operations and support are important observation points because they detect effects of architectural changes, process failures, customer problems, and emerging patterns that must return to development and leadership. Communication mechanisms themselves can also be evaluated through feedback: information that never influences decisions may not need to be distributed in its current form, recurring incidents may reveal deficient observation mechanisms, and repeated execution failures may originate in knowledge flows rather than execution. The organization can therefore learn not only about its products, systems, and customers but also about its communication architecture, adapting information flows based on their observed effectiveness. When this learning is preserved, it becomes organizational memory rather than remaining dependent on individual recollection.

Distributed organizational knowledge requires an external memory independent of individual minds. Documentation, Wikis, and knowledge bases therefore function as organizational infrastructure rather than merely administrative artifacts, especially when they preserve context that would otherwise disappear through turnover, absence, or forgotten details. Treating documentation as software, particularly through Wiki-as-Code approaches, adds versioning, review, automation, and integration with development, allowing code changes, incidents, and architectural changes to trigger documentation updates and preserve not only decisions but their context and evidence. Organizational memory must also preserve provenance, validity, and authority: it should be possible to determine where information came from, when it was produced, whether it remains applicable, and who has authority over the relevant knowledge. AI can add a layer that identifies related changes, organizes knowledge, proposes updates, and exposes organizational memory to internal systems or agents through mechanisms such as MCP servers, while retaining source, date, validity, and authority. AI can also translate knowledge between technical, operational, financial, business, and executive contexts without changing the underlying facts, provided that translation remains distinct from inventing or altering conclusions. Different representations can therefore share the same source while exposing the abstraction appropriate to each audience. This combination of versioned, contextualized organizational memory and AI-based filtering can address both information scarcity and information overload by making relevant knowledge accessible at the required level of abstraction. The resulting flow is that humans produce knowledge, systems preserve its provenance and relationships, governance establishes validity and authority, AI organizes and contextualizes it, humans validate critical content, and humans and agents consume it in context.

Structured organizational knowledge can become a practical onboarding mechanism rather than remaining an archive. New technical professionals do not primarily need onboarding to revalidate their ability to program; they need to develop a mental model of how the organization builds systems. A practical exercise derived from an existing production architecture can provide an artificial problem containing relevant business rules, integrations, constraints, and accidental complexity, requiring the new employee to implement a solution using the organization's actual patterns for services, dependencies, observability, messaging, configuration, errors, contracts, and integration. This transforms organizational standards from presentation material into practical experience and allows the exercise to evolve as production architecture evolves. Production systems can therefore generate knowledge, documentation can structure that knowledge, documentation can generate onboarding exercises, and new professionals can learn organizational patterns through practice. Onboarding becomes not only a process of integrating individuals but also a mechanism for propagating organizational architecture and transmitting accumulated knowledge into the future.

Organizations often respond to communication problems by adding meetings, channels, reports, dashboards, emails, and notifications, but the central need is usually better paths for relevant information rather than greater information volume. Organizational communication should reduce the distance between reality, knowledge, and decision-making by determining what should be communicated, to whom, when, at what level of detail, and through which channel. AI can provide contextual filtering by analyzing changes, incidents, and events and presenting stakeholders with information relevant to their context while preserving access to original evidence. An effective organization therefore does not require everyone to know everything; it requires people to have the context necessary for their decisions, mechanisms for important information to cross organizational boundaries, decision-making close to operational reality, and protection against critical knowledge becoming concentrated in individuals. Communication architecture determines how information circulates, is filtered, contextualized, transformed into knowledge, and used in decisions, while feedback from those decisions allows the communication system itself to adapt. In this model, communication becomes infrastructure for organizational learning rather than merely the transmission of information.
