# Human Support as a Mechanism for the Evolution of Automation

_How to combine artificial intelligence, human-centered support, and feedback to build support that is more efficient, scalable, and capable of learning from its own customers._

**Summary:** I argue that the future of support does not lie in choosing between AI and human support, but in making the two work together. AI should solve what we already know and can automate, while humans should handle cases that require context and judgment, turning recurring solutions into knowledge that expands automation. Customer feedback completes this cycle by revealing not only what needs to be automated, but also what needs to be documented or eliminated from the product itself. The goal, therefore, is not to eliminate human contact, but to reduce customer effort and turn every interaction into an opportunity to improve the system.

---

Every now and then, I find myself looking at the reputation of major brands on Reclame AQUI. It is interesting to see how extremely well-known companies, with sophisticated products, huge operations, and enormous investments in technology, can have poor ratings precisely in one of the most sensitive points of their relationship with customers: support when something goes wrong.

This has always caught my attention because there is an important difference between delivering a good product and being there when the product does not work as it should. When everything goes well, the relationship between customer and company is almost invisible. I buy, use the service, solve whatever I needed to solve, and get on with my life. The real test of this relationship happens when an exception arises. A charge that should not exist, a transfer that was not completed, a product that did not arrive, a feature that stopped working, a question that is not documented, or simply a situation that does not fit into the flow anticipated by the company.

This is when support stops being merely a customer service operation and becomes part of the product itself. Perhaps that is why I find it interesting to observe Reclame AQUI. Regardless of the limitations of any review platform, it provides a public portrait of a dimension of the experience that many companies would rather keep invisible: what happens when the customer needs the company.

And this led me to a question that I increasingly consider relevant: why are we trying to make support increasingly automated precisely at a stage of the journey where automation will not always be enough to solve the problem?

I do not think the answer is to abandon automation. Quite the opposite. I believe we have an opportunity to build a much better support model than the ones we have today. But there is an irony in this pursuit of automation: perhaps we are eliminating precisely the human layer that, in addition to solving what AI still cannot solve, can provide the knowledge it needs to evolve.

## The Misguided Debate Between AI and Humans

In recent years, the discussion about customer support seems to have been framed as a competition between two extremes. On one side, there is the view that artificial intelligence will make it possible to eliminate much of the cost associated with support by automating responses, reducing teams, and providing service at scale 24 hours a day. On the other, there is the defense of human support as something necessarily superior, especially when there is frustration, complexity, or a need for personal interaction. I believe both sides are looking at the problem in an excessively binary way.

I see no reason for a person to manually answer a question that an AI can correctly solve in a few seconds. Likewise, I see no reason to force a customer to persist in a conversation with an AI that clearly does not understand the problem. The point should not be choosing between artificial intelligence and human support, but understanding where each is most effective and building an experience in which one complements the other.

AI is excellent for scale. It can handle large volumes of requests simultaneously, answer recurring questions, retrieve information, execute standardized procedures, and be available at any time. For problems we already know, can structure, and know how to solve, automating support not only makes sense but can significantly improve the customer experience. Automation is particularly effective when there is predictability, repetition, and enough knowledge to guide resolution.

Human support, on the other hand, should not simply be treated as a more expensive version of automation. There are situations that require judgment, context interpretation, negotiation, accountability, or information that has not yet been incorporated into the system. Not every situation is predictable enough to be reduced to a decision tree or knowledge base. And some situations may continue to require human intervention even after all available knowledge has been incorporated into automated operations.

There is also an important difference between an AI being able to simulate a human conversation and a customer actually being assisted by a person. An interaction can be polite, contextualized, and efficient without being human-centered. These are different experiences, and we do not need to pretend that one completely replaces the other. For some customers, automation will be the most convenient option. For others, the ability to speak with a person will be an important part of the experience.

This is precisely why I do not see human support as the opposite of automation. I see it as one of the pieces that allow automation to evolve. When AI cannot solve a problem, the case can be forwarded to a person, who receives the context of the interaction and resolves what automation still cannot handle. If that situation reveals a pattern that can be generalized, the solution can be transformed into knowledge, incorporated into operations, and made available to AI again. What requires human intervention today can become another case that automation can solve on its own tomorrow.

The goal, therefore, is not to turn all human support into automated support. Some problems will continue to require human judgment, and that is fine. The goal is to identify what is repeatable, understand what is not yet, and use each interaction to deliberately expand the boundary of automation.

## The Role of Human Support

When AI cannot solve a problem, that should not represent the end of the journey. It should represent a change of layer. The customer can start by talking to AI and, if the request is simple and within the available knowledge, the problem is resolved right there. If AI does not have enough knowledge, identifies an out-of-pattern situation, or recognizes that the case requires human judgment, the interaction should be escalated to a person. The existence of this second layer is important not only because of its problem-solving capacity, but because it ensures that automation has a clear path for what is still beyond its capabilities.

This transition, however, needs to be well designed. The customer should not have to start everything over when support moves from AI to a person. The support agent should receive the conversation context, the information already collected, the attempts already made, and ideally a summary of the problem. This way, AI does not function as a barrier between the customer and the agent, but as a preparation layer that allows the human to focus their time precisely on what requires their capabilities. One of the most frustrating experiences in automated support is not necessarily talking to a machine, but explaining the entire problem to it and then, after finally getting to speak with a person, having to repeat exactly the same story.

But there is an even more important function in this process. Human support should not merely be the place where we send what AI cannot solve. It can also be one of the main sources of knowledge for enabling AI to solve more problems in the future. Imagine a situation that appears for the first time. AI does not know that scenario, the case is forwarded to an agent, and after investigating the circumstances, the agent identifies the cause and finds a solution. That solution should not die with that ticket. If that situation can happen again, the knowledge acquired can be analyzed, structured, and documented in a Knowledge Base, incorporated into internal procedures, and, when appropriate, made available to AI itself.

That “when appropriate” is important. Not every solution found by an agent should automatically become a new rule for AI. It is necessary to assess whether the problem is recurring, whether the solution is generalizable, and whether there is enough confidence to automate it. An exception may truly be an exception, and trying to turn it into a rule can produce automation that is more complex or riskier than the original problem. The goal is not to automate everything a human can solve, but to identify what can be solved consistently without human intervention.

When this process works, a situation that previously required human intervention can be resolved automatically the next time it occurs. The benefit, therefore, is not limited to solving that specific support case, but lies in transforming a recurring exception into reusable knowledge. AI expands its resolution capabilities, while humans remain available for cases that continue to require context, judgment, or knowledge that has not yet been incorporated into automation.

This is where the hybrid model becomes truly interesting. AI solves what we already know and can standardize. Humans solve what still requires context, judgment, or knowledge that is not available to automation. When there is potential for generalization, the solution found by the human feeds the knowledge base and expands what AI will be capable of solving in the future. Human support therefore stops being merely a more expensive alternative to automation and becomes a mechanism for the evolution of automation itself.

## Support That Learns

We can think of support as a continuous learning cycle in which both AI and people learn from the cases that reach the operation. AI solves what we already know and can structure. When it encounters something it does not know, the problem is forwarded to a human, who investigates the case, identifies the cause, and finds a solution. In this process, however, it is not only AI that learns. The agent can also learn from new situations, identify patterns that were not yet documented, and improve the way similar cases are investigated and resolved. When this knowledge is structured, part of it can be incorporated into the Knowledge Base, internal procedures, and, when appropriate, AI itself. The next time it occurs, what previously required human intervention can be resolved automatically. The goal, therefore, should not simply be to reduce the number of human support interactions, but to make every interaction capable of increasing the entire operation’s ability to solve problems in the future.

This also changes how we should view a support team. It is not merely a structure responsible for answering tickets, meeting SLAs, and resolving complaints. It is one of the parts of the organization most exposed to the real problems faced by customers. While product and engineering teams work primarily from requirements, metrics, and hypotheses about how people use a solution, support is faced every day with situations in which these hypotheses encounter reality. Support agents know where processes break, which questions appear repeatedly, which features confuse users, which information is missing, and which situations were not anticipated when the product was designed. This knowledge has enormous value, but it is not automatically converted into intelligence for the organization.

There is an important difference between simply storing conversations and turning experiences into knowledge. There is no point in accumulating thousands of tickets in a database and expecting that, by itself, it will produce better AI or better human support. It is necessary to identify patterns, understand causes, separate isolated cases from recurring problems, structure solutions, and decide where that knowledge should be applied. In some cases, it should feed AI. In others, it may result in a change to the support procedure, new training for the team, an update to the documentation, or a change to the product. Knowledge needs to be treated as an operational asset, not merely as an interaction history.

More importantly, not every recurring problem should result in a new response for AI. If hundreds of customers need to contact support to find out how to perform a particular task, perhaps the solution is not to teach AI to explain the procedure better. Perhaps the solution is to simplify the interface itself. If a particular charge generates recurring complaints, perhaps the problem is not to create a better script for the agent, but to fix the process generating the charge. If customers frequently ask where to find certain information, perhaps it is necessary to improve the product experience instead of simply adding that answer to the Knowledge Base.

Support, in this sense, can function as a sensor for the product. It reveals not only what customers want to know, but also what is making their journey difficult. A mature organization should be able to distinguish between problems that need to be automated, problems that need to be documented, and problems that need to stop existing. The first category can expand AI’s capabilities, the second strengthens operations, and the third improves the product itself. This distinction is important because automating a bad solution is still a bad solution, only at a larger scale.

This is what makes the hybrid model particularly powerful. Human support does not exist merely to fill the gaps left by automation, just as AI does not exist merely to replace human work. AI learns from the patterns we can structure; people learn from cases we do not yet know; the organization learns from both. Customer contact reveals problems that can be transformed into knowledge, but also problems that no automation should attempt to conceal, because the correct solution lies in changing the product, process, or experience.

In the best-case scenario, therefore, support does not merely respond to the consequences of problems. It helps the organization understand their causes. With every interaction, there is an opportunity to improve AI’s response, better prepare the agent, improve a process, or eliminate friction from the product. The better a company can transform these interactions into knowledge and improvement, the less effort will be required to resolve similar problems in the future. Support stops being merely an operation that reacts to problems and becomes part of a system that learns from them.

## Feedback as Part of the System

This is where the third component of the model comes in. User feedback has a relatively low cost and can be one of the most valuable sources of information about the real experience of a product. An isolated complaint may represent a one-off situation, but dozens or hundreds of similar reports can reveal a structural problem. More than simply saying whether a customer liked something or not, feedback makes it possible to see where the experience is failing, which expectations are not being met, and which situations were not considered when the product or process was designed.

The problem is that many companies seem to treat feedback as something inconvenient. Some websites make it difficult to find a way to get in touch. Others hide the support or feedback section behind unintuitive menus. In some cases, users are repeatedly directed to an automated support center without finding a clear way to explain what is happening or speak to someone when automation cannot help them. From an operational perspective, this may seem efficient. Fewer contacts mean fewer tickets, lower demand on the team, and consequently lower costs.

But there is a fundamental difference between receiving fewer complaints and having fewer dissatisfied customers. A customer who cannot find support has not necessarily stopped having a problem. They may simply have given up complaining, abandoned the product, looked for an alternative, or taken their dissatisfaction somewhere else. The absence of a complaint does not necessarily mean satisfaction. Sometimes it simply means the absence of an accessible channel for complaining.

A complaint, in this sense, can be an opportunity. The customer who gets in touch is saying that there is a problem and, in a way, is giving the company the opportunity to fix it. There is an important difference between a customer who complains and a customer who leaves without saying anything. The former is still trying to establish a relationship with the company. If the organization can solve their problem, it may not only retain that customer but also discover something that can prevent similar problems in the future.

This does not mean, however, that every piece of feedback should be treated as absolute truth. Customers can interpret the same situation differently, certain profiles may be more likely to complain, and a report may accurately describe the symptom without revealing its cause. The value of feedback lies precisely in combining it with other sources of information. Tickets, usage metrics, product data, user behavior, and observations from support can help transform an isolated complaint into a pattern that deserves investigation.

When there is no accessible channel for this, the company loses both things. It loses the opportunity to recover that customer and loses a source of information about the product itself. It is a kind of backwards economy: the apparent cost of support is reduced precisely by eliminating some of the information that could help the company improve its operations.

For this reason, feedback should be part of the same learning cycle as support. It can show where AI is failing, which situations require human intervention, which information is missing from the Knowledge Base, and which problems should be solved directly in the product. It can also reveal problems that do not appear in traditional usage metrics. A person may complete a given task, for example, and still have gone through a confusing, laborious, or frustrating experience. Feedback helps explain what isolated user behavior does not always reveal.

In this model, the cycle is no longer simply customer, AI, and resolution. The customer interacts with AI, AI resolves what it can and forwards what it cannot to a human. The human resolves the case and turns the experience into knowledge. The customer can then provide feedback about the entire process, indicating where the experience worked and where it failed. This information can feed back into AI, the Knowledge Base, support itself, and also Product, UX, Engineering, and Operations teams. Feedback is not merely an assessment of what happened; it helps determine what should happen next.

With each cycle, the company learns something. AI can become more capable, support can become better prepared, documentation can become more complete, and the product can become simpler. Feedback stops being merely a post-support assessment and begins to function as a permanent sensor of the customer experience. When combined with the other signals available to the organization, it makes it possible to identify not only where operations are failing, but also where there is an opportunity to eliminate the problem at its source.

Perhaps this is precisely why making support difficult to find is a more dangerous decision than it seems. By hiding the contact channel, the company is not merely reducing the number of tickets. It is reducing the amount of information reaching it about what its customers are experiencing. In the name of immediate operational efficiency, it may be eliminating one of the cheapest mechanisms for discovering where the product, process, and automation itself need to improve.

## When the Customer Wants to Talk to Someone

There is also a dimension that should not be treated merely as a matter of operational efficiency: customer preference. Some people simply prefer to talk to another person. Not necessarily because AI is bad, nor because they do not know how to use it, but because they consider human interaction more appropriate for certain situations. When there is frustration, uncertainty, a high-impact problem, or simply a need to explain a situation that falls outside the standard pattern, knowing that someone responsible is on the other side can be part of the trust the customer places in the company.

A humanized experience should also not be confused with an AI that uses human language. A machine can be polite, contextualized, fast, and extremely efficient. It can understand the context of a conversation, adapt its language, and even produce an interaction that the user considers pleasant. Even so, it remains an interaction with a machine. This is not necessarily a problem. They are different experiences, with different characteristics and values, and there is no need to pretend that one can completely replace the other.

There is, naturally, a financial trade-off. Human support costs more than automation, especially when we are talking about operations with large customer volumes. It would be unrealistic to argue that every request should be forwarded to a person. If AI can solve a simple question in a few seconds, requiring an agent to participate in that interaction represents an unnecessary cost and can even make the experience worse. The question, therefore, is not to eliminate the cost of human support or ignore it, but to decide where that cost produces enough value to justify it.

This calculation, however, should not consider only the cost of the ticket. Human support can represent the cost necessary to recover a frustrated customer, prevent a cancellation, resolve a high-impact situation, or preserve trust in a brand. Likewise, an excessively restrictive support experience can generate costs that appear elsewhere: customer loss, public complaints, negative reviews, and missed opportunities to understand product problems. Human support is more expensive per interaction, but the cost of not offering it can also be significant.

For this reason, I believe a good support architecture should preserve a real entry point to human support. This does not mean putting an agent between the company and the customer for every possible request. That would simply waste one of the main advantages of automation. It means allowing that, when the situation requires it, when AI cannot solve the problem, or simply when the customer prefers it, there is a clear way to reach a person.

This door also needs to be accessible. It makes no sense for a company to claim that it offers human support while hiding the path to reach it behind an endless sequence of menus, automated responses, and attempts to make the user return to AI. If the chatbot’s only function is to prevent the customer from reaching human support, then it has stopped being a support tool and has become a barrier.

Choice also matters. A customer may prefer to resolve a simple issue on their own in a few seconds with AI. Another may prefer to talk to a person from the beginning. A third may accept automation for most of the journey but want a person when an out-of-pattern problem arises. A well-designed support experience does not need to impose the same journey on everyone. It needs to offer appropriate paths for different needs, balancing customer convenience and company efficiency.

The question is not to guarantee that every customer speaks with a human. It is to recognize that there is economic and experiential value in keeping that possibility available. Automation should be the natural choice when it solves the problem efficiently. Human support should be available when it adds value. And the customer should not discover, at the moment they need the company most, that cost reduction has made this option deliberately inaccessible.

## Support Is Also Part of the Product

Perhaps it is necessary to abandon the idea that support is something that exists after the product. For the customer, this separation simply does not exist. When everything works, support practically disappears from the experience. When something goes wrong, it immediately becomes part of it. An excellent product accompanied by inaccessible support still provides a poor experience when the customer encounters an exception. Likewise, efficient support can partially compensate for a product failure by quickly resolving a situation that should not have happened.

This should also change the way we measure support efficiency. A company can track the number of tickets, average support cost, automation percentage, and average response time, and all these metrics can be important to operations. But they tell only part of the story. AI can respond in two seconds and not solve anything at all, while an agent can take longer to respond and definitively solve a complex problem. In the end, perhaps the more important question is another one: how much effort did the customer have to make to reach a solution?

This perspective also changes how we interpret an apparently positive metric: the reduction in tickets. Fewer tickets can mean that AI is solving more problems, but it can also mean that customers have simply given up trying to get help. The number of tickets, by itself, does not tell us which of these two things is happening. A truly efficient operation should seek to reduce the effort required to solve problems, not simply reduce the number of times customers are able to contact the company.

This suggests that support should be treated as part of the product not only in terms of experience, but also strategically. Support is at the boundary between what the company believes it has built and what the customer can actually use. Every difficulty encountered at this boundary can reveal a product, process, communication, or automation failure. When this information is handled in a structured way, support stops being merely an operation that absorbs problems and starts functioning as a permanent source of signals for the organization.

In this context, perhaps one of the most important metrics is not how many support interactions we can eliminate, but how much effort we can eliminate from the customer journey. A reduction in tickets is good when it means the problem was solved before reaching support. It is bad when it simply means the customer gave up looking for help. Likewise, increasing the percentage of automation is positive when automation solves more problems with quality. It is not necessarily positive when it merely shifts the difficulty to the customer, forcing them to persist, rephrase questions, or seek other channels to get an answer.

The true potential of hybrid support lies precisely in this combination of operational efficiency and learning. Automation allows us to scale what we already know how to solve. Human support handles what requires context, judgment, or knowledge that has not yet been structured. Feedback helps reveal where the experience is failing. And the information produced by this process can improve not only support, but also AI, documentation, processes, and the product itself.

The goal, therefore, should not be to reach 100% automated support. That would turn the elimination of human contact into an indicator of success in itself, when what really matters is the ability to solve problems with the least possible effort for the customer and with the appropriate use of company resources. In some cases, the best experience will be an automatic response in a few seconds. In others, it will be a conversation with a person. And in some, the best solution will be to make that problem stop existing in the product.

Perhaps the more interesting question, therefore, is not how much of our support we can automate. It is how much we can learn from each interaction so that, the next time, the customer needs to make less effort to get what they need.
