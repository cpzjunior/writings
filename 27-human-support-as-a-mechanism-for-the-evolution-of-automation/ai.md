Every now and then, I find myself looking at the reputation of major brands on Reclame AQUI and noticing how companies with sophisticated products, large operations, and significant technology investments can still perform poorly when customers need help. This highlights a distinction between delivering a good product and supporting customers when something goes wrong: while successful usage makes the company almost invisible, exceptions expose the real quality of the relationship. Problems such as incorrect charges, failed transfers, missing deliveries, undocumented questions, broken features, or situations outside anticipated flows are precisely where support becomes part of the product experience. Public complaint platforms offer an imperfect but useful view into this otherwise less visible dimension. This raises the question of why companies increasingly seek to automate support at exactly the point where automation may not be sufficient. The alternative is not abandoning automation, but combining it with human support so that automation handles known problems while human interaction resolves exceptions and generates knowledge that can progressively expand what automation can handle.

The debate between AI and human support is unnecessarily binary. AI is well suited to high-volume, repetitive, predictable problems for which sufficient knowledge exists to provide reliable answers, retrieve information, or execute standardized procedures at scale and at any time. Human support should not simply be viewed as a more expensive version of automation, because some situations require judgment, contextual interpretation, negotiation, accountability, or knowledge that has not yet been incorporated into systems, and some may continue to require human intervention even after extensive automation. An AI can simulate a human conversation effectively without making the interaction equivalent to speaking with a person, and different customers may value these experiences differently. Human support is therefore not the opposite of automation but one of the mechanisms through which automation can evolve: when AI cannot resolve a case, a human can intervene, and when the resulting solution is recurring and generalizable, it can be transformed into reusable knowledge and incorporated into operations and AI. The objective is not to automate every human interaction, but to identify what is repeatable, understand what is not yet repeatable, and deliberately expand the boundary of automation.

When AI cannot solve a problem, the interaction should move to a human layer rather than end, with the transition designed so the customer does not have to restart the process. The agent should receive the existing conversation, collected information, previous attempts, and ideally a concise problem summary, allowing AI to function as a preparation layer rather than a barrier. Human support also provides an important source of knowledge for future automation: a previously unknown scenario can be investigated by an agent, resolved, and then analyzed to determine whether the solution is recurring and sufficiently generalizable to enter the Knowledge Base, internal procedures, or AI. This conversion should not happen automatically for every solution, because isolated exceptions may not justify new rules and overgeneralization can make automation more complex or risky than the original problem. When the process is applied selectively, recurring exceptions become reusable knowledge, expanding AI's resolution capabilities while preserving human intervention for situations that continue to require context, judgment, or unstructured knowledge. The hybrid model therefore makes human support not merely an alternative to automation but a mechanism through which automation itself becomes more capable.

Support can be understood as a continuous learning cycle in which AI, people, and the organization learn from the cases reaching the operation. AI handles structured, known problems; humans investigate unfamiliar ones; agents themselves learn from new situations and identify previously undocumented patterns; and validated knowledge can be incorporated into documentation, procedures, the Knowledge Base, or AI. The objective is consequently not merely to reduce human interactions but to make each interaction increase the operation's future problem-solving capacity. This also changes the role of the support team: it is not simply responsible for tickets, SLAs, and complaints, but is continuously exposed to the real problems customers encounter when product assumptions meet reality. Support sees recurring questions, broken processes, confusing features, missing information, and unanticipated situations that may be less visible to product and engineering teams. Simply accumulating tickets does not convert these experiences into organizational intelligence; patterns must be identified, causes understood, isolated cases distinguished from recurring problems, and solutions structured according to where they should be applied. Some problems should feed AI, others should improve procedures or documentation, and others should result in product changes. Recurring support demand may indicate that a procedure needs better automation, but it may instead indicate that the interface is unnecessarily difficult, a problematic charge should be eliminated at its source, or missing information should be incorporated directly into the product. Support therefore acts as a sensor for the product, helping distinguish problems that should be automated, documented, or eliminated. The resulting system allows AI to learn from structured patterns, people to learn from unfamiliar cases, and the organization to learn from both, turning support from a reactive operation into a mechanism for identifying and addressing underlying causes.

User feedback completes this learning cycle by providing relatively inexpensive information about the real customer experience. Individual complaints may be isolated, while repeated reports can expose structural problems, unmet expectations, or situations not anticipated during product and process design. Making feedback difficult to provide can create an apparent operational benefit by reducing ticket volume, but fewer complaints do not necessarily mean fewer problems: customers may simply give up, abandon the product, seek alternatives, or express dissatisfaction elsewhere. A complaint can therefore be an opportunity to recover a customer and discover a problem before it affects others, although feedback should not be treated as unquestionable evidence because different customers interpret situations differently, some groups may complain more frequently, and reported symptoms may not reveal underlying causes. Its value increases when combined with tickets, usage metrics, product data, behavioral signals, and support observations. In the hybrid model, feedback can identify failures in AI, gaps in human support, missing Knowledge Base content, and product problems that should be fixed directly rather than automated or documented. It can also expose confusing experiences that conventional usage metrics may not reveal. The resulting cycle connects customer interaction, AI, human resolution, knowledge creation, and feedback, with the resulting information feeding AI, support, documentation, Product, UX, Engineering, and Operations. Making support inaccessible therefore risks eliminating not only tickets but also a relatively inexpensive source of information about where the product, process, and automation need improvement.

Customer preference is another dimension that cannot be reduced entirely to operational efficiency. Some customers simply prefer interacting with another person, particularly when they are frustrated, uncertain, facing a high-impact problem, or dealing with circumstances outside standard patterns. A humanized experience is not equivalent to an AI that uses natural human language: AI can be polite, contextualized, fast, and effective while still being a machine interaction, and both experiences can have different forms of value. Human support necessarily has a higher per-interaction cost, so routing every request to an agent would waste the scalability advantages of automation. The relevant calculation should nevertheless include more than ticket cost, because human intervention can help recover frustrated customers, prevent cancellations, resolve high-impact cases, and preserve trust, while overly restrictive support can create costs through customer loss, public complaints, negative reviews, and missed product insights. A sound support architecture should therefore retain a genuine and accessible path to a person without placing a human between the company and every routine request. Customers may prefer AI for simple problems, human support from the outset, or automation followed by human intervention when an exception occurs. The system should accommodate these different needs rather than impose one journey universally. The objective is not to guarantee human interaction for everyone, but to preserve it as a meaningful option when automation cannot solve the problem or when the customer considers human assistance valuable.

Support should be treated as part of the product because customers do not experience the artificial separation between product and post-product support. When everything works, support is nearly invisible; when something fails, it immediately becomes part of the experience. Support metrics such as ticket volume, cost, automation rate, and response time remain useful operational measures, but they do not necessarily indicate whether customers are actually reaching satisfactory resolutions. A two-second AI response can fail to solve a problem, while a slower human interaction can resolve a complex case definitively. The more meaningful measure is therefore the effort required from the customer to reach a solution. A reduction in ticket volume may indicate that automation is successfully preventing problems from reaching support, but it may also indicate that customers have abandoned their attempts to obtain help; similarly, a higher automation rate is beneficial when it improves resolution quality, not when it merely transfers effort to the customer. Support sits at the boundary between what the organization believes it has built and what customers can actually use, making every difficulty at that boundary a potential signal of a product, process, communication, or automation failure. Hybrid support combines the scalability of automation for known problems, human judgment for unresolved ones, feedback about failures, and organizational learning that can improve AI, documentation, processes, and the product itself. The objective is therefore not 100% automation, because eliminating human contact is not inherently a measure of success; the appropriate outcome may be an immediate automated answer, a human conversation, or a product change that eliminates the problem altogether. The central question is how much the organization can learn from each interaction so that customers need to make less effort the next time they need something.
