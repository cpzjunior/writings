Over the past few weeks, discussions about the Brazilian National Team and Carlo Ancelotti prompted a reflection on how easily soccer analysis reduces complex problems to individual responsibility: replace the coach, change players, alter the formation, and start again. A team, however, is shaped by history, culture, player characteristics, development structures, infrastructure, processes, relationships, and decisions made long before the current match. A new coach can change tactics and extract different qualities from the same players, but inherits an existing system rather than a blank slate. Companies operate similarly: replacing a manager or employee may sometimes be necessary, but neither action independently rebuilds the team because the new manager inherits an established culture, processes, systems, relationships, previous decisions, and organizational architecture. Current performance is therefore partly the visible manifestation of structures accumulated over time.

A long-established soccer club is much larger than its current squad: it includes infrastructure, academies, staff, recruitment and development systems, history, and an identity that survives successive generations of players. Hiring is only the beginning of building a functioning team; players must be integrated, understand how the team operates, develop relationships, acquire the necessary repertoire, and find ways to contribute within a shared culture. Companies face the same challenge: recruiting capable people is only one stage of team building, followed by onboarding, development, responsibility definition, training, relationships, tools, information, and the gradual construction of common working practices. Culture is not primarily a written set of values but the behaviors reinforced over time—how people respond to mistakes, disagreement, difficult decisions, newcomers, recognition, and uncertainty—and mature cultures provide shared references that allow people to make decisions without explicit rules for every situation. This affects performance because teams with similarly capable people and resources can behave very differently depending on whether they encourage experimentation or risk avoidance, learning or blame, information sharing or internal competition. New employees therefore enter an existing behavioral and relational system and can eventually influence it themselves. Culture should not mean homogeneity: diverse perspectives can strengthen a team if a common foundation provides sufficient coordination. The purpose is not for everyone to play identically, but for different people to understand what they are collectively trying to build.

Soccer illustrates that individual talent does not automatically produce collective excellence: the Real Madrid Galácticos assembled major stars such as Figo, Zidane, Ronaldo, and Beckham without achieving proportional superiority as a team, while Brazil's 1986 squad contained extraordinary players including Zico, Sócrates, Falcão, Júnior, and Careca yet exited in the quarterfinals. Players depend on one another through positioning, complementary abilities, shared responsibilities, and interactions, so a player's value includes what their presence enables others to do. Companies similarly need complementary professionals rather than simply the strongest individual performers available: specialists, connectors, executors, problem structurers, creative thinkers, and process-oriented people can combine into a stronger collective capability than a team optimized for individual excellence in every role. Exceptional talent also carries trade-offs; characteristics that create value in one context can create difficulties in another, as illustrated by Romário's exceptional ability alongside behavioral characteristics that required particular management. The same applies to professionals whose creativity, discipline, questioning, unpredictability, consistency, or interpersonal behavior may be valuable or problematic depending on context and team needs. Building a strong team therefore requires understanding these trade-offs and finding combinations that support the chosen strategy. Sometimes the most valuable professional is not the person who produces the most individually but the person whose presence raises the performance of those around them. Collective capability is an emergent property of how individual capabilities interact, which makes team construction fundamentally different from simply accumulating talent.

In soccer, strategy establishes how a team wants to play, where it seeks advantages, how it intends to occupy space, and which risks it accepts, while tactics translate those principles into decisions under changing match conditions. A decision such as pressing high provides direction but cannot specify every action because opponents continually create situations that require players to interpret circumstances and decide whether to press, retreat, cover space, or follow an opponent. Strategy therefore should not become a detailed script; in complex environments, principles remain useful precisely because they continue to guide behavior when circumstances differ from the original plan. Effective teams train not only movements but principles and situations, teaching players why they should act a certain way so that they can adapt rather than merely reproduce memorized sequences. Companies face the same requirement: processes, policies, and standards matter, but they cannot anticipate every situation, so training should build the repertoire needed to make sound decisions when explicit instructions are insufficient. Teaching tasks alone creates dependency on higher-level instructions, whereas teaching the underlying work and objectives develops autonomy. Managers similarly should not become decision points for every issue; their role is to provide enough strategic context and clarity for decisions to be distributed throughout the organization. A coach cannot enter the field to correct every play, and a manager should not need to intervene in every new situation. The objective is to create people capable of interpreting changing circumstances consistently with the broader strategy.

Speed is not equivalent to performance: in soccer, running more, accelerating every play, or acting with constant intensity does not necessarily produce better control or results, because sometimes the correct decision is to slow down, reorganize, and wait. Fundamentals become more important as the game accelerates because players have less time to correct mistakes. Companies can make the opposite mistake by treating documentation, planning, review, testing, and processes as bureaucracy in the pursuit of speed, even though these practices can make speed sustainable. Rapid delivery, immediate decisions, or instant responses can look efficient while simply moving errors, rework, maintenance costs, or corrections forward. Likewise, a soccer team can win despite structural weaknesses, and a company can achieve an exceptional short-term result despite poor processes, dependence on particular individuals, or unsustainable effort. The important distinction is between speed and intelligent timing: a team should accelerate when an opportunity exists and slow down when control or analysis is more valuable. Mature organizations should similarly distinguish situations where speed creates competitive advantage from those where additional analysis prevents much larger downstream costs. The underlying problem is not pursuing speed itself but treating speed as the objective, because prioritizing rapid delivery over quality, immediate responses over correct ones, or short-term measurements over the foundations of long-term performance can cause essential fundamentals to be regarded as obstacles.

Highly sophisticated soccer systems create a tension between coordination and predictability: detailed tactics, data, physical preparation, and opponent analysis can encourage organizations to make each player's behavior increasingly predictable, but excessive predictability can become a weakness when circumstances change. Companies face the same tension with processes, metrics, methodologies, and structures that are necessary for coordination but can make organizations excessively optimized for executing what they already know. Professionals who question decisions, challenge processes, or propose alternatives may therefore be treated as difficult or disruptive, although their behavior can also reveal something others have missed. The relevant distinction is not simply whether someone violates a pattern but why: abandoning a position because one refuses to follow the system differs from doing so because the specific situation makes the standard response inferior; similarly, resistance differs from questioning intended to improve a shared objective. The disruptive player is therefore not merely someone who behaves differently but someone who understands the system sufficiently well to recognize when deviation may create more value than literal compliance. At the same time, not every deviation is innovation: abandoning responsibilities can be indiscipline, and questioning can simply be resistance. The difference lies in whether the exception remains connected to the team's larger objective. The Batman reference from Christopher Nolan's films illustrates the broader idea of an actor who may not conform to expectations but can sometimes address what the system needs. In organizations, questioning professionals can likewise be valuable when they reveal changes or problems that others have missed, although organizations must distinguish purposeful challenge from refusal to cooperate.

People within a team occupy different professional stages and contexts, so similar capabilities can produce different performance depending on the situation. A player in a slump may still possess the underlying ability and require time, guidance, or a different use rather than immediate replacement; a competent player may be out of position and need reassignment rather than criticism; and a rookie may lack experience rather than potential, meaning that training and playing time can matter more than immediate comparison with experienced professionals. People also move through different phases of life, with changing priorities, responsibilities, ambitions, risk tolerance, and desired career trajectories, so equal performance expectations do not necessarily make sense across different contexts. Context should not become an excuse for every performance problem, however; management requires diagnosis to determine whether the issue is training, position, circumstances, time, capability, or commitment. Someone who does not yet know how to perform a task may need development, someone unable to perform effectively in the current context may need a change, and someone unwilling to contribute may represent a different problem entirely. Treating all these situations as simply having a “bad professional” obscures whether the underlying problem lies with the person, role, training, system, or environment. Management therefore involves understanding why performance looks the way it does and choosing among interventions such as replacement, reassignment, training, or support rather than applying the same response to every case.

The corporate expression “wearing the shirt” often treats commitment as an attribute employees are expected to bring with them, but the analogy suggests that organizations and managers also have responsibility for making the shirt meaningful. A player who grew up in a club, knows its history, shares experiences with previous generations, and understands what its crest represents has accumulated reasons for identification through experience rather than through an abstract demand for loyalty. Companies similarly create belonging through what people observe in practice: how managers behave under pressure, how mistakes are treated, who receives recognition, whether questioning is tolerated, and whether organizational behavior matches stated values. Identification develops through daily experience, including whether employees understand why their work matters, have opportunities to contribute, and see consistency between organizational promises and reality. Commitment remains partly the individual's responsibility, but managers also need to create conditions that give people reasons to commit. This reframes engagement from simply asking whether an employee is committed to asking whether the organization provides sufficient purpose, context, autonomy, recognition, and development to support commitment. Wearing the shirt also does not mean obedience: someone can be deeply committed while disagreeing with a coach or challenging a decision they believe harms the team. Commitment is better associated with responsibility for the collective result than with repetition of organizational values, and that relationship is built through culture, relationships, and management over time.

A long-established soccer club represents more than its current squad because its identity incorporates achievements, failures, idols, stories, and symbols created by previous generations. Players who wear its shirt become temporary participants in a story that existed before them and should continue afterward, while their own contributions can become part of collective memory. Companies can similarly preserve contributions across changing projects, managers, teams, and strategies through improved processes, shared knowledge, technologies, relationships, and people who developed within the organization. Talent development therefore has a purpose beyond reducing recruitment costs: a strong internal development system creates opportunities for new people to enter the organization's history while allowing experienced professionals to transmit knowledge and experience. Development can also involve changing roles as people progress; an experienced soccer player may contribute through coaching or another organizational role after physical abilities decline, just as an experienced employee may move into leadership, mentoring, specialization, or another area where accumulated knowledge becomes more valuable. Internal development can provide both economic benefits through reduced dependence on external hiring and cultural benefits by creating continuity across generations. Legacy does not necessarily mean preparing someone to replace oneself; it can mean leaving behind solutions, knowledge, opportunities given to others, or cultural improvements that later generations can build upon. Organizations, like clubs, can therefore be understood as institutions whose significance extends beyond the immediate output of the people currently occupying their roles.

The central analogy is ultimately between a company and a soccer club rather than between a company and a single team: the team represents the people currently on the field, while the club encompasses the culture, infrastructure, development mechanisms, history, processes, symbols, and institutional capacity that allow successive teams to exist. Different employee situations—slumps, poor positioning, lack of experience, changing career needs, constructive disruption, or genuine lack of commitment—require different diagnoses and responses, and treating them uniformly can turn organizational problems into simplistic judgments about individuals. Management is therefore not merely the selection of who plays but the construction of conditions under which different people can work together: establishing strategy, developing culture and infrastructure, understanding individual trade-offs, developing talent, and maintaining enough structure to provide direction without eliminating judgment. A strong organization need not be defined by having the best individual players or by quickly replacing people when results fail to appear; rather, it should provide conditions for people to perform, learn from one another, adapt when circumstances change, develop successors, and preserve useful knowledge. The organization should be larger than its current personnel, with each manager ideally leaving behind something that continues to function and evolve after their departure. As in soccer, plans cannot anticipate everything that happens once the match begins, so the quality of the system is ultimately revealed by how well its people can interpret and respond to reality once the game is underway.
