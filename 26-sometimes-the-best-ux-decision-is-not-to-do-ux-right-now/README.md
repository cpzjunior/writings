# Sometimes, the Best UX Decision Is Not to Do UX Right Now

_The investment in user experience should be proportional to the product's maturity, the uncertainties of the business, and the risks involved in each decision._

**Summary:** I believe UX should not be treated as a mandatory process, but as a strategic decision: the effort needs to be proportional to uncertainty, the risk and cost of being wrong, and the possibility of correcting the decision later. In products under validation, an experience that is good enough to test the hypothesis is often sufficient, while critical and difficult-to-reverse decisions require more investigation. Likewise, in mature products, changing an interface can generate more adaptation cost than value. In the end, UX maturity is not about doing more, but knowing where the next minute of UX will produce the most value.

---

I have been thinking a lot about the importance some startups place on UX. Not because I think it is exaggerated. Quite the opposite. Understanding the user, their needs, and how they interact with a product is fundamental. What catches my attention is when the effort dedicated to the experience seems disproportionate to the number of things we still do not know about the product itself.

A startup can spend weeks researching users, designing journeys, prototyping flows, and refining an experience that, a few weeks later, ceases to exist because the business hypothesis was not confirmed. The product may pivot, the audience may change, or the carefully designed functionality may simply be discarded. For those who work closely with technology and architecture, the situation is familiar: we can spend a great deal of time discussing the best solution to a problem that may cease to exist before we even finish implementing it.

This made me think that there is an important difference between considering UX and investing deeply in UX. A product under validation may need an experience that is good enough for its hypothesis to be tested, without that meaning optimizing every detail before knowing whether we are building the right thing. If the experience is bad enough to prevent the user from understanding the value proposition, it compromises the experiment itself. But beyond that condition, there is a decision to be made about how much additional effort is actually worthwhile.

Perhaps the question is not how much UX we should do, but what decision we need to make now, how much uncertainty lies behind it, and how much effort is worth investing to reduce it.

## UX Is Also a Strategic Decision

It is common to associate UX with a set of practices that, when placed in sequence, seem to form a predictable process: research, interviews, journeys, prototypes, tests, and refinements. All of them can produce enormous value, but there is a risk when this process starts being treated as a recipe. A methodology should help reduce uncertainty, not determine in advance how much effort each problem deserves. The fact that an approach works very well in a particular context does not mean it needs to be applied with the same depth in every other one.

Every product operates under constraints. There are deadlines, limited team capacity, budgets, competitive pressure, technical dependencies, operational risks, and different levels of uncertainty. An experimental feature in a product that is still trying to find its market does not carry the same risk as a change to a critical flow used daily by thousands of people. Likewise, redesigning an interface that has been used for years by specialized users can have very different consequences from designing an experience for someone entering the product for the first time.

These constraints also mean that the time devoted to one decision is no longer available for others. A week spent on research is a week that will not be used to implement another hypothesis, put a solution into production, or investigate another problem. This does not mean that research is wasteful. It simply means that the effort dedicated to UX competes with other forms of learning and delivery and therefore also needs to be allocated according to the value it can produce.

The same reasoning applies to the level of rigor. A reversible, low-risk decision may justify a simpler process than a change that is difficult to undo and has a major impact on users. A still-uncertain hypothesis may deserve a quick experiment, while a change to a critical operation may justify much more careful investigation. In some cases, it will be necessary to research before building. In others, it may be more efficient to build something simple, put it into production, and observe real behavior. In still others, the best decision may be not to change an experience that already works.

This is where UX becomes a strategic decision. The question is not only to discover what experience could be better, but to decide how much effort is worth investing to reach that answer. The cost of investigating, designing, and testing needs to be considered alongside the cost of being wrong, the potential impact of the decision, and how difficult it will be to correct it later. The greater the risk and the lower the reversibility, the stronger the case tends to be for investing in evidence before deciding. This choice does not necessarily belong to a UX professional or department. In many products, especially in the early stages, there may not even be a function dedicated to the discipline. It is up to whoever leads the product or project to decide how much time, budget, and uncertainty they are willing to assume.

There is, however, an important counterpoint to the idea of simply putting something into production and learning afterward. Experimenting directly can be an excellent strategy when the decision is reversible and the cost of error is low. But not every decision offers that possibility. Some changes create dependencies, alter established behaviors, or affect users at a scale that makes later correction much more expensive. In these cases, discovering that the decision was wrong may happen too late for the learning to have an acceptable cost. The speed of learning is only an advantage when there is still room to act on what was learned.

Therefore, the question should not be only which set of activities needs to be executed, but which uncertainty needs to be reduced, how valuable it is to obtain that information before deciding, and what the most efficient way to obtain it is. In some cases, this will require an in-depth investigation. In others, a simple hypothesis put into production may produce more relevant information. The difference lies in recognizing that the level of investigation is also a product decision, conditioned by risk, cost, and the consequences of being wrong.

## Before Optimizing the Experience, We Need to Know Whether There Is an Experience Worth Optimizing

This may be the easiest case to visualize when talking about a product under validation. A team may be trying to discover whether the identified problem is actually relevant, whether the value proposition makes sense, and whether there is someone willing to use or pay for it. While these questions remain open, spending weeks refining the experience may mean dedicating effort to a dimension of the product that is not yet its primary source of uncertainty.

This logic, in fact, is not unfamiliar to contemporary UX practices themselves. Approaches such as Lean UX and Product Discovery are based precisely on the idea of working with hypotheses, identifying the main uncertainties, and directing investigative effort toward what can change a decision. The point is not to eliminate research, prototyping, or testing, but to understand which question needs to be answered before deciding how much rigor to apply. In a product under validation, the most important question may concern the problem or the value proposition, rather than the details of the experience.

This does not mean deliberately building something bad. If the experience is so confusing or difficult that it prevents the user from understanding the value proposition, the experiment itself will be compromised. In that case, UX ceases to be refinement and becomes a condition for the hypothesis to be tested. There is an important difference between making a product usable enough to learn from it and trying to make it excellent before knowing whether we are building the right thing.

As answers emerge, the nature of the problem may also change. It may be necessary to understand whether a real problem exists, which parts of the solution actually matter, and, later on, how to improve conversion, retention, efficiency, or satisfaction. Refining the experience starts to make more sense as the more fundamental uncertainties are reduced. The discovery process itself can therefore indicate when it is worth deepening the investment in UX.

The risk lies in reversing this order. We can build an extremely well-resolved experience for a product that will be pivoted a few weeks later, or dedicate time to a feature that will be discarded when we discover how users actually use the system. In this scenario, the problem was not doing UX poorly. It was doing the right work at the wrong time, consuming time that could have been used to answer a more important question.

It makes no sense to optimize an experience before knowing whether there is an experience worth optimizing.

## Professional Experience Reduces Effort, but Does Not Replace Context

Experienced professionals can make decisions more quickly because they accumulate knowledge, recognize patterns, and have already faced similar problems. This allows them to identify paths that probably will not work, anticipate some consequences, and reduce the effort required to reach a decision. The problem begins when this knowledge is treated as a substitute for context.

A solution that worked in one product may not work in another. Different users have different goals, knowledge, and expectations. A flow used occasionally by consumers may have completely different characteristics from an operation performed hundreds of times by a specialized professional. Experience can reduce the effort required for investigation, but it does not eliminate the need to understand for whom, where, and why that decision is being made.

In practice, there is also the pressure of development itself. Deadlines, launch commitments, and time-to-market expectations often create situations in which a decision needs to be made within hours, even though an adequate investigation could take days. In these cases, it is natural to ask the UX professional to be faster. The problem is when speed comes to mean simply compressing an activity that, by its nature, needs a certain amount of time to produce reliable evidence. If an investigation needs a few days to adequately answer a question, reducing it to a few hours can produce something useful, but not necessarily equivalent. We may be reducing the scope of the investigation, changing the type of evidence obtained, or simply accepting a higher level of uncertainty.

This does not mean every investigation needs to be long. A quick exploratory study can be perfectly appropriate when the question is simple and the risk is low. An informal test can reveal an obvious problem and prevent a bad decision. The point is not to confuse these shortcuts with compressed versions of investigations that would require another level of rigor. Reducing the scope of an investigation is different from simply compressing its duration.

This distinction is particularly important when there is a business decision putting pressure on the outcome. Sometimes, the correct conclusion in the face of a deadline is not to try to reproduce in a few hours the work that would normally take days. It may be to recognize that there is not enough time to obtain a certain level of evidence, reduce the ambition of the decision, choose a more reversible alternative, or explicitly accept a higher degree of uncertainty. Speed can be part of the decision, but it does not automatically turn a limited investigation into a complete one.

Perhaps one of the most important manifestations of professional experience is precisely recognizing this difference. An experienced professional can look at a decision and conclude that further investigation is not worthwhile because the risk is low, the solution is reversible, or the available knowledge is already sufficient. But they can also recognize when they do not have enough information to answer confidently, even when there is pressure to reach an answer immediately.

Experience, therefore, does not eliminate the discovery process. It helps decide when it needs to be deeper, when it can be simpler, and when it may not need to happen at all.

## Not Doing UX Does Not Mean Not Considering UX

Not performing a specific UX activity is different from removing UX from the product evolution cycle. A team may decide not to conduct research because the cost of the investigation is not justified given the risk involved, not to create a prototype because the change is small and reversible, or not to redesign an interface because current users already have mastery of it. In all these cases, UX remains part of the decision. What changes is the form and intensity with which the discipline is used.

This becomes particularly evident in mature products. Not every product needs to constantly change its interface to improve the experience. A tool used daily by specialized professionals may have users who have developed, over the years, familiarity, muscle memory, shortcuts, and very specific mental models about how its operations work. A change may make the interface more modern, consistent, or intuitive for newcomers, but represent only a relearning cost for those who already master the system.

As a frequent user of products such as those from AWS, some interface changes cause me more frustration than satisfaction. Not necessarily because the new interface is objectively worse, but because an interaction that had already been incorporated into the workflow no longer worked as expected. The benefit of an apparently more modern interface needs to be compared with the cost of making experienced users relearn something they already mastered.

This reveals an important characteristic of the experience in mature products: stability can also be a UX characteristic. For a new user, discoverability may be a priority. For an advanced user, speed, predictability, and familiarity may be worth much more. A change that improves one of these dimensions can harm another. Therefore, there is not necessarily a better experience in absolute terms, but an experience that is more appropriate for a given user, context, and objective.

In this scenario, not changing something can also be a UX decision. Preserving a familiar interaction may be more appropriate than redesigning it. Postponing research may be more rational than conducting it without a relevant question to answer. Not starting a redesign project may be more responsible than introducing a change whose benefit is uncertain and whose adaptation cost is known.

## Cost Is Also Part of the Decision

It would be equally mistaken to turn this argument into a defense of speed at any cost. Not conducting an investigation can create rework. Not testing an interaction can result in abandonment. Not considering accessibility can exclude users. Not properly analyzing a critical operation can produce errors whose financial impact exceeds the effort required for a more careful investigation many times over.

Therefore, the decision should not simply be treated as “do UX” versus “do not do UX.” There is a cost on both sides. Investigating, testing, and refining consume time, money, and team capacity. That time is also no longer available to implement another hypothesis, analyze another problem, or put a solution into production. On the other hand, failing to investigate can increase the probability of a wrong decision and transfer the problem to a later stage, when fixing it may be more expensive.

The greater the potential impact of a wrong decision, the stronger the case tends to be for investing in evidence before implementing it. The more reversible the decision and the lower the cost of error, the stronger the case may be for experimenting directly and learning from the result. Between these extremes lies a wide variety of situations, and that is precisely why there is no single level of rigor appropriate for every problem.

A small, easily reversible visual change does not necessarily need the same process as a change to a critical flow. An acquisition experiment does not necessarily need the same level of investigation as a change that could affect a financial operation. In each case, there is a different relationship between cost, risk, impact, and reversibility.

The level of rigor, therefore, needs to follow the risk. The goal is not to minimize investment in UX, just as it is not to maximize the amount of work performed. It is to find a level of investigation and refinement proportional to the cost of being wrong, the effort required to obtain evidence, and the ability to correct the decision later.

Perhaps this is the most pragmatic way to look at the issue. Not asking only how much it costs to do UX, nor only how much it costs not to do it, but how much it is worth obtaining a given piece of information before making a decision. When information can significantly change the direction of the product, investing to obtain it tends to make more sense. When there is little probability that it will change the decision, it may be better to act, observe, and learn from the result.

## When Doing Less Can Mean Doing Better

There is no universally correct amount of UX. There is an appropriate investment for the decision facing the team, considering what we still do not know, the risk of being wrong, the cost of investigating, and the possibility of correcting the decision afterward. The investment in experience should follow these variables, rather than a predefined number of activities.

The problem appears at both extremes. When methodology becomes a procedural obligation, every decision goes through the same sequence of research, prototyping, and validation, regardless of context. The process can then stop reducing uncertainty and start merely consuming time. On the other hand, when pressure for speed turns every investigation into a superficial activity, the team can create a false sense of validation and discover too late that it made a bad decision.

Maturity lies precisely in distinguishing these situations. Sometimes the best decision is to research. Sometimes to prototype. Sometimes to launch a solution that is good enough and observe real behavior. And sometimes, especially in mature products, to look at an interface that could be modernized and conclude that the best thing for users is to leave it exactly as it is.

This does not mean UX has become less important. It means UX does not need to be treated as a procedural obligation. It can be understood as part of the decision about where the team's effort will produce the most value.

Perhaps maturity is not about doing more UX. Perhaps it is about knowing where the next minute of UX will produce the most value.
