Metaverse thesis: the core intuition—that computing will become spatial, contextual, and integrated with the physical world—may be valid; the potentially mistaken assumption was that this requires replacing physical reality with persistent virtual environments. VR remains well suited to games, simulation, training, engineering, architecture, and 3D visualization, but everyday computing may favor AR because it preserves the user’s existing environment rather than requiring deliberate entry into a digital one. The smartphone succeeded partly because it augmented existing reality instead of creating an alternative one.

AR can be understood as bringing digital information into physical context. A mature AR navigation system, for example, could indicate directions directly on streets instead of requiring users to translate a 2D map into the physical environment. The distinction is therefore functional rather than absolute: VR maximizes immersion in another environment; AR integrates information with the environment already occupied by the user.

Video-game HUDs provide a useful model. A HUD exposes otherwise imperceptible or inconvenient system states—health, ammunition, maps, objectives, time—at the moment decisions must be made. Real-world instruments serve the same purpose because humans cannot directly perceive many relevant states: vehicle speed, aircraft parameters, industrial pressure, temperature, vibration, etc. Current interfaces separate this information from the context of action by placing it on dashboards or screens. AR could spatially associate information with the physical object or activity: machinery parameters over components, engineering data over constructions, navigation over roads, or equipment information in a healthcare environment.

However, real-world AR cannot rely on a fixed HUD because relevant information varies by user, activity, environment, and objective. A technician, tourist, doctor, patient, pedestrian, and driver may require different information in the same location. The central AR problem is therefore not merely displaying information but determining what is relevant and what should remain hidden. A conventional AR interface that constantly exposes notifications, applications, messages, maps, and advertisements would merely turn the smartphone into an attention-consuming visual layer. Useful AR should minimize rather than maximize displayed information.

AI changes this problem by enabling contextual interfaces. A contextual agent could perceive the environment, identify relevant objects and states, infer the user’s activity and objective, retrieve associated information, and dynamically determine what to present. For example, it could recognize a machine, detect an active alert, infer that a technician is inspecting it, surface only relevant diagnostic information, correlate the alert with historical data, explain the issue, retrieve the repair procedure, and guide execution step by step.

The distinction is between HUD and agent: a HUD communicates system state; an agent interprets system state in relation to the user’s objective and can potentially act. This continues a broader evolution from computer-as-destination toward computing-as-an-ambient layer: PC → smartphone → potentially computing distributed around the user. Traditional graphical interfaces still require users to understand applications, menus, functions, searches, and commands. Agents can instead accept intentions and determine how to achieve requested outcomes.

JARVIS illustrates the relevant concept less through its holographic display than through contextual intelligence: environmental awareness, system monitoring, interpretation of the user’s activity, anticipation of needs, relevant information presentation, and action execution. The important future interface component may therefore be the intelligence behind the display rather than display properties such as resolution, field of view, refresh rate, holography, or gestures. Multimodal models, computer vision, sensors, tool-using agents, and language interfaces already provide some necessary components, although a continuous, natural, reliable integration is still far from JARVIS.

An AI-based HUD could be generated dynamically rather than designed as a permanent layout. Navigation information could appear while traveling; operational data while working; object information while studying; and the interface could disappear during ordinary conversation. The interface becomes contextual and temporary rather than fixed.

This also changes the attention model. Contemporary computing frequently interrupts users through notifications, icons, and applications competing for attention. A contextual interface could instead determine when information is actually needed and remain invisible otherwise. The quality of AR may therefore correlate with its ability to disappear: better contextual understanding requires less persistent visual output.

The broader model combines three functions: AR solves spatial placement by putting information where it is relevant; AI solves contextual interpretation by understanding what is happening; agents solve operational execution by acting on that understanding. Together they could reduce the distance between human intention and action: the user could look at equipment, ask what is happening, have the system identify the equipment and context, retrieve relevant data, explain the problem, and execute an authorized action without manually opening applications, locating dashboards, navigating menus, or switching screens.

The proposed evolution is therefore not primarily “more information in front of the eyes,” but computing that adapts to human context instead of requiring humans to adapt to machine interfaces. The hypothesis remains uncertain because glasses may face unresolved form-factor, battery, optical, privacy, cost, and social-acceptance constraints, and users may prefer screen-based computing.

The metaverse’s decline can consequently be interpreted as separating two previously coupled propositions: computing may become increasingly spatial, contextual, and environmentally integrated, while that transformation does not imply that users must enter persistent virtual worlds. The alternative trajectory is computing entering and understanding everyday physical environments rather than users entering the computer.
