The introduction frames the metaverse’s decline after the 2021–2022 period not necessarily as a rejection of spatial computing, but as a possible failure in choosing the direction through which computing should become more integrated with everyday life. The central hypothesis is that the intuition of moving computing beyond conventional screens may have been correct, while the assumption that this required abandoning the physical world may have been mistaken.

The discussion distinguishes virtual reality’s legitimate applications from its limitations as a general-purpose everyday interface. VR remains useful for games, simulation, training, engineering, architecture, and visualization, where immersion is itself valuable, but making people routinely wear headsets and deliberately enter digital environments creates friction for activities that already function adequately. Augmented reality represents a different proposition: rather than replacing the physical environment, it can add digital information to it, potentially allowing people to navigate, understand, and interact with the world without leaving it. The smartphone is presented as a precedent because its success came from extending existing reality rather than creating an alternative one.

The distinction between VR and AR is therefore framed primarily as a difference in problem-solving rather than a binary judgment about the technologies. VR transports users into a simulated environment, which is advantageous when the goal is immersion or environmental substitution. AR keeps users in their existing environment while bringing digital information into that environment. For everyday computing, this could be significant because the computer would adapt to the physical context instead of requiring the user to adapt their surroundings and attention to the computer.

Video-game HUDs provide a model for understanding why contextual overlays could be useful. A HUD exposes information about system states that the player cannot directly perceive, such as health, objectives, ammunition, direction, or remaining time, allowing decisions to be made without leaving the game environment. Real-world systems have the same problem: drivers, pilots, industrial operators, and other professionals cannot directly perceive every relevant variable, so dashboards, instruments, displays, and alarms create a second informational layer. AR could bring this information closer to the physical context where decisions occur, allowing technicians, engineers, drivers, and healthcare professionals to see relevant data directly associated with the objects or environments they are interacting with.

The analogy with HUDs has an important limitation because real-world contexts are far less predictable than games. A fixed interface cannot efficiently serve everyone because different users performing different activities require different information. Consequently, the central challenge of useful AR is not merely displaying information within the field of view, but determining what information is relevant and what should remain hidden. Poor AR could simply distribute the smartphone interface across the user's vision, creating another stream of notifications and distractions. More useful AR would understand the user's context and selectively expose information according to the activity and objective.

Artificial intelligence potentially changes AR from a static display system into a contextual system. Instead of permanently presenting machine parameters, for example, an AI-based system could recognize the equipment, detect an active alert, infer that the technician is investigating it, retrieve historical information, explain the problem when asked, provide the appropriate procedure, and guide the technician through execution. The distinction is between a HUD that reports system state and an agent that interprets system state in relation to the user's objective. This also represents a broader shift in computing: rather than requiring users to locate applications, menus, functions, and information, agents can increasingly interpret natural-language intentions and determine how to accomplish the requested outcome.

Spatial perception could extend this agent model substantially. An agent capable of seeing what the user sees, understanding their location and activity, and accessing relevant digital systems would become more than a chatbot displayed through different hardware; it could function as a cognitive layer over the physical environment. The JARVIS example from *Iron Man* illustrates this concept because its defining feature is not the voice or holographic interface but the apparent ability to understand context, monitor systems, infer needs, provide relevant information, and execute actions. From this perspective, much discussion about future interfaces may overemphasize displays, resolution, field of view, refresh rates, holograms, and gestures while underemphasizing the intelligence that determines what the interface should actually do.

The necessary components for such systems are beginning to exist independently, although their integration remains far from the JARVIS ideal. Multimodal models can process images, audio, and language; computer-vision systems can identify objects and environments; sensors can determine position and movement; and software agents can access tools, systems, and execute tasks. The unresolved challenge is combining these capabilities into a continuous, sufficiently natural interface in which computing becomes an ambient layer accompanying human activity rather than an object that must be deliberately consulted.

AI could also change the HUD from a predefined interface into a dynamically generated one. Instead of maintaining a permanent collection of visual elements, the system could construct the information layer required by the current situation: navigation while traveling, operational information while working, contextual knowledge while examining an object, or no visible interface at all during a conversation. In this model, the interface becomes responsive to context rather than a fixed structure that users must continuously manage.

This contextual approach could also change computing's relationship with human attention. Current computing frequently operates by interrupting users through notifications, icons, applications, and other mechanisms that demand attention and encourage users to interrupt their ongoing activity. A contextual interface could instead determine when attention is actually required and remain unobtrusive otherwise. Under this model, effective AR may become less visible as its contextual understanding improves: the system's success would partly be measured by how rarely it needs to intrude on the user's attention.

The broader transformation may therefore emerge from combining three distinct capabilities: AR addresses the spatial problem by placing information where it is relevant; AI addresses the contextual problem by interpreting what is happening; and agents address the operational problem by taking action based on that understanding. Together, these technologies could create a computing model in which users interact primarily through intent rather than application navigation. A person could look at equipment, ask what is happening, have the system identify the object, retrieve relevant data, explain the situation, and execute an authorized action without manually opening applications, locating dashboards, or navigating multiple screens. The interface effectively recedes because the system can translate intention into action.

The resulting conception of spatial computing is therefore less about increasing the amount of information presented visually and more about reducing the distance between human intention and computer-mediated action. Computing has historically become more powerful while retaining interaction models that require humans to understand and accommodate the machine's structure. The proposed next step reverses that relationship by making computing increasingly responsive to human context, potentially allowing the system to determine how best to fulfill an expressed intention rather than requiring the user to know which computational mechanism to invoke.

The conclusion maintains significant uncertainty about this trajectory. Technical and social constraints—including device form factor, battery life, optics, privacy, cost, and whether people actually want a persistent digital layer over reality—could prevent AR glasses or related interfaces from becoming mainstream, and conventional screen-based computing may remain dominant for a long time. Nevertheless, the decline of the metaverse can be interpreted as useful because it separates two ideas that were previously conflated: the possibility that computing will become increasingly spatial, contextual, and integrated with the environment, and the separate assumption that achieving this requires people to enter persistent virtual worlds. The unresolved question is therefore not whether the metaverse's particular model succeeds, but whether spatial computing ultimately develops by bringing humans into computers or by making computers increasingly capable of understanding the physical and contextual environment in which humans already operate.
