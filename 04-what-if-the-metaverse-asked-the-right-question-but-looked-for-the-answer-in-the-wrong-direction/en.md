# What if the metaverse asked the right question, but looked for the answer in the wrong direction?

_A speculation about augmented reality, HUDs, contextual agents, and the future of computing interfaces_

**Summary:** I think the metaverse was not wrong to imagine computing becoming more integrated with the real world, but rather to assume that this would require abandoning the physical world. The more promising direction seems to be another one: combining augmented reality, AI, and contextual agents to place information in the environment, understand context, and act based on the user’s intent. In this model, the interface stops demanding our attention and starts adapting to what we are doing. Perhaps the future of spatial computing is not entering the computer, but making the computer enter our everyday lives.

---

At some point between 2021 and 2022, it seemed that the future of computing had been decided. The metaverse would be the next major platform. Companies would invest billions of dollars, social networks would transform into virtual worlds, meetings would take place in digital offices, and we would spend increasingly more time using virtual reality headsets. A few years later, the word had practically disappeared from the technological vocabulary. Enthusiasm gave way to artificial intelligence, agents, and, interestingly, a new generation of physical devices. It is easy to interpret this as a rejection of the idea of spatial computing, but perhaps that conclusion is too quick. What if the metaverse did not get the question wrong, but merely the vector?

Perhaps the intuition that computing could stop being confined to screens is correct. Perhaps the problem was imagining that, to make computing more present in our world, we would need to abandon the real world. Virtual reality continues to have extremely interesting applications in games, simulators, training, engineering, architecture, and three-dimensional visualization. The problem arises when we try to turn this experience into an everyday computing interface. To do that, we need to convince people to put a device on their face, alter their relationship with their surroundings, and deliberately enter a digital experience to perform activities that often already work well enough without it. A technology can be impressive without being indispensable.

The smartphone won for a very different reason. It did not need to create an alternative world. It made the existing world more accessible. It put a camera, telephone, maps, internet, music, and communication into a single device that could be taken anywhere. There was no need to learn a new reality to use it. The device simply fit into the reality we already had. Perhaps augmented reality has a proposal closer to this logic. Imagine walking through an unfamiliar city. Today, we take our smartphone out of our pocket, open a map application, search for the address, interpret a two-dimensional representation of the city, and look back at the real world. In mature augmented reality, perhaps none of this would be necessary. You would simply look at the street and a direction would appear in the environment. Digital information would not replace reality: it would make it more informative.

This difference may seem small, but it could be fundamental. Virtual reality tries to transport the user into a digital environment. Augmented reality tries to transport digital information into the user’s environment. This is not necessarily about deciding that one technology is good and the other is bad. It is about recognizing that they can solve different problems. Immersion is an advantage when we want to simulate another environment. For everyday computing, however, it may be more interesting to keep the user in the world they are already in and make the computer adapt to that world.

Video games offer a particularly precise metaphor for this. A HUD, or Heads Up Display, is a layer of information placed over the environment of a game. It can show health, ammunition, a map, direction, objectives, time, and countless other variables without requiring the player to leave the world in which they are immersed. The reason HUDs exist is deeper than simply improving the visual experience. A game is a complex system, and the player needs to make decisions about that system without being able to directly perceive all of its internal states. They cannot directly observe their exact health level, know the position of all objectives, or know precisely how much time remains for a given action. The HUD transforms abstract system states into information that can be quickly perceived and interpreted.

There is a direct parallel with real life. A driver cannot directly perceive the exact speed of a vehicle simply by looking at the road. A pilot cannot simultaneously observe all aircraft parameters. An industrial operator cannot see pressure, temperature, flow rate, vibration, and hundreds of other variables distributed throughout a facility. That is why we create instruments, panels, displays, and alarms. These interfaces exist because the world has relevant states that are not directly perceptible through our senses. Current technology solves this problem by creating a second informational reality. The driver looks at the dashboard, the pilot looks at the instruments, and the operator looks at a screen. The information is available, but it is separated from the context in which the decision takes place.

Augmented reality could bring these two things closer together. A technician looking at a machine could view its parameters directly over the corresponding components. An engineer could see project information overlaid on the physical object they are building. A driver could receive a spatial indication directly on the road. A healthcare professional could view relevant information about a piece of equipment without having to divert their attention to another screen. In this sense, the HUD in video games is not merely a convenient metaphor for augmented reality. It represents a solution we already know for a fundamental human-computer interaction problem: making relevant information perceptible at the moment when we need to make a decision.

But there is an important difference between a video game HUD and what truly interesting augmented reality could do. A traditional HUD is designed in advance. Its designers know what information the player will probably need and create an interface to present it. The real world is much more complex. There is no single interface that works for everyone, everywhere, and for every activity. An engineer looking at a machine needs different information from a tourist looking at the same object. A doctor needs different information from a patient. A person walking through a city needs different information from someone driving. This means that the challenge of augmented reality is not simply putting graphics in the field of view. It is knowing what to show and, perhaps even more importantly, knowing what not to show.

Bad augmented reality would simply be a smartphone spread across the field of view, with notifications, messages, maps, advertisements, and applications constantly competing for our attention. Truly useful augmented reality would need to do the opposite. It should understand the context and show only what is relevant. This is where artificial intelligence completely changes the equation. Imagine a technician entering an industrial room. A traditional HUD could permanently display temperature, pressure, vibration, and other indicators, but perhaps none of this information is relevant at that moment. A contextual agent could recognize the machine in front of them, identify that there is an active alert, realize that the technician is inspecting that equipment, and present only the information related to the problem. If the technician asks what happened, the agent can correlate the alert with historical data. If they ask how to solve it, it can present the procedure. If they begin carrying out the procedure, it can guide them step by step. In this scenario, we are no longer talking only about an interface. We are talking about a system that perceives, interprets, decides what is relevant, and can act.

This is the difference between a HUD and a contextual agent. The HUD informs about the state of the system. The agent understands the state of the system in relation to the user’s objective. This distinction may seem subtle, but it is enormous. For decades, interaction with computers was based on a very specific premise: the computer is somewhere, and we need to go to it. First we went to the personal computer. Then we put the computer in our pocket. Now we may be reaching a point where the computer no longer needs to be in any specific place. It can be around us. But there is another problem. Even with computers available everywhere, we still need to learn how to operate them. We open applications, navigate menus, search for functions, type queries, and select options. The graphical interface radically improved usability, but preserved a fundamental idea: the user needs to figure out how to ask the computer for what they want.

Artificial intelligence agents are beginning to reverse this relationship. Instead of asking which application we should open, we can simply express an intention. Instead of looking for the right function, we can describe the result we want. Instead of knowing where certain information is, we can ask for it. The machine begins to take on part of the work of figuring out how to accomplish the task. When we add spatial perception to this, the change could be even greater. An agent that can see what we see, understand where we are, interpret what we are doing, and access the relevant digital systems ceases to be merely a chatbot with a different screen. It becomes a cognitive layer over the environment.

This is where JARVIS, from *Iron Man*, becomes an interesting reference. The most futuristic part of JARVIS is not his voice or the holographic interface. It is the fact that he appears to understand the situation. He knows the environment, monitors systems, interprets what Tony Stark is doing, anticipates needs, presents relevant information, and can execute actions. The visual interface is merely the manifestation of that intelligence. Perhaps we have spent too much time imagining the future of interfaces from the perspective of the display, discussing resolution, field of view, refresh rate, holograms, and gestures, when the most important element may be behind the interface. A better screen does not know what you want. A transparent display does not know what you are looking at. A hologram does not know why you are looking at a particular object. A contextual agent can, in principle, know.

This possibility becomes especially interesting with the development of multimodal models and software agents. We are still very far from JARVIS, but some of the necessary components already exist. Models can interpret images, audio, and language. Computer vision systems can identify objects and environments. Sensors can determine position and movement. Agents can use tools, access systems, and execute tasks. What is missing is to integrate these capabilities into a continuous and sufficiently natural interface so that the computer stops being an object we consult and becomes a layer that accompanies what we do.

This could transform the very nature of the HUD. A traditional HUD is designed. An AI-based interface could be generated. Instead of dozens of elements permanently positioned on the screen, the system could build the interface needed for each situation. If you are traveling, it can show navigation. If you are working, it can show operational data. If you are studying an object, it can show information about it. If you are having a conversation, it can disappear completely. The interface stops being a fixed structure and becomes a response to context.

This change also alters our relationship with attention. Today, much of computing works by capturing our attention. Notifications appear, icons demand interaction, applications compete for our time, and we interrupt what we are doing to consult the machine. A contextual interface could move in the opposite direction. Instead of constantly demanding our attention, it could try to understand when our attention is necessary. Perhaps the goal of good augmented reality is precisely to disappear. The better the system understands context, the less it needs to show.

For this reason, perhaps the great transformation is not augmented reality itself. AR solves the spatial problem and puts information in the right place. AI solves the contextual problem and understands what is happening. Agents solve the operational problem and do something based on that understanding. Together, these technologies can create a form of computing very different from what we are accustomed to. Imagine looking at a piece of equipment and asking what is happening. The system identifies the equipment, understands the context, accesses the relevant data, explains the problem, and, if you authorize it, executes an action. You did not open an application, search for a menu, find a dashboard, or navigate through five screens. You simply asked. The interface disappeared because the computer began to understand the intention.

Perhaps this is the most interesting evolution of spatial computing. Not putting more information in front of our eyes, but reducing the distance between intention and action. For decades, we have built increasingly powerful computers and increasingly sophisticated interfaces, but we have continued adapting ourselves to the logic of the machine. Perhaps the next step is to reverse this relationship and make the machine adapt to the human context.

It is possible that all of this is wrong. Perhaps glasses will never reach a form factor comfortable enough. Perhaps battery life, optics, privacy, and cost are greater obstacles than we imagine. Perhaps people simply do not want a digital layer over reality. Perhaps computing will remain predominantly screen-based for much longer. We do not know.

But perhaps the failure of the metaverse is interesting precisely because it allows us to separate two ideas that were put together. One thing is to believe that computing will become increasingly spatial, contextual, and integrated with the environment. Another is to believe that this requires building a virtual world that people need to enter.

Perhaps the metaverse simply chose one possible direction for a transformation that has not yet ended. What if the future of spatial computing is not entering the computer, but finally making the computer understand where we are?
