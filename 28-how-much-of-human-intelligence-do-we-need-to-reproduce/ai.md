Artificial general intelligence may not require reproducing the full breadth or biological implementation of human intelligence. Human intelligence is an integrated combination of distinct capabilities—learning, memory, abstraction, perception, planning, reasoning, world modeling, tool use, adaptation, and environmental interaction—but the human brain is an existence proof of one implementation, not necessarily an architectural specification. Engineering should distinguish reproducing an implementation from reproducing the capabilities it enables.

“General” should be distinguished from “human” and from domain-specific competence. AGI could be understood functionally as a system able to learn, adapt, transfer knowledge, recombine capabilities, formulate strategies for unfamiliar problems, and pursue goals across sufficiently diverse domains. This does not inherently require reproducing all human cognitive mechanisms, emotions, consciousness, bodily characteristics, or subjective experience. A collection of specialized capabilities is not automatically general; generality requires adaptive composition, transfer, and recombination when facing problems not explicitly anticipated by the architecture. Likewise, human embodiment can be abstracted functionally: physical sensors, virtual environments, simulators, APIs, databases, robots, and other interfaces can provide perception, state information, action, constraints, and feedback without reproducing the human body. Consciousness should similarly not be assumed to be an engineering requirement merely because it is part of human experience; whether subjective experience is necessary for particular intelligent behaviors remains an open scientific and philosophical question.

Biological complexity should not automatically be interpreted as necessary complexity. Evolution produced the human brain through historical accumulation, adaptation, and selection rather than from an explicit specification for general intelligence. Some biological mechanisms may be fundamental, but others may reflect contingent evolutionary paths. Biology provides evidence that certain capabilities and relationships are possible and can reveal useful principles, but it does not necessarily prescribe the implementation for an artificial system. The engineering objective can instead be to identify required functions and determine which mechanisms are sufficient to produce them. As with flight, an artificial system can preserve functional properties while using a mechanism radically different from the biological reference.

This distinction motivates treating intelligence as potentially distributed rather than necessarily concentrated in a single agent. Perception, memory, planning, execution, evaluation, hypothesis testing, and other capabilities could be implemented by specialized components. None would individually need to constitute general intelligence; generality could reside in the architecture’s ability to decompose problems, select relevant capabilities, coordinate them, interpret their outputs, and adapt their composition to context. The unit of analysis therefore shifts from the isolated agent to the interacting system and its loops.

Distributed intelligence does not eliminate complexity; it reorganizes it. Coordination requires shared context, state, communication, conflict resolution, authority, consistency, fault tolerance, prioritization, and mechanisms for handling partial failures and incompatible local objectives. More agents increase potential specialization but also increase communication and coordination costs. Breadth of available competencies is insufficient: a system composed of many specialized agents remains merely a collection of specialists unless it can dynamically determine which capabilities are relevant, question unreliable results, revise plans when evidence changes, reformulate problems, and decide when further investigation or alternative hypotheses are needed.

Operational intelligence can be modeled through an OODA cycle: Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. The system observes the environment, interprets information in context, chooses an action, acts, and receives new information from the resulting state. This makes intelligence a continuous interaction process rather than only a static property of a component. Temporal scale becomes an architectural concern: fast environmental reactions, intermediate planning loops, and slower strategic evaluation may require different latencies and synchronization patterns. Intelligent capability therefore depends not merely on throughput but on sustaining perception, decision, action, and learning cycles at appropriate timescales.

OODA alone does not guarantee learning. A system can repeatedly observe, decide, and act while repeating the same strategic mistakes. PDCA—Plan, Do, Check, Act—provides a complementary improvement loop: outcomes are evaluated, failures and assumptions are analyzed, and subsequent strategies or procedures are modified. Operational adaptation and learning are therefore distinct. Learning can occur not only in a foundational model but in memory, procedures, tools, decision policies, execution parameters, and coordination strategies. Components can learn when to invoke other components, recognize competence boundaries, use tools more effectively, and modify their own behavior; the architecture can also learn which combinations of capabilities and strategies work best for different problem classes.

Communication, memory, tools, observability, and state form an architectural backbone for such a system. Components can exchange signals through events, messages, queues, streams, APIs, buses, or shared state. The implementation need not resemble biological neural communication; what matters are functional properties such as appropriate latency, reliability, persistence, ordering, and context. A planning component can produce a strategy for execution components; execution can return results and evidence; evaluation can identify failures or inconsistencies; other components can request reevaluation. The objective is not universal shared knowledge but sufficient information flow for each component to fulfill its role in a coordinated process.

Observability can become part of the cognitive architecture rather than merely an operational facility for human operators. Logs, metrics, traces, events, assumptions, resource consumption, constraints, execution times, failures, and environmental effects can become inputs to agents evaluating decisions and detecting recurring patterns. Telemetry can therefore function as evidence about the system’s own behavior. OODA requires signals for observation and orientation; PDCA requires evidence for checking results and improving strategies; memory preserves relevant experience. Communication, observability, and memory consequently participate directly in adaptation.

Tools also become part of the cognitive process. An intelligent system need not internally possess all information required for a task. It can query databases, execute programs, search, simulate situations, call other agents, test hypotheses, and obtain new environmental observations. Intelligence can involve identifying what information is missing, formulating a strategy to obtain it, acting, and using the resulting evidence to determine the next step. The environment and tools thus expand the system’s effective cognitive and operational capabilities.

Composition can produce capabilities unavailable to individual components, but composition itself must be engineered. A perception component may not plan; a planning component may not execute; an execution component may not evaluate; an evaluation component may not redesign strategy. Their coordinated interaction can nevertheless produce behavior broader than any individual capability. Generality may therefore reside in the capacity for composition rather than universal competence within every component. Specialization becomes compatible with generality if the architecture can discover when and how to use specialized capabilities, interpret their outputs, compare conflicting evidence, and integrate them into broader strategies.

The architecture must also manage competing objectives and local-versus-global optimization. Cost reduction can conflict with quality, performance with operational risk, security with immediate execution, and speed with accuracy. Communication alone does not create coordination. The system requires contracts, policies, authority, priorities, arbitration, confidence handling, and mechanisms for determining when decisions should be accepted, reviewed, or rejected. A distributed cognitive architecture therefore inherits familiar distributed-systems problems while adding the difficulty that its components interpret situations and potentially make autonomous decisions.

The body can likewise be understood as a set of functions rather than necessarily as a biological component. It supplies sensing, internal-state information, constraints, action, and continuous environmental interaction. Artificial systems can obtain analogous functions through physical sensors, virtual environments, APIs, databases, simulators, robots, metrics, or other agents. Different environments—documents, financial systems, industrial controls, physical robotics—provide different forms of embodiment, but all can support perception, action, and feedback cycles. Research connecting brain, body, and artificial systems, including work associated with Miguel Nicolelis, is relevant because it challenges an overly isolated conception of the brain, while still leaving open whether the observed biological relationships must be reproduced literally or only their relevant functions.

The central engineering question therefore shifts from reproduction to sufficiency. Instead of asking how much human intelligence must be copied, ask which functional properties are necessary for sufficiently general behavior and what architecture can provide them. A possible architecture combines operational OODA loops, improvement-oriented PDCA loops, specialized agents, communication, memory, tools, observability, objectives, environments, and feedback. None needs to reproduce its biological counterpart. The difficult problems remain: generalization, learning, planning, memory, coordination, uncertainty, reliability, objectives, and computational cost. Distribution may shift complexity rather than remove it.

The resulting hypothesis is not that simply dividing intelligence among agents is sufficient for AGI. The hypothesis is that AGI may not need to be a single entity capable of everything. It could instead be a system capable of discovering which capabilities a new problem requires, composing those capabilities, acting through tools and environmental interfaces, evaluating outcomes, learning from experience, and changing its strategy. Under this view, generality is primarily an architectural capacity for adaptive composition rather than a fixed inventory of human-like competencies.

Human intelligence remains evidence that broad intelligence is possible, but not necessarily a blueprint that artificial systems must reproduce. Some human cognitive characteristics may eventually prove fundamental; others may turn out to be contingent implementations. The key unresolved question is therefore not how much of the human brain must be reconstructed, but what architecture is sufficient to generate sufficiently general, adaptive behavior.
