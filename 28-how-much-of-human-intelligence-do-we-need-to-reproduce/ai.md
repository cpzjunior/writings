The article argues that artificial general intelligence may not require reproducing the full architecture of human intelligence. Human intelligence integrates perception, memory, learning, reasoning, planning, abstraction, tool use, adaptation, and interaction with the environment, but the fact that these capabilities coexist in humans does not establish that their biological implementation is necessary for an artificial system. The author distinguishes reproducing an implementation from reproducing a capability, using engineering analogies such as aircraft and birds: the relevant question is not how to copy the mechanism, but which properties are necessary to produce the desired behavior. Drawing on the relationship between brain, body, and environment explored in Miguel Nicolelis's work, the article treats intelligence as potentially dependent on interactions among multiple functions rather than on an isolated brain-like component. The central proposition is deliberately tentative: instead of assuming that every human characteristic must be reproduced, we should investigate which capabilities are fundamental, which can be replaced by alternative mechanisms, and which behaviors can emerge from the interaction of simpler components.

The concept of “general” intelligence creates a key distinction between reproducing the breadth of human cognition and possessing the functional ability to learn, adapt, transfer knowledge, and solve new problems across domains. A system can be highly competent without being general, while a collection of specialized capabilities is not automatically general unless the architecture can adaptively recombine them for unforeseen problems. This reframes AGI as an engineering problem: identify the functional properties required for generality rather than attempting to reconstruct every human cognitive mechanism. Memory, perception, planning, execution, evaluation, and learning could potentially be separate components coordinated by communication and feedback rather than concentrated in a single entity. The same reasoning applies to consciousness and embodiment: their presence in human intelligence does not demonstrate that they are necessary engineering requirements for producing intelligent behavior. An artificial system might interact with environments through sensors, APIs, databases, simulators, robots, or other mechanisms without reproducing the human body. Generality could therefore be understood as an emergent functional property of an architecture capable of combining capabilities adaptively, rather than as a biological property that must be instantiated in human-like form.

The complexity of the human brain should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that all of its complexity is necessary for intelligence. The brain is the result of evolutionary history, accumulated adaptations, constraints, and contingencies rather than the implementation of an explicit engineering specification. Biology provides evidence that certain capabilities can coexist and produce sophisticated behavior, but it does not necessarily provide the optimal or mandatory architecture for reproducing those capabilities artificially. Engineering can instead begin with desired functions and determine what mechanisms are sufficient to achieve them, while using biology as a source of principles and hypotheses rather than as a blueprint. This does not imply that arbitrary simplification is possible: relationships among perception, memory, action, and learning may contain dependencies that are not yet understood, and some apparent complexity may prove fundamental. The relevant distinction is therefore between reproduction and sufficiency: the goal is neither to copy the brain nor to find the smallest architecture regardless of capability, but to identify an architecture that contains enough functionality and integration to achieve the desired degree of generality without assuming that biological complexity must be replicated.

Intelligence can potentially be understood as a distributed-system property rather than something concentrated in a single agent. Specialized components could handle perception, knowledge retrieval, planning, execution, evaluation, hypothesis testing, or inconsistency detection, with generality residing in the architecture's ability to decompose problems, select resources, coordinate components, and adapt their composition. This does not remove complexity but relocates it into coordination, communication, shared context, conflict resolution, latency, partial failures, and global consistency. The OODA loop—Observe, Orient, Decide, Act—provides a model for continuous interaction with the environment, while PDCA—Plan, Do, Check, Act—adds a mechanism for evaluating outcomes and improving future behavior. Together, these loops distinguish operational adaptation from learning: a system can change its response to circumstances without changing the strategy that produced those responses, whereas learning requires using experience to modify future behavior. Tools become part of the cognitive process because an intelligent system can act to obtain information rather than requiring all relevant knowledge internally. Observability also becomes potentially cognitive: logs, metrics, traces, and events can provide evidence that agents themselves use to evaluate decisions, detect patterns, and modify behavior. Communication infrastructure therefore acts as an architectural backbone through which state, events, evidence, and feedback circulate, although communication alone does not guarantee coordination or intelligence.

A distributed cognitive architecture requires infrastructure capable of transporting information among specialized components with appropriate latency, reliability, persistence, ordering, and context. Agents can publish completed objectives, invalid assumptions, failures, inconsistencies, evaluation requests, strategies, results, and evidence, allowing components to perform bounded responsibilities without requiring every component to know the complete system state. Observability can become an internal source of information rather than merely a mechanism for human operators: telemetry can describe assumptions, resource consumption, constraints, timing, failures, and environmental effects, allowing other components to evaluate decisions and identify recurring patterns. Communication, memory, observability, and events consequently become active participants in the system's learning and adaptation loops. However, distributed communication does not solve coordination: autonomous components can pursue conflicting objectives, provide contradictory recommendations, operate with different confidence levels, or make locally correct decisions that damage global outcomes. A viable architecture would therefore need mechanisms for authority, priority, conflict resolution, consistency, and decisions about when actions should be accepted, reviewed, or rejected. The analogy with distributed systems is useful precisely because it highlights that connecting components is only the beginning; contracts, coordination, fault tolerance, and governance of interactions remain necessary.

The hypothesis that intelligence may reside in composition shifts attention from the capabilities of individual agents to the relationships among them. Specialized components can collectively perform functions that none can perform alone, much as complex networks and distributed software systems derive their behavior from protocols and interactions rather than from a single component. Under this model, generality may depend less on having a universal agent capable of everything and more on the system's ability to determine which specialized capabilities are relevant, combine them appropriately, evaluate their outputs, and adapt the composition when circumstances change. Specialization can therefore be compatible with generality, provided the architecture can discover and coordinate how specialized capabilities should be used. Yet this introduces explicit integration problems: component selection, ordering, context sharing, conflicting information, confidence assessment, incomplete execution, competing objectives, and decisions about when to continue investigating or change strategy. A large collection of specialized agents can create the appearance of broad capability without actually producing general intelligence. The architecture must instead be able to adapt its own composition, connect operational cycles with learning cycles, and transform experience into changes in strategy or coordination. The resulting hypothesis is not that simply distributing intelligence among agents is sufficient, but that the fundamental unit of analysis may be the cycle connecting agents—perception, decision, action, evaluation, and change—rather than the isolated agent itself.

The human body illustrates how functions can be separated from their biological implementation. It provides sensors, constraints, internal state, action capabilities, and continuous environmental interaction, but an artificial system could implement equivalent functions through physical sensors, virtual environments, APIs, databases, simulators, robots, metrics, or other agents. This permits different forms of embodiment in which “body” refers functionally to the interfaces through which a system perceives its environment, receives information about its state, and produces effects. Nicolelis's work is relevant because it emphasizes the relationship among brain, body, and environment, but the architectural question remains whether those relationships must be reproduced biologically or merely functionally. Consciousness presents a similar distinction: subjective experience may be important to human intelligence, but there is insufficient basis to treat it as an established engineering requirement for systems capable of perception, modeling, planning, tool use, execution, evaluation, learning, and adaptation. The article therefore separates the scientific and philosophical question of what consciousness is from the engineering question of which mechanisms are necessary to produce particular intelligent behaviors. Both body and consciousness reinforce the broader principle that a human characteristic can be studied for its function without assuming that the same implementation must exist in an artificial architecture.

The final argument replaces the question “how much human intelligence must we reproduce?” with “which properties must we implement to produce sufficiently general behavior?” Under this framing, OODA represents operational perception, decision, and action; PDCA represents evaluation and improvement; specialized agents provide capabilities; communication enables composition; observability supplies evidence; memory preserves experience; tools extend action; and environments provide constraints and feedback. None needs to reproduce its biological counterpart, although the overall problem remains difficult because generalization, learning, planning, memory, coordination, objectives, uncertainty, reliability, and computational cost still have to be addressed. AGI might therefore be conceived not as an entity capable of doing everything independently, but as an architecture capable of determining which capabilities a new problem requires, combining them, evaluating the outcome, and modifying its strategy. Whether this is sufficient for AGI remains unresolved, since some characteristics of human cognition may prove fundamental while others may turn out to be only one possible implementation. The concluding proposition is consequently a question rather than a claim of resolution: rather than asking how much human intelligence must be copied, we might investigate what architecture is sufficient to produce the desired degree of generality.
