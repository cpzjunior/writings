# How Much of Human Intelligence Do We Need to Reproduce?

_A reflection by a solutions architect on complexity, abstraction, and artificial general intelligence._

**Summary:** My thesis is that we may not need to reproduce all of human intelligence to build artificial general intelligence. Just as in distributed systems, we can decompose capabilities such as perception, memory, planning, and execution into specialized components, connected through communication, observability, tools, and feedback loops. In this model, intelligence emerges less from an isolated agent and more from coordination between relatively simple parts. The challenge, therefore, is not to copy the brain, but to discover what distributed architecture is sufficient to produce an intelligent system.

---

I have been thinking a lot about what it really means to build artificial general intelligence. Not so much about when it will arrive, nor about which model will be responsible for it, but about a question that seems more fundamental to me: when we talk about artificial general intelligence, how much of what we understand as human intelligence are we assuming needs to be present?

The question seems simple, but it quickly becomes difficult. Intelligence is not a single, well-defined capability. Learning, memorizing, abstracting, perceiving, planning, reasoning, creating models of the world, using tools, adapting to new situations, and interacting with the environment are different capabilities that, in humans, appear deeply integrated. When we try to imagine a machine capable of doing something similar, it is tempting to look at this integration and conclude that some level of the same complexity will also be necessary.

But perhaps that conclusion is merely a consequence of the way we know intelligence. When we talk about general intelligence, humans are the main natural reference we have. We are capable of learning very different things, transferring knowledge across domains, dealing with new situations, creating tools, and modifying the environment around us. It is therefore understandable that our own cognitive architecture is used as a starting point. But a reference is not necessarily a specification.

As a solutions architect, I have an almost automatic tendency to distrust excessively complex systems when we still do not know which parts are actually necessary. Faced with a sophisticated solution, the question that interests me is not necessarily how to reproduce it, but which properties we need to preserve to obtain the result we want. There is an important difference between reproducing an implementation and reproducing a capability. Sometimes, the best solution is not to rebuild the original system, but to abstract what matters and find another way to implement it.

This distinction seems particularly relevant when we think about artificial general intelligence. The human brain is the biological implementation we know of a system capable of producing a wide variety of cognitive capabilities. That does not mean that those capabilities necessarily depend on that specific implementation. An airplane needs to produce lift, stability, and control in order to fly, but it does not need to reproduce the anatomy of a bird. The function can be preserved while the mechanism is completely different.

Perhaps we should ask the same question about intelligence. Instead of starting by trying to reproduce the architecture that biology built, perhaps it would be more productive to identify which capabilities are necessary for a system to learn, generalize, plan, adapt, and solve new problems on a sufficiently broad scale. From there, we can ask which mechanisms are necessary to produce those capabilities and which can be replaced by other solutions.

This question also made me revisit some ideas present in the work of Miguel Nicolelis. His research at the interface between the brain, body, and artificial systems is an interesting reminder that intelligence does not need to be thought of as something isolated inside the brain. Perception, action, the body, and the environment participate in the system that produces behavior. But this observation opens an architectural question that interests me more than biological reproduction itself: do we need to implement these relationships in the same way nature implemented them, or do we simply need to ensure that the relevant functions are present?

The difference is subtle but important. I am not suggesting that the body, perception, memory, learning, or interaction with the environment are irrelevant. Nor that a distributed or agentic architecture is automatically capable of producing general intelligence. The question comes earlier: shouldn't we avoid assuming that a property needs to be reproduced exactly as it exists in humans before demonstrating that this specific implementation is necessary?

Perhaps some human characteristics are indispensable. Perhaps others can be replaced by completely different mechanisms. Perhaps some are simply not necessary for certain types of intelligence. And perhaps properties that appear to require a central component can emerge from the interaction between several smaller components.

I do not have a definitive answer to this. And perhaps that is precisely the point. The discussion about AGI still involves open questions about which capabilities are fundamental, which can be replaced, which need to be integrated, and which properties can emerge from the combination of different systems and mechanisms. My provocation is more modest: perhaps we do not need to reproduce human intelligence. Perhaps we need to discover which properties of it are actually necessary and what architecture is sufficient to produce them.

## What Does It Mean to Be General?

Perhaps part of the difficulty in the discussion about AGI lies precisely in the word “general.” When we talk about general intelligence, we may be mixing at least two different ideas: a machine capable of reproducing the breadth of human capabilities and a machine capable of learning, adapting, and solving new problems across different domains. The two things seem equivalent when we use humans as the reference, but they are not necessarily so. One describes similarity to a specific intelligence. The other describes a functional property of the system.

If we understand AGI as a machine that needs to fully reproduce what we call human intelligence, the complexity of the problem becomes extraordinary. We would have to consider not only reasoning and language, but perception, memory, learning, planning, social interaction, adaptation, bodily perception, motivation, and an enormous number of other phenomena that we do not even fully understand. The more human characteristics we incorporate into the definition, the closer building AGI comes to building an artificial human.

But perhaps “general” does not need to mean “human.” An intelligence can be considered general because it can apply capabilities flexibly across different contexts, learn new tasks, transfer knowledge between problems, build strategies for unknown situations, and deal with goals for which it was not previously given a specified solution. This is a sufficiently demanding definition, but it does not, by itself, require all human cognitive mechanisms to be present.

This also forces us to separate generality from competence. A system can be extremely competent in a specific domain without being general. It can outperform humans on a complex task and still struggle to transfer what it has learned to a structurally different problem. Likewise, a system that combines many specialized competencies is not automatically general simply because it can perform many tasks. At some point, we need a capacity for adaptation, transfer, and recombination that allows it to deal with problems that were not explicitly anticipated by the architecture.

This distinction changes the engineering question. Instead of trying to reproduce all human cognitive characteristics, we can seek to identify which functional properties are necessary to achieve a certain degree of generality. The problem stops being about reconstructing an intelligence we already know and becomes about determining which capabilities need to be present, how they need to interact, and which mechanisms can perform these functions.

This is where the idea of a “fraction” of human intelligence needs to be treated carefully. We are not talking about removing a small part of the brain and expecting it to produce general intelligence. The question is whether a smaller set of capabilities, architecturally different from human cognition, could be sufficient to produce sufficiently general behavior. Perhaps some human capabilities are fundamental. Perhaps others can be replaced by different mechanisms. Perhaps some are simply not necessary for certain types of generality.

This distinction also makes room for a different architectural hypothesis. Some capabilities that, in humans, appear integrated into a single experience could be implemented as distinct components in an artificial architecture. Memory, planning, perception, execution, evaluation, and learning do not necessarily need to be concentrated in a single entity. They could, in principle, be distributed among different components and coordinated through communication and feedback mechanisms. But this introduces an important condition: generality would not simply reside in the existence of these components. It would reside in the system's ability to combine them adaptively when facing new problems.

This is where the question of consciousness can be posed more precisely. A system may not possess consciousness, emotions, or subjective experience and still perform activities we associate with intelligence, such as reasoning, planning, building models, using tools, and adapting its behavior. This does not demonstrate that consciousness is irrelevant to intelligence, nor does it allow us to conclude that it is impossible or unnecessary in any architecture. It simply means that we should not turn a characteristic of human experience into an engineering requirement without first demonstrating that it is functionally necessary for the behavior we intend to produce.

There is an important difference between asking whether a particular characteristic is part of our experience of intelligence and asking whether that characteristic is necessary to produce a given intelligent behavior. These are different questions. The first also belongs to the fields of philosophy of mind and cognitive science. The second can be treated, at least in part, as an architectural question.

The same reasoning applies to the body. Perhaps an artificial intelligence does not need to possess a human body, but that does not mean interaction with an environment can simply be ignored. An organism provides sensors, constraints, internal states, capacity for action, and a continuous relationship with the world. These functions may be relevant to certain types of learning and decision-making, even if their artificial implementation is completely different from the biological one.

A system could use physical sensors, virtual environments, simulators, APIs, databases, robots, or other mechanisms for interacting with the environment. It could receive metrics as state signals, query external memory, and use tools to produce effects on the environment. The point would not be to reproduce the human body, but to understand which functions of interaction with the environment are necessary to produce a given degree of generality.

In that case, the question stops being “how do we reproduce the human body?” and becomes “which functions of interaction with the environment are necessary to produce a given degree of generality?” This is a question much closer to engineering.

And it leads us to an interesting consequence. If generality can be a functional property rather than a biological one, perhaps we also do not need to concentrate it in a single component. We can begin to imagine intelligence as an architecture composed of different capabilities, connected through mechanisms for communication, decision-making, execution, memory, and feedback.

But from that moment onward, a new problem arises. If we distribute the capabilities, who coordinates the system? If there is no central “brain” responsible for integrating everything, where exactly is the intelligence?

Perhaps the answer lies in the architecture itself. This is the point where the discussion stops being only about what an intelligence needs to possess and becomes about how different capabilities can be composed to produce behavior that no component could produce on its own.

## Nature Does Not Need to Be Copied to Be Understood

There is a temptation, when we look at the human brain, to interpret its complexity as an indication that all of that complexity is necessary. If a structure took millions of years to form and is capable of producing such a broad range of capabilities, it seems reasonable to imagine that its countless components and interactions are, in some way, indispensable.

But there is a difference between observed complexity and necessary complexity. The human brain is the product of an extremely long evolutionary history. It brings together mechanisms, adaptations, constraints, and dependencies that were incorporated over different contexts and needs. Many of these characteristics are fundamental to the functioning of the organism as we know it. Others may be consequences of the specific path evolution took. The fact that a characteristic exists in a biological architecture is not, by itself, evidence that it is necessary for any artificial implementation of the capability it helps produce.

Evolution also did not begin with a functional specification stating that it would be necessary to produce an entity capable of writing poetry, building bridges, playing chess, programming computers, and developing scientific theories. It arrived at these capabilities through a historical process in which different solutions were preserved, modified, and combined in response to environmental pressures. There was no initial design of which the current brain is the final implementation. There was a process of accumulation, adaptation, and selection.

We are in a different position. We are not starting from zero and are certainly still subject to computational, energetic, economic, and scientific constraints. But we can, at least in principle, start from the problems we want to solve and deliberately seek an architecture capable of solving them. We can choose which properties we want to produce, which constraints we accept, and which mechanisms seem most appropriate for each function.

This does not mean we can simply choose any capability and implement it in any way. A property may depend on mechanisms we do not yet understand. There may be relationships between perception, memory, action, and learning that we still do not know how to abstract correctly. There may be complexity that appears accidental when observed from the outside but is fundamental to the behavior we want to reproduce. Abstraction, therefore, is not a license to ignore what we do not yet understand.

This is precisely why the distinction between function and implementation is so important. Nature gives us a demonstration that a certain set of capabilities can coexist in a system and produce extraordinarily sophisticated behaviors. It provides evidence about what is possible, about relationships that may be important, and about mechanisms that deserve investigation. But this does not necessarily mean that it provides us with the engineering specification we need to build another system with similar properties.

An airplane does not need to reproduce the anatomy of a bird to fly. It needs to produce sufficient lift, control, stability, and propulsion to perform the desired function. The mechanism is completely different, but certain physical properties need to be present. Engineering did not copy the bird. It abstracted a problem and found another implementation to solve it. Perhaps intelligence can be treated similarly.

Engineering often starts with a function and seeks an appropriate implementation. It does not necessarily start with an existing implementation and attempt to copy it. This difference seems trivial when we think about conventional machines, but it becomes much more interesting when the system we are trying to build is intelligence itself.

Perhaps we should apply the same discipline to artificial intelligence. The goal does not need to be building a machine that is a digital human. It can be building an architecture capable of learning, generalizing, planning, adapting, and solving new problems, even if the way these capabilities are implemented is radically different from the way they emerged in us.

This difference between reproduction and sufficiency is, for me, one of the most interesting points in the discussion about AGI. It shifts the question from “how do we build an artificial brain?” to a question much closer to systems engineering: “what architecture is sufficient to produce the degree of generality we are seeking?”

And there is an important difference between this question and simply looking for the simplest architecture. The smallest architecture possible may not be sufficient, just as an excessively complex architecture may reproduce mechanisms that are not necessary. The engineering problem is precisely to find the point at which we have sufficient capabilities, sufficient integration, and sufficient generality without assuming in advance that we need to reconstruct all the complexity found in biology.

Perhaps this is one of the most interesting contributions an engineering perspective can make to the discussion about artificial intelligence. Instead of starting with what exists and asking how to reproduce it, we can start with the behavior we want to produce and investigate which capabilities are necessary to achieve it. We can study biology to discover principles, mechanisms, and relationships that help answer this question, without turning the human brain into a mandatory architectural blueprint.

Nature remains one of our best sources of evidence about intelligence. But evidence is not a specification. We can study the system it produced without assuming that we need to build another system by following exactly the same path. And this allows us to consider a different possibility: perhaps some of the capabilities we perceive as integrated in a human being can be distributed among different components and still produce integrated behavior.

## Intelligence as a Distributed System

We are accustomed to imagining intelligence as a property of a unit. An individual perceives, interprets, decides, and acts, and this integrated experience is probably one of the reasons why we tend to imagine that an artificial intelligence would also need to be concentrated in a single entity. But this is not necessarily an architectural constraint. The fact that we experience intelligence as something apparently unified does not mean that a machine needs to organize its capabilities in the same way. In distributed systems, complex capabilities can emerge from the composition of specialized components, each responsible for part of the problem. One component handles authentication, another persistence, another processing, another messaging, another observability. None of them represents the entire system. The system exists in the interaction between them, in the interfaces that connect them, and in the rules that determine how their capabilities are combined.

Perhaps we can think about intelligence similarly. Instead of trying to build an artificial entity that concentrates perception, memory, reasoning, planning, execution, and evaluation in a single core, we could build an architecture in which these capabilities are distributed among different agentic components. One agent could interpret the environment, another retrieve and organize knowledge, another plan, another execute actions, another evaluate results, another look for inconsistencies, and another test alternative hypotheses. None of them would need to be, individually, a general intelligence. What would need to be sufficiently general would be the architecture's ability to combine these competencies to deal with new problems. Generality, in this case, would not necessarily reside inside a specific component, but in the system's ability to decompose problems, select resources, coordinate agents, and adapt that composition according to context.

This distinction changes the unit of analysis. Instead of asking whether a particular agent is intelligent, we can ask whether the architecture has mechanisms capable of producing intelligent behavior when its components interact. A payment service does not need to know all the identity rules of an organization, just as an identity service does not need to understand the logic of every transaction that will be performed. Each component has a bounded responsibility, while the system produces results that none of them would produce alone. A cognitive architecture could follow a similar principle: perception, memory, planning, execution, and evaluation could be different responsibilities, provided mechanisms exist to coordinate them consistently.

This, however, does not mean that distributing capabilities eliminates complexity. Often, it simply moves it elsewhere. If we have ten agents, we need to coordinate them; if we have a hundred, the problem grows. We need to decide which agents participate in a task, how they share context, how they resolve conflicts, how they identify inconsistent information, and how they prevent a locally correct decision from producing a globally poor result. We also need to deal with latency, communication cost, shared state, partial failures, and different interpretations of the same problem. A distributed architecture is not an alternative to complexity. It is a different way of organizing it.

For this composition to produce intelligent behavior, however, it is not enough to connect components. Interaction cycles need to be established. Different agents need to be able to perceive states, interpret information, make decisions, execute actions, and observe their consequences. The OODA cycle, Observe, Orient, Decide, Act, offers an interesting abstraction for representing this process. The system observes what is happening, interprets the information within a context, decides on an action, and acts on the environment. The action modifies that environment and produces new information, which feeds the next cycle. Intelligence then ceases to be thought of only as a static property of a component and also becomes understood as a continuous process of interaction.

There is also an architectural property that tends to become less apparent when we think only about components: the temporal scale of the loops. If distributed intelligence depends on perception, communication, decision, action, and feedback, the latency of these interactions becomes part of the system's capability itself. An architecture in which every decision requires successive calls between agents can accumulate delays rapidly, especially when components depend on one another synchronously. This does not mean that every intelligent process needs to operate at the same speed. Different problems require different temporal scales. A system may need fast loops to react to the environment, intermediate loops to plan and execute tasks, and slower loops to evaluate results and modify strategies. The question is not simply how many requests per second the architecture can process, but whether it can sustain the different cycles of perception, decision, action, and learning within the time scales required by the problems it intends to solve.

This perspective also changes the role of tools. An agent does not need to internally possess all the knowledge required to solve a problem. It can query a database, execute a program, call another agent, perform a search, test a hypothesis, simulate a situation, or obtain a new observation from the environment. The ability to act on the world increases the ability to learn about it. An intelligent system does not necessarily need to know the answer before acting; it needs to be able to identify what it needs to know, formulate a strategy to obtain that information, execute an action, and use the result to decide what to do next. In this context, a tool ceases to be merely an operational resource and becomes part of the cognitive process.

OODA, however, mainly represents operation. A system can continuously observe, decide, and act and still repeat the same mistakes. Adapting a decision to the current state of the environment does not necessarily mean learning to improve the strategy itself. This is where the PDCA cycle, Plan, Do, Check, Act, can function as a second abstraction. If OODA represents the operational cycle of perception, decision, and action, PDCA represents a cycle of evaluation and improvement. The system plans an action, executes it, checks the result, and uses the knowledge gained to modify its next attempt. This distinction makes it possible to separate operational adaptation from learning: a system can change its decision in response to a different situation without necessarily modifying the way it makes future decisions.

Imagine an agent responsible for planning. It executes a given strategy and obtains a poor result. In a purely operational architecture, the failure may simply end that execution. In an architecture capable of learning from its own behavior, the failure can become an object of analysis. The system can investigate which assumptions were wrong, which decisions contributed to the outcome, which signals could have indicated the problem before execution, and how the strategy should be modified in similar situations. Learning, in this model, does not need to be concentrated in the foundational model. It can reside in memory, procedures, tools, decision policies, execution parameters, or even in the way different agents are coordinated.

This creates an important possibility. An intelligent architecture would not need to improve only by increasing the capability of a central model. It could also improve through the way its components learn to work together. An agent can learn to use a tool more effectively, recognize when it needs to turn to another agent, or identify the limits of its own competence. The system, in turn, can learn which strategies produce better results and which combinations of capabilities are more effective for different classes of problems. In this case, part of the capability gain would lie in the organization of the architecture itself, rather than necessarily in making each individual component more intelligent.

OODA and PDCA can therefore be understood as two complementary loops. The first relates to interaction with the environment and decision-making; the second, to evaluation and improvement of the process itself. A system could execute thousands of OODA cycles without necessarily improving its strategy. PDCA introduces the possibility of turning the experience accumulated in those cycles into systematic change. In this model, part of intelligence lies not only in making a good decision, but in recognizing when the decision was bad, understanding why it failed, and modifying the process that produced that decision.

This is where observability stops being merely an operational concern and begins to take on a cognitive role. In a traditional architecture, logs, metrics, traces, and events exist mainly to allow humans to understand system behavior. In an agentic architecture, those same pieces of information can be consumed by the components themselves to evaluate results, detect failures, identify patterns, measure performance, and guide future decisions. The system does not need to observe only the external environment. It can also observe itself.

This observability requires communication infrastructure. In an organism, different parts of the system need to exchange signals so that information about state, stimuli, and consequences can influence the behavior of the whole. In an artificial architecture, this function can be implemented through events, messages, queues, streams, APIs, or other distributed mechanisms. The analogy with the nervous system is useful only at this functional level. We do not need to reproduce nerves or biological structures; we need some mechanism capable of transporting relevant information between components that depend on it to make decisions.

This allows us to imagine a kind of architectural backbone formed by communication, state, events, and observability. One agent can publish that a given objective has been completed, another can signal that an assumption is no longer valid, another can record a failure, another can identify conflicting information, and another can request a new evaluation. These pieces of information can feed different agents and loops without there necessarily being a central core that knows the entire state of the system. The infrastructure takes on a fundamental role: it does not decide by itself, but it allows distributed decisions to be influenced by information produced elsewhere in the architecture.

In this model, perhaps there is no single place we can point to and say “this is where the intelligence is.” It would lie in the composition of the components, in the interfaces that enable their interaction, and, above all, in the loops that allow the system to perceive, decide, act, evaluate, and modify its behavior. This does not mean that simple components automatically become intelligent when connected. It means that certain properties can emerge from how different capabilities are organized, coordinated, and subjected to continuous feedback cycles.

The most interesting consequence is that the fundamental unit of analysis is no longer necessarily the agent. It may be the system and its loops. An agent can perceive. Another can reason. Another can execute. Another can evaluate. But it is the ability to transform these activities into a continuous process of perception, decision, action, and learning that can cause the architecture to exhibit behavior much broader than that of any isolated component.

This is perhaps the difference between simply building a collection of agents and building a cognitive architecture. In the first case, we have components capable of executing tasks. In the second, we have components capable of participating in cycles that allow the system to use its own experiences to decide what to do next and, eventually, modify how it makes decisions. Intelligence does not disappear when we stop concentrating it in a single core. It becomes a property of the system and the relationships between its parts.

## A Backbone for Intelligence

If we distribute cognitive capabilities among different components, an immediate problem arises that any distributed-systems architect recognizes: communication. Isolated components can possess sophisticated capabilities, but without mechanisms for sharing information, context, and state, there is no coordinated system. The more we distribute perception, memory, planning, execution, and evaluation, the more important the infrastructure responsible for connecting these capabilities becomes.

In the human organism, different parts of the system continuously exchange information about external stimuli, internal state, movement, and the consequences of actions. We do not need to reproduce the biological mechanisms responsible for this communication to take advantage of its architectural function. The relevant point is that a component needs to be able to produce signals that other components can interpret and use to modify their own behavior. In an artificial architecture, this could occur through events, messages, queues, streams, APIs, buses, shared state, or other communication mechanisms. The implementation can be completely different; the functional requirement remains.

We can imagine this infrastructure as a kind of architectural backbone. One agent could publish that a given objective has been completed. Another could report that an assumption used in a decision is no longer valid. Another could record a failure, detect an inconsistency, or request a new evaluation. A planning component could produce a strategy that will be consumed by execution components, while those components return results, metrics, and evidence to the components responsible for evaluation. The goal would not be for everyone to know everything, but for each component to have access to the signals it needs to fulfill its role within the system.

The analogy with the nervous system can be useful here, provided it remains a functional analogy. We do not need to build artificial nerves to transport signals, just as we do not need to reproduce neurons or muscles to implement communication and action. What we need are mechanisms capable of transporting information with appropriate properties of latency, reliability, persistence, ordering, and context. In a sufficiently complex architecture, communication ceases to be an implementation detail and becomes part of the system's capability itself.

This is where observability takes on a different function from the one we normally assign to it. In traditional systems, logs, metrics, traces, and events exist mainly to allow human operators to understand application behavior. They are instruments for answering what happened, when it happened, where a failure occurred, and which components were affected. In an agentic architecture, however, there is no reason for this information to be intended exclusively for humans. If the components themselves can interpret telemetry, observability can become a source of information for the system to make decisions about its own operation.

Imagine an agent that executes an action and records not only the result, but also the assumptions used, the resources consumed, the constraints encountered, the time required, the failures observed, and the effects produced on the environment. Another component could use this information to evaluate the quality of the decision. A third could identify recurring failure patterns. A fourth could compare different strategies and recommend a procedural change. In this scenario, telemetry ceases to be merely an external description of the system and becomes part of the information the system itself uses to interpret its experience.

This change is particularly relevant to the cycles we discussed earlier. OODA needs signals to observe and orient. PDCA needs evidence to check results and identify opportunities for improvement. Agents need communication to share this information. And some memory mechanism needs to preserve what may be relevant to future decisions. Communication, observability, and memory cease to be merely supporting infrastructure and begin to participate directly in the loops through which the architecture learns and adapts.

This also changes the meaning of some traditional elements of software engineering. A log can be evidence about a decision. A metric can be a signal that a given strategy is degrading. A trace can represent the sequence of decisions that led to an outcome. An event can trigger a new evaluation. A failure can cease to be merely an operational condition and become data for an improvement process. Observability, in this context, is not merely looking at the system. It is providing the system with some of the data it needs to look at itself.

We therefore begin to have an architecture quite different from an isolated model. We have specialized components, communication mechanisms, memory, tools, observability, and decision and improvement cycles. None of these elements, individually, needs to reproduce a structure equivalent to that found in the brain. What matters is the ability of the whole to keep information flowing, transform observations into decisions, decisions into actions, and the results of those actions into new information.

But there is a difficulty that distributed architecture does not automatically solve. Communication is not coordination. A system can perfectly transport all its signals and still make poor decisions. Components may pursue incompatible local objectives, agents may produce conflicting recommendations, information may arrive with different levels of reliability, and strategies that work locally may harm global behavior. The more autonomy we grant components, the greater the need for mechanisms capable of resolving conflicts, establishing priorities, and determining when a decision should be accepted, reviewed, or rejected.

This may be one of the points where the analogy with distributed systems becomes most interesting. In a conventional architecture, connecting services is not enough. We need to define contracts, authority, policies, consistency, fault tolerance, and coordination mechanisms. In a cognitive architecture, these problems continue to exist, but they take on a different dimension because the components are not merely processing data. They are interpreting situations, proposing actions, and potentially making decisions with some degree of autonomy.

## Intelligence May Be in the Composition

At this point, a central question arises: if no component needs to be intelligent in the human sense, where exactly is the intelligence? Perhaps the answer lies less in any isolated component and more in the composition. A perception agent may not know how to plan. A planning agent may not know how to execute. An execution agent may not know how to evaluate. An evaluation agent may not know how to modify a strategy. Yet if these components can work together, share context, and use one another's results, the architecture can produce behaviors that none of them could produce in isolation.

The idea is not new in engineering. Complex systems often depend on properties that cannot be attributed to a single component. A computer network, for example, is not merely a collection of devices connected by cables. Its capabilities depend on the interaction between addressing, routing, protocols, transport, congestion control, and various other mechanisms. None of these elements, in isolation, represents what we understand as a functional network. The system's behavior depends on the relationships between them. In distributed systems, this distinction is even more evident: a service can have a very specific responsibility and still participate in an operation that no individual service could perform.

Applied to intelligence, this perspective reduces the importance of the idea of a “universal agent.” Perhaps a sufficiently general architecture is not one in which each component can do everything, but one capable of combining different competencies according to the problem. Faced with a mathematical task, certain resources may be activated. In programming, others. In a situation requiring interaction with the physical world, specialized sensors, actuators, and systems may enter the process. Faced with a completely new problem, the architecture itself may need to identify which capabilities it has, which are missing, and how to combine the available resources to construct a strategy.

In this scenario, generality lies in the capacity for composition. A component can be extremely specialized and still be part of a general architecture, as long as the system can determine when to use it, interpret its output, confront it with other evidence, and incorporate it into a larger strategy. Specialization ceases to necessarily be a limitation and becomes an architectural property. What matters is not only what each component knows how to do, but whether the system can discover how to use what each component knows how to do.

There is, in fact, an interesting parallel with human intelligence itself. An individual does not need to deeply master every discipline to solve complex problems. We can consult specialists, use tools, seek information, form teams, divide problems, and combine different kinds of knowledge. Part of the problem-solving capacity we attribute to an individual depends, in practice, on an external infrastructure of knowledge and collaboration. The difference is that, in humans, this composition usually occurs in a relatively informal and limited manner. An artificial architecture could turn this compositional capability into an explicit and systematic property of the system.

But this is precisely where the main counterpoint appears. Distributing capabilities does not eliminate the integration problem. In fact, it can make it more explicit. If multiple agents can participate in the same task, we need to determine which ones should be activated, in what order, with what context, and under what criteria. We need to deal with conflicting information, different levels of confidence, partial failures, and decisions that are locally correct but globally inappropriate. We also need to decide when a task is sufficiently complete and when the system should continue investigating, test another hypothesis, or ask for a second opinion.

The issue becomes even more complex when components have different objectives. An agent responsible for reducing costs may recommend a solution that harms quality. A performance-oriented component may increase operational risk. A security mechanism may block an action that another component considers necessary. One agent may prioritize speed while another prioritizes accuracy. In such an architecture, defining responsibilities is not enough. We need mechanisms to arbitrate conflicts and preserve some degree of global coherence.

This problem is particularly important because a distributed architecture can create an illusion of capability. The more specialists we add, the more tasks the system apparently becomes capable of executing. But breadth of competencies is not necessarily generality. A collection of specialized agents may simply be a collection of specialized agents. For something closer to general intelligence to exist, the architecture needs to be able to adapt its own composition to the characteristics of the problem. It needs to know when a given capability is relevant, when an answer should be questioned, when new information changes the plan, and when the original problem needs to be reformulated.

This is where composition begins to connect with the loops discussed earlier. OODA provides a way to structure the interaction between perception, decision, and action. PDCA allows the results of these interactions to become improvement. Communication infrastructure transports signals between components. Observability provides evidence about system behavior. Memory allows previous experiences and decisions to be preserved. These mechanisms do not constitute intelligence by themselves, but they create the conditions for different capabilities to operate as parts of a common process.

Even so, we do not have a recipe for building AGI. What we have is a change in the unit of analysis. Instead of asking how to build an entity that contains all the capabilities we associate with human intelligence, we can ask how to build an architecture capable of coordinating different capabilities in a robust, adaptive, and sufficiently general way.

This distinction also helps avoid a simplistic conclusion. The thesis is not that simply dividing intelligence into agents is enough. It is not. A distributed architecture can be extremely complex, inconsistent, and inefficient. It can create new failure points, increase communication costs, and make decisions harder to explain. Distribution solves an architectural problem only to introduce others. What it offers is an alternative to concentrating all capabilities in a single entity, not a way to eliminate the complexity required to integrate them.

Perhaps, therefore, the most interesting question is not where intelligence is, but which relationships need to exist for different capabilities to function as a system. Intelligence may not reside in a specific component, nor be a property that simply appears when we connect several agents. It may lie in the architecture's ability to transform specialization into composition, composition into action, action into experience, and experience into change.

If this hypothesis is correct, the fundamental unit of the architecture ceases to be the isolated agent. It becomes the cycle between agents. And this leads us to an even deeper question: for that cycle to be truly intelligent, it is not enough for the components to know how to act. The architecture needs to know what it is trying to achieve, how to measure whether it is moving toward that objective, and when it should change strategy.

## Body and Consciousness as Functions, Not Necessarily Components

This perspective also changes the way we can think about the body. The human body is not merely a physical structure that carries the brain. It provides sensors, constraints, state signals, capacity for action, and a continuous relationship with the environment. In other words, it participates in the cycle through which the organism perceives, interprets, and modifies the world. But recognizing this function does not mean that an artificial implementation needs to biologically reproduce a human body.

An artificial system could have physical sensors, operate in a virtual environment, use APIs as perception and action interfaces, query databases as external memory, use simulators as experimentation environments, activate robots as extensions of its capacity for action, and receive metrics as state signals. It could also use other agents as extensions of its cognitive capabilities. In this model, “body” no longer necessarily means an organism and instead comes to mean, in architectural terms, the set of interfaces through which the system perceives its environment, receives information about its own state, and produces effects on what surrounds it.

This allows us to think about different forms of embodiment without needing to establish a rigid boundary between physical and digital systems. An agent that operates on documents has an environment and mechanisms for interacting with it. An agent that manages financial systems has another. An industrial control system has sensors, actuators, and physical constraints. A robot has a body in the more conventional sense. The implementations are different, but all can participate in cycles of perception, action, and feedback. What changes is the way these functions are performed and the nature of the environment with which the system interacts.

It is in this sense that I consider the ideas present in the work of Miguel Nicolelis relevant to this discussion. His research into the relationship between the brain, body, and artificial systems helps challenge a view excessively centered on the brain as an isolated unit of intelligence. Perception, action, the body, and the environment participate in the system that produces behavior. But recognizing this relationship does not require us to biologically reproduce its components. Neuroscience can help us understand which functions participate in these processes. Engineering can then ask whether those functions need to be implemented in the same way in an artificial architecture.

Consciousness presents a different problem, but the distinction between function and implementation remains useful. We do not know exactly which aspects of subjective experience are necessary to produce the different behaviors we associate with intelligence. Perhaps some are relevant. Perhaps none is necessary for certain types of behavior. And perhaps the question of consciousness itself requires scientific understanding that we do not yet possess.

From an architectural perspective, however, there is a more limited and operational question: if a system can perceive information, build models, plan actions, use tools, execute tasks, evaluate results, learn from previous experiences, and adapt its behavior, do we necessarily need to assume that it also has subjective experience? We do not have enough evidence to turn that hypothesis into a design requirement.

This does not mean asserting that consciousness is irrelevant to intelligence, nor that it can easily be reproduced or replaced. It simply means separating two questions that are often treated as if they were one. One is investigating what consciousness is and what relationship it has with human intelligence. The other is determining which mechanisms are necessary to build systems capable of executing certain behaviors we associate with intelligence. The first is an open scientific and philosophical question. The second can also be investigated as an engineering problem.

This distinction is important because it prevents human characteristics from becoming artificial requirements without sufficient evidence. The fact that a property exists in us demonstrates that it participates in our form of intelligence. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that it is necessary for any system capable of exhibiting intelligent behavior.

Body and consciousness therefore reinforce a distinction that runs throughout this article. We can observe a property in human architecture, investigate what function it performs, and then ask whether that function needs to be reproduced, replaced, or perhaps not implemented at all in another system. Between the observed property and the required implementation there is considerable room for abstraction.

And perhaps that space is precisely where systems engineering can contribute to the discussion about AGI: not by deciding in advance which parts of humans need to be copied, but by testing which functions are actually necessary to produce generality, adaptation, and capacity for action.

## Reproduction or Sufficiency?

After going through this architecture, my initial question begins to change. At the beginning, I was asking how much of human intelligence we need to reproduce. Now, the question seems to be different: which properties do we need to implement for a system to be able to produce sufficiently general behavior?

This change allows us to move away from a direct comparison between machine and human. We do not need to ask whether a given system has a brain, body, consciousness, memory, or perception exactly like ours. We can ask which functions these elements perform and which other implementations could perform equivalent functions within an artificial architecture.

In this model, OODA can represent the operational cycle of perception, orientation, decision, and action. PDCA, the cycle of evaluation and improvement. Specialized agents provide different capabilities. Communication enables their composition. Observability transforms system behavior into signals that can feed new cycles. Memory preserves experiences. Tools expand the capacity for action. Environments provide problems, constraints, and feedback. None of these elements needs to reproduce its biological equivalent. What matters is the function and, above all, the interaction between them.

This does not mean that intelligence becomes simple. Generalization, learning, planning, memory, coordination, objectives, uncertainty, reliability, and computational costs remain difficult problems. A distributed architecture can shift complexity without eliminating it. The question is whether we can organize it in a way that differs from the one found in biology.

Perhaps artificial general intelligence does not need to be an entity that knows how to do everything. Perhaps it is a system capable of discovering which capabilities it needs to mobilize when facing a new problem, combining them, evaluating the results, and modifying its strategy. In that case, generality would lie less in a fixed set of competencies and more in the architecture's capacity for composition and adaptation.

We do not know whether this approach will be sufficient to produce AGI. Perhaps some characteristics of human cognition are fundamental. Perhaps we will discover that many of them are merely one of the possible ways to implement intelligence. Evolution gives us a demonstration that intelligence is possible, but not necessarily an engineering specification that we need to reproduce.

An airplane does not need to be a bird to fly. Likewise, an artificial intelligence does not need to be a brain to produce behaviors that we recognize as intelligent. If we accept this distinction, perhaps the most interesting question ceases to be how much of human intelligence we need to copy and becomes another: what is the smallest architecture capable of producing sufficiently general behavior?
