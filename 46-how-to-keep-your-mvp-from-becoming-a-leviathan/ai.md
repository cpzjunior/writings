An MVP is the smallest necessary set of capabilities for testing a relevant hypothesis with sufficient reliability and producing useful learning; it is not simply the smallest amount of software that can be deployed. Scope should therefore be proportional to what must be discovered at the current stage. A feature can be technically easy yet indispensable to the experiment, while sophisticated capabilities can be unnecessary if they address questions the product has not yet raised. Building too little can remove essential elements and make the experiment uninformative; building too much can consume resources solving hypothetical problems before the core thesis is validated.

MVP scope tends to expand through individually reasonable decisions: adding integrations, accommodating exceptions, generalizing structures, anticipating scale, supporting additional configurations, or responding to user requests. The cumulative result can become a “Leviathan”: a product whose features, dependencies, business rules, states, exceptions, and operational requirements have grown beyond their original experimental purpose. The true cost of a feature extends beyond implementation to testing, monitoring, documentation, support, maintenance, dependencies, additional execution paths, and constraints on future changes. Complexity can therefore escape the original intent without any single decision being obviously wrong.

Anticipating every possible future problem is particularly dangerous while the product thesis remains uncertain. Concerns about scalability, internationalization, integrations, user profiles, permissions, data volumes, business models, and future markets may all be legitimate, but incorporating all of them into the first version amounts to paying today for futures that may never exist. Architecture should address real and material risks rather than every imaginable possibility. Investment in a concern should be proportional to its importance and likelihood at the current stage. Otherwise, the solution begins evolving from hypothetical futures rather than from evidence about the actual problem.

Complexity is not inherently undesirable. Security, auditing, resilience, observability, access control, regulatory or operational requirements may be intrinsic to the product and cannot responsibly be removed merely because the product is an MVP. An MVP involving money, sensitive data, or critical processes still needs the essential properties required by its domain. User-facing simplicity can coexist with substantial internal technical complexity. The relevant objective is therefore not minimizing complexity absolutely, but determining which complexity is necessary at the current stage given both the hypothesis being tested and the risks that cannot be accepted. Product and engineering concerns can legitimately diverge: product may seek reduced scope for learning, while engineering may need to address technical risks or irreversible decisions. Architecture must balance these concerns according to the product’s stage rather than treating simplicity or sophistication as universal principles.

Cutting scope in reaction to excessive complexity can also damage the experiment. Reducing what is built is not equivalent to increasing the quality of learning. Some hypotheses depend on specific elements of the user experience to be tested representatively. Removing those elements can make the MVP smaller while causing it to test a different proposition from the intended one. A failed experiment may then indicate only that the overly simplified implementation failed, not that the underlying solution lacks value. The objective is consequently not minimal construction but construction of only what is necessary for the outcome to provide meaningful information. In some cases, the best MVP is not software at all: a prototype, manual process, or limited pilot may test the hypothesis with less investment and complexity.

An MVP must also be diagnosable. Its result should provide enough information to guide the next decision. Failure can originate from the problem hypothesis, solution, user experience, pricing, acquisition channel, technology, or operational model, and these factors can interact. Changing too many things simultaneously makes results harder to interpret. Perfect experimental control is unrealistic in real products, but the MVP should preserve enough interpretability to distinguish meaningful learning from noise created by the experiment itself. A useful MVP can therefore support decisions to modify the solution, reformulate the hypothesis, or abandon a direction.

“Minimum” is dynamic. As evidence changes the thesis, the questions being asked also change. An initial solution may only need to establish whether a problem exists; later versions may need to test solution value, adoption, retention, scale, integrations, or operational viability. A more sophisticated thesis may legitimately require a more sophisticated solution. This is not necessarily MVP failure or bloat; it can be the consequence of learning enough to ask a better question. The critical distinction is direction: complexity should follow validated knowledge and evolving questions, not anticipate them. Growth is justified when acquired knowledge creates a new requirement; growth is problematic when it is built in advance and justified only by hypothetical futures.

The clearest sign of a Leviathan is not feature count, codebase size, or architectural component count, but loss of a clear relationship between complexity and the purpose of the first version. Warning signs include components whose purpose cannot be connected to a question being investigated, decisions driven by scenarios that do not yet exist, exceptions shaping the primary behavior, and increasing effort devoted to managing the system’s own complexity. The corrective question is: what are we trying to discover? This question must work in both directions: it can justify removing unnecessary complexity, but it can also justify retaining capabilities essential to valid experimentation.

A Leviathan is therefore not merely a large MVP; it is an MVP that has lost control over why its complexity exists. A first version should be a deliberately limited response to the questions and risks that exist now, rather than a reduced implementation of everything imagined for the future. As knowledge increases, the thesis, questions, and solution may legitimately evolve. The danger arises when construction precedes understanding and the future is expected to retroactively justify what has already been built, potentially creating a path whose accumulated complexity makes changing direction difficult.
