An MVP is not simply a product with few features; it is the smallest necessary set of capabilities capable of testing a relevant hypothesis with enough validity to produce useful learning. The central problem is balancing excess and insufficiency: building too much introduces unnecessary complexity and prematurely solves hypothetical future problems, while building too little can remove elements essential to testing the hypothesis. The appropriate size of an MVP is therefore relative to what the team needs to discover at a given stage, rather than to code volume or development effort.

The growth of an MVP into a “Leviathan” usually occurs incrementally through individually reasonable decisions: adding integrations, exceptions, generic abstractions, configurations, scalability provisions, or requested features. Each addition also creates secondary costs in testing, monitoring, documentation, maintenance, dependencies, business rules, and constraints on future changes. As these decisions accumulate, the product can drift away from its original experimental purpose without any single decision appearing obviously wrong. The resulting complexity is therefore often the product of many locally rational choices whose combined effect exceeds what the original hypothesis required.

Trying to solve every possible future problem is another major source of unnecessary complexity. Teams may anticipate multiple user types, business models, data volumes, integrations, international markets, permission structures, and scaling scenarios because they know that some architectural decisions are expensive to change later. While these concerns can be legitimate, allowing all of them to shape the initial product means investing in hypothetical futures before establishing whether those futures will exist. Architecture should address real and relevant risks rather than every imaginable possibility, with investment proportional to the importance and likelihood of the problem being addressed.

Complexity is not inherently undesirable, because some products require substantial technical properties even at the MVP stage. Security, auditing, resilience, observability, access control, and operational safeguards may be intrinsic to the solution, particularly when handling money, sensitive information, or critical processes. Simplifying such systems by removing necessary properties can make the experiment invalid or irresponsible. The relevant distinction is therefore not between simple and complex systems, but between complexity that is necessary at the current stage and complexity without a concrete justification. Product and engineering concerns can both be legitimate, and architecture must balance hypothesis validation against technical and domain-specific risks.

Reducing scope can itself become a mistake when simplification removes elements necessary to test the hypothesis meaningfully. A smaller product does not automatically produce a better experiment: removing a crucial part of the value proposition can lead to conclusions about a version that no longer represents the intended solution. The objective is therefore not to minimize what is built, but to preserve the minimum needed for the result to provide useful information for the next decision. Depending on the hypothesis, that minimum may not even require software; prototypes, manual processes, or limited pilots can sometimes test the relevant question with less investment and complexity.

An MVP also needs to be diagnosable, meaning that its results should retain enough interpretability to guide the next decision. Outcomes can be affected by the problem hypothesis, solution quality, user experience, pricing, acquisition, technical limitations, or operational constraints, and changing too many variables simultaneously makes it harder to determine what the observed result actually means. Perfect experimental control is unrealistic in real products, but the MVP should avoid unnecessary complexity that makes its results opaque. Its purpose is not merely to run, but to provide evidence that can support correction, reformulation of the hypothesis, or abandonment of an unpromising direction.

The meaning of “minimum” changes as the product learns. An initial MVP may establish whether a problem exists, while later versions may need to test solution value, adoption, retention, scale, integrations, or operational viability. A more sophisticated product at a later stage does not necessarily indicate that the original MVP became bloated; it may reflect the fact that new evidence produced more sophisticated questions requiring additional capabilities. The important direction is that complexity should follow the evolution of the thesis rather than anticipate it. Growth is justified when it results from knowledge acquired, not when it is constructed in advance for hypothetical future requirements.

The clearest sign that an MVP is becoming a Leviathan is not its number of features or architectural components, but the loss of a clear relationship between its complexity and its current purpose. When teams can no longer explain which questions a component helps answer or which concrete risk justified it, and when hypothetical scenarios and exceptions increasingly shape the core product, complexity has begun to dominate the experiment. The appropriate response is to return to the question of what the team is trying to discover while preserving elements genuinely necessary for a valid test. A healthy MVP is deliberately limited to the knowledge, questions, and risks relevant at the current stage; as those change, the product may legitimately grow, but when growth precedes the knowledge that would justify it, the MVP risks becoming a system that creates its own path before the team has learned whether that path is the right one.
