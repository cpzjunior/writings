# Artificial Intelligence Is Not Killing Creativity

_We are confusing the ability to produce with the ability to create._

**Summary**: I don't believe artificial intelligence is killing creativity. I think we are confusing the ability to produce with the ability to create. AI lowers the technical barriers between imagining and bringing ideas to life, expands our possibilities, and can even lead us to ideas we would not have reached on our own. This does not mean it replaces experience, sensitivity, or critical thinking, but rather that it transforms the way we create. To me, its greatest value lies in helping us explore ideas, question concepts, and go beyond our limitations without giving up human intention and judgment. After all, producing more has never been the same as creating better.

---

I have been following discussions about generative artificial intelligence, and I am struck by the intensity of the reactions it provokes, especially in circles related to artistic and creative production. With every new image, video, or text generated by AI, the same debate resurfaces: are we witnessing a revolution in creative possibilities, or the beginning of a process that will replace human creativity?

I understand some of this discomfort. The proliferation of generic, repetitive content with no aesthetic consideration whatsoever—the already familiar AI slop—is exhausting. Concerns among professionals who see this technology as a threat to their work, the value of their skills, and the economic sustainability of their professions are also legitimate. These are not issues that should be ignored or dismissed as a natural resistance to change.

Even so, my perspective is different. I don't believe artificial intelligence is threatening human creativity. I think we are confusing two things that, although they often go hand in hand, are not necessarily the same: the ability to produce and the ability to create.

Creativity is related to the ability to imagine, establish intentions, develop concepts, assign meaning, and find connections between experiences and knowledge. Production, in turn, involves transforming these ideas into something tangible. This distinction does not mean execution is a mechanical activity or one devoid of creativity. Often, we discover what we want to create precisely as we try to bring an idea to life. Experimentation, technique, and even the limitations of the tools being used can profoundly influence the outcome.

What I am questioning is the need to concentrate all these skills in the same person. For a long time, the possibility of turning an idea into reality was directly dependent on the technical skills of the person conceiving it or on their ability to bring together people who could execute it. Artificial intelligence is beginning to change this relationship, significantly reducing the distance between imagining and doing.

And perhaps that is precisely where its most interesting contribution lies.

## Creating Does Not Necessarily Mean Executing

Consider a film director. They don't need to operate the camera, build the sets, or personally edit every scene to be responsible for a film's artistic vision. An architect doesn't need to lay the bricks of a building to design it. An art director doesn't need to individually produce every visual element of a campaign. In all these cases, there is a distinction between conceiving, directing, and bringing something to life.

This does not diminish the importance of those who execute. On the contrary, it recognizes that creation can involve different skills and forms of contribution. A good idea may depend on technically exceptional execution to reach its full potential, just as technically flawless execution may have no meaningful significance when there is no intention guiding it.

Artificial intelligence expands this possibility of separation. Someone with a clear vision of a fictional universe but no drawing skills can explore its visual identity. A person who imagines a game can experiment with settings, characters, and mechanics without mastering all the disciplines involved in developing that kind of product. An entrepreneur can turn a business idea into prototypes and interactive experiences without having to individually develop every technical component.

From my experience with solution architecture and product development in startup environments, I see an interesting parallel. A solution does not emerge from the technology chosen, but from understanding a problem, defining objectives, and building a value proposition. Technology is a means of bringing that vision to life. Knowing how to use a particular tool is not the same as knowing what to do with it.

In practice, this means technical knowledge continues to have value, but is no longer an absolute barrier between imagining and doing. Artificial intelligence allows people to explore possibilities that previously depended on specialized knowledge, multidisciplinary teams, or resources that simply weren't available.

This does not make execution irrelevant. It simply expands who can participate in it and how.

## So, Who Is the Author?

This transformation leads us to an inevitable question: what does it mean to be the author of a work when a significant part of its realization can be carried out by artificial intelligence?

I am not referring here to legal ownership or intellectual property rules, which involve specific and equally important issues. My reflection concerns authorship in the creative and expressive sense.

I believe authorship is much more closely related to intellectual contribution and the decisions that guide a creation than to the physical execution of each of its stages. Having an idea is a starting point, but it is not enough. One must actively participate in its development, establish what one intends to communicate, evaluate possibilities, make choices, and take responsibility for the result.

Imagine someone who conceives a game about a civilization that believes it lives inside a simulation. This person establishes the premises of that universe, defines its conflicts, develops its characters, and determines which questions they intend to explore. They use AI to experiment with different settings, develop visual elements, test mechanics, and expand the narrative. Throughout the process, they question suggestions, discard alternatives, modify concepts, and incorporate discoveries that emerge from interacting with the tool.

There is a human creative contribution here that goes far beyond simply requesting something to be produced. The original idea guides the project, but it can also be transformed by the possibilities that emerge during its development.

Compare this with someone who asks AI for an interesting image, chooses one of the options presented, and simply publishes it. I don't consider these equivalent situations. In one, there is a process of conception, exploration, and decision-making. In the other, human participation may be limited to selecting an output generated from a generic prompt.

Naturally, there is no objective formula capable of determining how much human participation is sufficient to qualify something as a creation. But that doesn't mean all forms of AI use are equivalent. There is a difference between using a tool to develop an idea and simply consuming what it produces.

It is also important to recognize that the discussion about authorship does not end with the contribution of the person using the tool. The origin of the data used to train models, creators' consent, compensation, and respect for intellectual property are legitimate issues. Defending the creative potential of artificial intelligence does not mean indiscriminately agreeing with every practice used to develop and commercialize it. It is possible to recognize the value of a technology while simultaneously questioning the economic and legal models that underpin its development.

To me, these discussions need to coexist. One does not invalidate the other.

## AI Slop Is Human Slop with a Turbocharger

One of the most frequent criticisms of generative AI concerns the proliferation of low-quality content. Pointless images, absurd videos, generic texts, and productions that seem to exist solely to take up space on social media. It is understandable that this causes rejection, especially when such content starts competing for attention with work that required time, study, and dedication.

But there is something about this discussion that bothers me: we are treating superficiality as if it were an invention of artificial intelligence.

Mediocre content did not emerge with generative models. We have always had books written exclusively to capitalize on commercial trends, films produced by following repetitive formulas, songs composed to meet market demands, and articles published solely to generate traffic. Human production is also perfectly capable of generating disposable, repetitive, and meaningless content.

The difference is that we now have a technology capable of reproducing this behavior on a much larger scale and at a significantly lower cost.

AI did not invent superficial production. It changed its economics. When producing content becomes extremely cheap, mediocrity ceases to be merely a characteristic of certain productions and can become a scalable business model. This affects platform dynamics, competition for attention, and even the economic sustainability of those who produce original content.

I like to think that AI slop is, to a large extent, just human slop with a turbocharger. The problem is not necessarily the tool, but the absence of intention, experience, and concern for what is being produced.

This does not mean that all AI-generated content is bad or that all human production is good. Artistic quality and technological origin are different dimensions. An AI-generated image can be extraordinary, just as a hand-drawn illustration can be completely irrelevant. The same applies to texts, music, videos, and any other form of artistic expression.

What determines the value of a creation is not simply the tool used, but what it intends to communicate and the quality of the decisions that guided its development.

## Democratizing Production Does Not Mean Democratizing Talent

If artificial intelligence lowers the technical barriers required to bring ideas to life, it is only natural that more people will start producing content. And I consider that a positive development.

For a long time, countless ideas never came into existence simply because their creators lacked the resources or skills needed to execute them. Someone might imagine an extraordinary visual universe without knowing how to draw, conceive a story without mastering literary techniques, or envision a game without having any programming knowledge.

AI reduces this distance between imagining and doing. More than that, it makes experimentation possible. Someone without musical training can explore compositional structures. Someone with no design experience can test different visual languages. A professional without programming knowledge can turn a hypothesis into a functional prototype.

This possibility is particularly interesting because experimentation is also part of discovery. Often, we don't know exactly what we want to build until we have the opportunity to visualize, test, and interact with an initial version of the idea.

But there is an important difference between democratizing access to tools and automatically democratizing the ability to create. A person can generate a technically impressive image without understanding composition, just as they can produce grammatically flawless text without mastering the subject they are writing about.

Technology lowers execution barriers, but it does not replace experience, critical thinking, sensitivity, or knowledge. In fact, I believe that the easier it becomes to produce something, the more important it becomes to know how to evaluate what has been produced.

This is where I see a potential transformation in the role of creative professionals. Certain technical skills may lose some of their economic value when they become easier to perform using generative tools. Others, related to conception, direction, evaluation, and refinement, may become more important.

This does not mean the transition will be simple. Professions may change, demand for certain services may decline, and compensation models may become obsolete. The survival of human creativity and the economic sustainability of creative professions are two different issues. The former does not automatically guarantee the latter.

For this reason, I don't think it is enough to address these professionals' concerns by saying that technology is just another tool. It can profoundly change working conditions and the distribution of value within certain activities. These consequences need to be discussed.

Even so, I see no point in defending the rejection of technology as a response to this transformation. The challenge is to find new ways to combine human skills and generative tools, preserving the value of specialized knowledge while expanding creative possibilities.

## AI as a Partner in Exploration

This brings us to an idea I consider particularly important for the future of creation: the concept of Human in the Loop, or human participation throughout the process.

In my view, artificial intelligence should function as a creative assistant, not as a replacement for human judgment. This does not mean it should be limited to following instructions. On the contrary, its greatest value may lie precisely in its ability to question ideas, present alternatives, identify inconsistencies, and suggest unexpected directions.

My golden rule when using AI in creative processes is simple: it should expand my thinking capabilities, not take responsibility for my decisions.

I like to think of this process as a cycle of exploration. I start with an idea, present the concept to AI, and use its responses to identify possibilities, inconsistencies, and opportunities for development. I question its suggestions, discard what doesn't make sense, incorporate what I consider relevant, and return with new questions. With each interaction, the original idea can gain complexity, change direction, or even reveal possibilities I had not imagined.

Imagine developing a game again. Starting from an initial premise, I can ask AI to explore different political structures for that universe, suggest conflicts between characters, propose mechanics that express certain philosophical dilemmas, or identify contradictions in the narrative.

I don't have to accept its suggestions. In fact, questioning them is part of the process.

The most interesting thing is that an unexpected response can lead me to an idea that did not exist in my original conception. AI may present a combination of concepts that makes me see an entirely new possibility. At that moment, it is not simply executing a preexisting idea. It is participating in the exploration that may transform it.

And I see no problem with that. Human creativity does not need to happen in isolation to remain human. Talking to someone, consulting references, studying other works, and experimenting with tools have always been ways of expanding our experience. Artificial intelligence introduces a new form of interaction into this process.

The difference lies in preserving critical participation. If we simply accept AI's first responses, we will stop using it as an instrument of exploration and start outsourcing our own thinking. Human in the Loop cannot simply mean human approval at the end of an automated production process. It needs to be present throughout development, in the decisions that define what will be built and in the judgment applied to the result.

AI can influence the direction of a creation, question its assumptions, and even trigger changes that were not originally anticipated. What should not be outsourced is responsibility for the choices, meanings, and results we decide to present to the world.

AI should help us think better, not think in our place.

## The Future of Creativity Is Human, but Not Necessarily Manual

There is another argument frequently used against generative AI that deserves some consideration: the idea that art needs to preserve human imperfections.

I agree that imperfection can have artistic value. A technically imperfect photograph can convey more emotion than a carefully produced image. A fragmented text can communicate a particular experience better than a perfectly structured narrative. A song with minor imperfections can convey an authenticity that an excessively polished production cannot reproduce.

But I don't see how this could be used as an argument for rejecting artificial intelligence.

The question is not choosing between perfection and imperfection, but understanding that both can have value depending on the artistic intention. Someone can use AI to produce something extremely refined or deliberately explore an imperfect aesthetic. The tool does not need to determine the artistic language, just as it should not determine what is or is not legitimate to create.

Perhaps we are placing too much importance on the effort required to produce something and too little importance on the meaning of what has been produced. Manual work has value, as do technical mastery and accumulated experience. But the amount of effort involved in execution is not, in itself, a measure of artistic quality or relevance.

A work does not become more interesting simply because it was harder to produce. Likewise, a creation does not automatically lose its value because it used tools that made its execution easier.

Ultimately, I believe we are witnessing a transformation in the very relationship between conception and execution. For a long time, the ability to create depended not only on imagination, but also on the possibility of bringing what we imagined to life. Artificial intelligence reduces this distance and allows more people to explore ideas that would previously have been unfeasible.

This does not mean everything that will be produced will have quality, originality, or meaning. We will probably see even more disposable content, more reproductions of trends, and more productions created exclusively to compete for our attention. But we will also see new forms of expression, experiments that would not have been possible before, and projects that would hardly have made it off the drawing board without this kind of technology.

I don't think we need to choose between human creativity and artificial intelligence. Nor do I believe we should measure the value of a creation by the amount of manual labor involved in its execution.

What really interests me is what we can do when we stop spending so much effort trying to overcome our technical limitations and start using that time to explore ideas, question concepts, and build things that once seemed impossible.

Artificial intelligence does not need to have the intention of creating something meaningful in order to help us create. It needs to be capable of expanding our possibilities, presenting different perspectives, and helping us turn ideas into experiences.

Perhaps the real risk is not AI learning to produce what only we could produce before. Perhaps it is becoming so accustomed to accepting what it produces that we stop caring about having ideas of our own.

Because, at the end of the day, producing more has never been the same as creating better.
