The article argues that artificial intelligence is not killing human creativity but transforming the relationship between conception and execution. It distinguishes the ability to produce from the ability to create: creativity involves imagination, intention, conceptual development, meaning, and critical judgment, while production translates ideas into tangible outputs. Although execution can itself be creative, AI reduces the technical barriers between imagining and realizing ideas, allowing more people to explore possibilities that previously required specialized skills, resources, or multidisciplinary teams.

Creation does not necessarily require personally executing every stage of a project. As demonstrated by film directors, architects, and art directors, authorship can reside in conceiving, directing, and making decisions about a work rather than performing every technical task. AI extends this model by enabling individuals to develop visual concepts, game prototypes, narratives, and interactive experiences without mastering every discipline involved. Technical expertise remains valuable, but access to execution is no longer exclusively dependent on it. The fundamental distinction is between knowing how to use a tool and knowing what to accomplish with it.

Authorship in AI-assisted creation should be understood primarily through intellectual contribution, creative intention, and decision-making rather than physical execution. Generating an output from a generic prompt and selecting a result involves a different level of creative participation from developing a concept, exploring alternatives, evaluating suggestions, refining ideas, and taking responsibility for the final work. AI can influence and transform the original vision through unexpected discoveries, but meaningful human involvement remains essential. This perspective does not eliminate legitimate concerns about intellectual property, training data, creators' consent, and compensation. The creative potential of AI and the ethical and economic practices surrounding its development must be evaluated independently.

The proliferation of low-quality AI-generated content, commonly called AI slop, reflects an existing problem amplified by technology rather than a phenomenon invented by it. Human industries have long produced repetitive, superficial, commercially driven, and disposable content. Generative AI changes the economics of this production by making it significantly cheaper and more scalable, intensifying competition for attention and threatening the sustainability of original content creation. However, technological origin alone does not determine artistic quality. AI-generated work can be meaningful and original, just as human-produced work can be mediocre. Quality depends on intention, relevance, and the decisions guiding the creative process.

AI democratizes access to production tools, but it does not automatically democratize talent, expertise, or creative judgment. Individuals without formal training can now experiment with music, design, writing, programming, and other disciplines, potentially bringing previously unfeasible ideas to life. Experimentation also facilitates discovery, since creators often identify what they want to build through interaction with early versions of their ideas. Nevertheless, producing technically impressive outputs does not necessarily imply understanding composition, mastering a subject, or developing an original concept. As execution becomes easier, the ability to evaluate, refine, and direct outputs becomes increasingly important. This shift may increase the value of conceptual thinking and creative direction while reducing the economic value of certain technical skills. It also creates legitimate concerns about employment, professional compensation, and the redistribution of value across creative industries. The preservation of human creativity does not automatically ensure the economic sustainability of creative professions.

The most productive approach to AI-assisted creation is Human in the Loop, with human judgment actively involved throughout development rather than limited to approving the final output. AI should operate as a creative partner that challenges assumptions, identifies inconsistencies, proposes alternatives, and introduces unexpected perspectives. An iterative process of presenting ideas, evaluating suggestions, rejecting unsuitable outputs, and refining concepts can expand creative thinking and lead to possibilities that would not have emerged independently. Human creativity has never depended on isolation; collaboration, research, references, and experimentation have always contributed to it. AI introduces another mechanism for expanding these capabilities. Its value lies in improving exploration and supporting better decisions, not replacing the responsibility for determining what should be created and what meaning the final work should convey.

The argument that art must preserve human imperfections also overlooks the fact that both perfection and imperfection can serve legitimate artistic intentions. Technical refinement does not guarantee emotional impact, and deliberate imperfections can communicate authenticity or particular experiences. Similarly, using AI does not prevent someone from pursuing an imperfect aesthetic. Manual effort, technical mastery, and accumulated experience have intrinsic value, but the difficulty of execution is not an objective measure of artistic quality or relevance. A work does not become inherently better because it required more effort, nor does it lose value simply because technology made its production easier.

Ultimately, AI represents a transformation in the relationship between imagination and execution. It will likely increase the volume of repetitive and disposable content while also enabling new forms of expression, experimentation, and projects that previously could not be realized. The relevant distinction is not between human creativity and artificial intelligence, but between intentional creation and passive production. AI does not need independent artistic intentions to contribute meaningfully to human creativity. It needs to expand possibilities, introduce perspectives, and help translate ideas into experiences while leaving critical judgment and responsibility with people. The greatest risk is not that AI will acquire the ability to produce things humans once created, but that humans will become so accustomed to accepting its outputs that they stop developing their own ideas. Producing more has never been equivalent to creating better.
