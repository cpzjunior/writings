The project presents Universum Arcanum as an original dark-fantasy universe influenced broadly by works such as Harry Potter, The Lord of the Rings, The Saxon Stories, D&D, Star Wars, Game of Thrones, and The Witcher, while aiming to establish its own history, institutions, conflicts, privileges, and contradictions. The central story follows Alden, a non-mage-born outsider whose late magical awakening allows him to enter the elite world that previously excluded him, where he becomes increasingly involved with its institutions and eventually discovers anti-magic, a power capable of severing the magical traces through which actions are normally investigated. His subsequent career as an investigator brings him into contact with corruption and institutional failures, while anti-magic gives him unprecedented freedom to act outside accountability. The project is explicitly framed as fantasy rather than an allegory for specific real-world institutions or events, although it explores universal themes of power, corruption, privilege, justice, and accountability through morally ambiguous characters and institutions. The author is using the condensed literary demo to determine whether Alden's journey and the broader universe generate enough interest to justify developing the concept as a novel, game, or another format, with particular interest in whether audiences would want to follow Alden's transformation and learn more about the world.

Alden grows up in rural Veridion among the non-mage majority, whose nominal citizenship does not provide the same rights, opportunities, or social standing enjoyed by mages. His family works for a magical household, leaving them dependent on people who possess authority by virtue of birth, and Alden gradually develops distrust not of every individual mage but of the structures that allow magical elites to command without meaningful accountability. His rural upbringing nevertheless gives him valuable practical abilities: hunting, tracking, archery, spear fighting, environmental awareness, patience, resource management, and the discipline to observe before acting. He also learns to recognize the subtle forms of social contempt directed toward non-mages and understands that even benevolent individual mages do not necessarily alter the unequal structure surrounding them. His worldview therefore becomes centered less on hatred of individuals than on suspicion of institutions that distribute power unequally and allow those who possess it to escape consequences. When his magic eventually awakens years later than normal, it gives him an opportunity to escape his family's social position without erasing the experiences that shaped his distrust of the magical elite.

Alden's late awakening offers him access to a world previously closed to him, but entering the magical elite does not automatically erase his outsider status or give him the advantages enjoyed by established magical families. Because he missed the childhood training normally required to master magic and cannot afford conventional education, he initially becomes an adventurer, using his existing skills in hunting, combat, exploration, and survival to navigate dangerous roads and dungeons. His practical competence eventually attracts the attention of magical institutions, which see value in his field experience, and he gains entry to the Academia Arcana Militaris Imperialis, an old military academy whose imperial name survives from the long-disappeared Aurelian Empire. The academy trains mages for defense, expeditions, and dangerous field operations, while prestigious positions remain disproportionately accessible to established magical families. Alden's experience allows him to compensate partly for his late start in magic, but his lack of lineage and connections leaves him socially inferior to many classmates, who call him “the Ascendant” publicly and “social climber” privately. He initially seeks recognition rather than revenge, wanting to prove that someone from his background can compete with the magical elite on its own terms, but the academy increasingly exposes him to the world he has spent his life distrusting.

During a break from the academy, Alden questions whether institutional life is actually the path he wants and returns to adventuring, choosing to explore a notoriously difficult dungeon where magic progressively loses effectiveness. His experience with non-magical survival allows him to advance farther than conventional spellcasters, eventually reaching a creature from another reality whose presence drains magical energy and whose existence appears as out of place in Veridion as Alden himself once felt. The creature explains that it crossed between realities and requires a particular energy produced through the combination of magical nature and intense emotional states; mages possess the necessary magical nature but insufficient emotional energy, non-mages possess the emotions but lack the compatible magical nature, while Alden possesses both because of his unusual history of powerlessness, resentment, and later magical awakening. In exchange for helping the creature survive, Alden can receive anti-magic, an ancient ability he knows only from obscure records. The creature explains that anti-magic does not merely increase destructive power but interrupts the connection between a magical action and the magical traces normally left behind, making it possible for effects to occur without conventional magical investigation identifying their source. Alden recognizes the potential significance and the dangers of the pact, does not know whether his encounter has any larger purpose or is merely coincidence, and accepts because both parties need something from the other despite having no trust or friendship.

After leaving the dungeon, Alden researches anti-magic in the academy's library, discovering that modern magical scholarship largely omits it or treats it as legend, while older records reveal that anti-mages historically existed and were systematically hunted and exterminated. The reason becomes clear: anti-magic does not simply provide unusual offensive power but removes the magical signatures that allow investigators to reconstruct actions and connect effects to their perpetrators. A fire, theft, or murder may remain physically evident, but the magical causal trail disappears, undermining one of the principal mechanisms through which magical society establishes responsibility. Alden realizes that his new ability could make him a threat regardless of whether he commits crimes, because institutions cannot easily distinguish an honest anti-mage from someone capable of acting without leaving magical evidence, and the historical records suggest discovery alone could lead to execution. He therefore decides that merely learning to use anti-magic is insufficient; he must understand how investigators detect inconsistencies, reconstruct events without signatures, evaluate evidence, identify manipulated scenes, and determine suspects. His decision to become an investigator initially arises as self-preservation rather than vocation: he wants to understand how someone like him would be hunted so he can avoid detection. However, learning how investigations work also means learning where they can fail, which evidence can be challenged, how hypotheses can be redirected, and how investigators can be led toward false conclusions, giving him the conceptual foundation for eventually manipulating the very system he seeks to understand.

Alden persuades his tutors to allow him to transfer from military training toward investigative work by arguing that his adventuring and field experience make him useful for investigations involving conflicts, war crimes, and clandestine organizations; the institutions also recognize that his ability to gather information about Veridion's enemies could have political value, including leverage and blackmail. He receives formal training in crime-scene analysis, magical evidence, interrogation, reconstruction of events, legal procedures, institutional hierarchies, and investigative authority, turning his original survival strategy into a professional competence. His first major case involves a necromancer sect, which his superiors initially do not want him to lead because of his inexperience, but Alden insists and successfully dismantles the organization, using anti-magic to move through protected environments without leaving conventional magical signatures while relying on his practical skills, patience, strategic thinking, and field experience. The success earns him a commendation and establishes a reputation for unusual investigative ability, after which increasingly difficult cases bring him closer to the center of Veridion's magical institutions. Eventually he becomes an observer on the Arcane Council, gaining access to discussions and decisions that shape magical society without yet possessing a councilor's formal authority. For Alden, the position represents more than prestige: it gives him access to the internal workings of the power structure he has spent his life questioning, and no one there knows that his proximity to power is also an opportunity to understand and potentially challenge it from within.

As an observer on the Arcane Council, Alden sees that the institution is more complicated than its public image as Veridion's guardian suggests. The Council consists of powerful and experienced mages responsible for stability and defense, many of whom sincerely believe in their responsibilities, but Alden discovers through informal conversations and institutional rumors that Tiberian Valerian, a respected archmage, is repeatedly connected to smuggling, extortion, and criminal factions. Investigators believe there is enough evidence to justify action, yet the case cannot advance because Tiberian has the protection of the Council president, Aurelius Aurelian Septimus, who controls whether a formal removal request can reach the Council's agenda. The rule requiring the president to place such a matter on the agenda has a legitimate institutional purpose because it prevents personal rivalries or factions from using the Council to arbitrarily remove powerful members, but Aurelius's control over the procedure turns that safeguard into protection for a potentially corrupt colleague. Alden is particularly affected because Aurelius had mistreated his family during his childhood and therefore represents both personal resentment and the structural privilege Alden has always distrusted. At the same time, Alden recognizes that not every Council member is corrupt or malicious; some genuinely believe institutional stability requires preserving the rules, even when others exploit those rules. He consequently begins to understand that an institution does not need to be entirely corrupt to produce unjust outcomes: a small number of actors can exploit procedural weaknesses while maintaining the institution's outward legitimacy, and power can protect itself not by openly breaking rules but by controlling who is able to enforce them.

Alden decides to exploit his anti-magic to pressure Aurelius after concluding that institutional mechanisms will not remove Tiberian while the Council president protects him. He begins secretly attacking Aurelius's properties, using anti-magic to bypass magical defenses and prevent investigators from identifying him, while infusing his fires with mana to make them unusually destructive. He initially establishes a strict rule that no one will die, choosing empty properties and treating the attacks as a way to damage Aurelius financially without harming his family. When Aurelius refuses to yield, Alden escalates the attacks until he considers targeting the president's family itself, despite recognizing that the family members did not cause the corruption and may not have participated in it. He rationalizes their targeting by arguing that they benefit from Aurelius's wealth and privileges, but recognizes internally that this is largely a justification for his resentment. The attack produces fear rather than physical casualties, and the resulting remorse briefly forces Alden to confront the difference between punishing corruption and terrorizing people associated with a corrupt man. Nevertheless, Aurelius eventually places Tiberian's removal on the Council agenda, apparently giving Alden the result he wanted. The situation becomes more complicated when Aurelius later approaches Alden cordially, recognizing him as a promising investigator without knowing that Alden has been attacking him. Alden is forced to reconcile his childhood image of Aurelius as an almost monstrous symbol of oppression with the ordinary, possibly genuinely concerned man standing before him. Aurelius then reveals that he suspects an anti-mage is operating in Veridion and asks Alden to investigate, placing Alden in the paradoxical position of investigating the existence of his own secret while potentially using that assignment to determine how close the authorities are to discovering him.

Alden discovers that Aurelius has not identified him and instead uses the anti-mage investigation as an opportunity to study how authorities might detect someone like himself while also investigating powerful people who would otherwise be difficult to scrutinize. At night, he secretly enters heavily protected residences using anti-magic and searches for documents, correspondence, financial records, and other evidence, then returns during the day to use his official investigative authority to guide legitimate investigations toward the information he has already obtained. This gives him an unusual advantage: he can gather evidence through methods unavailable to ordinary investigators and then construct apparently legitimate investigative chains that lead others toward the same conclusions. He discovers extensive corruption among powerful mages, including smuggling, extortion, clandestine agreements, criminal networks, and influential families protecting illicit operations; some cases are already suspected but lack evidence, while others are unknown to authorities. As Alden repeatedly solves cases, his colleagues interpret his success as extraordinary investigative intuition because they do not know that he has already entered relevant locations and gathered information before official investigations begin. His official reputation therefore reinforces the effectiveness of his secret activities: professional trust gives him access, anti-magic gives him information, the information produces successful cases, and successful cases give him greater authority and freedom. For a time, Alden uses this system to bring genuinely corrupt archmages to justice, convincing himself that he is finally using the institutions against those who exploit them, but the distinction between legitimate investigation and clandestine intervention becomes increasingly difficult to maintain.

Alden initially directs his secret actions against people who are demonstrably involved in serious crimes and who have escaped accountability through influence, disappearing evidence, intimidated witnesses, political interference, or manipulation of investigators. When the formal system repeatedly fails, he begins killing those he believes are beyond institutional reach, constructing justifications around their crimes and the people they have harmed. His role as investigator and Anti-Mage gradually merges: investigation no longer merely establishes guilt but becomes part of the process by which Alden decides who deserves punishment. The turning point occurs when an investigator close to him notices irregularities in Alden's cases, including his improbable ability to identify the right leads and his apparent knowledge of information he should not possess. Alden considers nonviolent ways to neutralize the threat, including discrediting, transferring, misleading, or temporarily withdrawing, but concludes that the man's knowledge could expose him and ultimately kills him. Unlike his previous targets, the investigator is not corrupt, criminal, or responsible for any injustice; he is dangerous only because he might discover Alden's secret. Alden attempts to justify the killing as necessary self-protection, but the justification no longer aligns with the moral framework he previously used. He has crossed from punishing people he considered guilty to eliminating someone who merely threatened his secrecy, forcing him to question whether his actions are still primarily driven by justice or whether he has begun acting because he wants the power to decide who lives and dies.

Alden's clandestine campaign produces consequences beyond the individual corrupt archmages he targets. Because archmages are also central to the defense and stability of Veridion's magical cities, their deaths create institutional and strategic vacancies, while political factions become more hostile and external magical societies perceive increased vulnerability. Alden recognizes too late that removing a corrupt individual can simultaneously weaken structures responsible for protecting thousands of people, meaning that his individual conception of justice does not necessarily account for collective security or the broader consequences of his decisions. In this increasingly unstable environment, Aurelius appoints Alden as an archmage because of his investigative reputation, military and field experience, independence from old magical families, and apparent incorruptibility; Aurelius does not know that Alden's reputation has partly been built on information obtained through illegal nocturnal investigations. Alden therefore reaches the highest level of the society he once regarded from below, becoming a colleague and authority figure among the people who historically represented the elite he distrusted. He temporarily suspends his activities as the Anti-Mage because he now has legitimate authority, resources, and influence that might allow him to reform the institutions from within, protect non-mages, reduce corruption, and constrain inherited privileges without destroying institutional stability. Yet the power of anti-magic, his experience of acting without accountability, and his history of manipulating investigations remain within him. Society sees a self-made and apparently trustworthy archmage who rose from the countryside through competence, while Alden knows that some of his achievements involved deception, illegal surveillance, manipulation, and killing, including the killing of at least one person who was not guilty of the crimes Alden originally opposed. Having finally reached the top, Alden faces the central contradiction of his journey: he now has legitimate power to reform the system he spent his life fighting, but he has also become someone accustomed to acting without answering to anyone, leaving unresolved whether he can accept institutional limits when he possesses enough power to ignore them and whether the Anti-Mage will remain necessary—or simply remain possible.
