Startup vocabulary is a practical language for describing how companies are built, operated, financed, owned, and eventually realize value. It is best learned progressively: first the basic actors, business models, and product concepts; then market validation and traction; then acquisition and retention; then unit economics and recurring revenue; then growth and go-to-market; and finally cash, fundraising, investment stages, ownership, financing instruments, employee equity, and exits. Definitions are practical rather than universal because terminology varies by context.

Basic vocabulary: a startup generally seeks a repeatable, scalable business model under uncertainty; being new or small does not by itself make a company a startup. A founder founded the company; a co-founder is one of multiple founders. A customer pays for the product, while a user uses it; they may be the same person or different entities, especially in B2B. B2B sells to businesses, while B2C sells directly to consumers, with implications for sales dynamics, decision-making, sales cycles, and distribution. SaaS provides software as a service, usually through recurring payment. A marketplace connects supply and demand so participants can transact. These terms become meaningful through their relationships rather than in isolation.

Product and market vocabulary begins with problem, solution, value proposition, and business model. The problem is the need or pain worth solving; the solution addresses it; the value proposition explains why the solution matters and why customers should choose it; the business model explains how value is created, delivered, and captured, including who pays and how revenue is generated. ICP (Ideal Customer Profile) identifies the customer profile with the strongest fit to the problem, potential value, and often efficient acquisition and servicing. TAM is the broad total addressable market; SAM is the portion serviceable under product, geographic, segment, or other constraints; SOM is the portion realistically obtainable. These concepts frame the opportunity.

An MVP (Minimum Viable Product) is the smallest product, version, or experiment capable of testing a relevant hypothesis and producing meaningful learning. It is not necessarily an incomplete production product. Validation is evidence supporting or weakening a hypothesis; stated interest is weaker evidence than actual usage, payment, retention, recurring behavior, or another relevant market behavior. PMF (Product-Market Fit) describes a condition in which the product and market fit, evidenced by consistent, sustained demand rather than a formal certification. A moat is a defensible competitive advantage that makes replication, competitive entry, or customer loss more difficult; possible mechanisms include network effects, technology, data, brand, switching costs, scale, and distribution. A pivot is a significant product or business-strategy change prompted by learning that invalidates a fundamental assumption, while retaining useful elements of previous work. Traction is evidence that the business is gaining ground through metrics such as revenue, customers, users, growth, or retention; traction is neither synonymous with revenue nor proof of PMF. A pitch is a concise structured explanation of the business, and a pitch deck organizes elements such as problem, solution, market, business model, traction, team, and capital needs.

Growth and user behavior are described through acquisition, funnel, lead, conversion rate, activation, retention, churn, and cohorts. Acquisition brings users or customers into the product or sales process through channels such as paid media, content, referrals, sales, partnerships, or organic search. A funnel represents successive stages such as awareness, lead, trial, activation, and purchase, allowing the company to identify where progression stops. A lead is a person or company showing interest and entering an acquisition or sales process; what qualifies as a lead depends on the business model. Conversion rate measures the proportion moving from one funnel stage to another. Activation is the first meaningful experience of the product's core value, defined specifically for each product. Retention measures continued use or customer continuation. Churn measures customer or revenue loss; customer churn and revenue churn can have very different economic consequences. A cohort is a group sharing a relevant characteristic, commonly the period in which users or customers entered the product, allowing retention and behavior to be tracked over time rather than hidden by aggregate averages. Increased acquisition does not establish healthy growth if activation or retention deteriorates.

Business economics are described through CAC, LTV, unit economics, payback, gross margin, MRR, and ARR. CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost) measures the cost of acquiring customers, with the exact scope depending on the company's methodology. LTV (Lifetime Value) estimates the economic value generated by a customer throughout the relationship; meaningful analysis should consider economic contribution rather than merely gross revenue. Unit economics evaluates the economics of an individual unit such as a customer, order, or transaction, considering value generated, acquisition cost, servicing cost, margin, and payback. Payback is the time required to recover CAC from the customer's economic contribution. Gross margin is revenue remaining after direct costs of delivering the product or service. MRR is Monthly Recurring Revenue; ARR is Annual Recurring Revenue. ARR is often simplified as 12 × MRR, but it is not necessarily identical to accounting revenue recognized during the period. Rapid MRR growth alone does not establish healthy economics; CAC, LTV, margin, payback, and cash generation must be considered together.

Growth strategy involves GTM, pricing, Sales-led Growth, and Product-led Growth. GTM (Go-to-Market) defines how the company reaches and operates in its market, including target audience, positioning, distribution channels, sales approach, and market presentation. Pricing concerns not only the nominal price but also pricing model, unit of charge, plans, limits, discounts, and alignment between price and perceived value. Sales-led Growth makes sales operations central to acquisition and conversion and is common where products are complex, high-ticket, or require human demonstration and negotiation. Product-led Growth (PLG) makes the product itself a major engine for acquisition, activation, conversion, retention, and expansion; PLG does not necessarily imply the absence of sales. Sales-led and product-led approaches can coexist and evolve. Companies can begin with founder-led sales, add sales teams, incorporate PLG mechanisms, or change strategy. Growth itself is not universally the objective: some businesses prioritize margin, cash generation, or slower growth rather than rapid scale or venture capital.

Cash and financing vocabulary concerns burn rate, runway, bootstrapping, and fundraising. Burn rate measures the rate at which the company consumes net cash, commonly monthly. A company can have revenue and still burn cash if operating expenses exceed cash generated. Runway estimates how long available cash can sustain operations under specified assumptions; for example, R$3 million of cash and R$500,000 monthly consumption implies six months of simplified runway. Real runway depends on expected changes in revenue, expenses, hiring, and other factors because burn is not necessarily constant. Runway affects when the company needs to reduce expenses, generate more cash, or raise capital. Bootstrapping finances the company primarily through founders' resources and operating revenue; it can reduce dependence on external capital and preserve ownership but may constrain growth speed or require earlier positive cash generation. Fundraising raises external capital and involves preparing the narrative and materials, contacting investors, answering questions, due diligence, negotiation, and closing. Capital can finance hiring, product development, customer acquisition, geographic expansion, or additional time to reach milestones, but commonly involves transferring ownership to investors. Because fundraising can take months, companies generally need to plan before runway reaches zero.

Investor vocabulary includes angel investors, venture capital, Pre-seed, Seed, and Series A. Angel investors are individuals investing their own capital, commonly at early stages, and may provide experience, contacts, market knowledge, or guidance. VC (venture capital) generally refers to specialized funds investing in companies with high growth potential; institutional fund capital is allocated according to an investment thesis, with the expectation that large successful outcomes compensate for portfolio losses. Financing-stage labels are contextual rather than rigid universal categories. Pre-seed commonly refers to very early companies, potentially before a fully developed product or strong market evidence. Seed generally indicates some validation while the product and business are being developed. Series A tends to involve more consistent evidence around product, market, and growth capacity. The round label alone does not establish maturity; context, metrics, and financing objectives matter.

Ownership vocabulary includes equity, cap table, dilution, option pool, stock options, ESOP, vesting, cliff, and exit. Equity is an ownership interest in the company. A cap table (capitalization table) maps ownership among founders, investors, employees with equity or options, and potentially other rights or future interests. New equity issuance or conversion can reduce existing holders' ownership percentages, producing dilution. Dilution does not inherently destroy economic value: a founder moving from 50% of a R$10 million company (approximately R$5 million stake) to 40% of a R$20 million company (approximately R$8 million stake) owns a smaller percentage but a larger approximate economic value. Ownership analysis therefore requires valuation, rights attached to different classes, transaction terms, and future interests rather than percentage alone. An option pool reserves ownership for future employee or other equity grants.

A stock option is a conditional right to acquire ownership in the future rather than necessarily current ownership. An ESOP (Employee Stock Option Plan) organizes employee option grants and aligns part of compensation or incentives with long-term company value. Vesting progressively earns the associated ownership right over time. A cliff is an initial period during which no ownership is earned; a common example is four-year vesting with a one-year cliff, where the first portion is earned after twelve months and the remainder subsequently under the schedule. “Partnership” can have a separate organizational meaning in investment banking, asset management, and professional services, where executives may simultaneously be owners or partners; this differs from a commercial partnership between companies. Ownership therefore evolves through issuance, dilution, vesting, and conversion.

Fundraising transaction vocabulary includes valuation, pre-money, post-money, SAFE, valuation cap, discount, and term sheet. Startup valuation is negotiated and depends on stage, growth, market, risk, traction, comparable companies, and transaction terms rather than necessarily having an objectively observable market value. Pre-money valuation is the company's valuation before a new investment; post-money valuation is the valuation after the new capital enters. Under a simplified structure, R$20 million pre-money plus R$5 million investment produces R$25 million post-money, and the R$5 million represents 20% of post-money, corresponding approximately to 20% ownership for the new investor. A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) provides capital now in exchange for a future right to equity under specified contractual events and conditions, so ownership need not be determined immediately. A valuation cap sets a valuation limit used in conversion, while a discount provides a reduction relative to the future round's price per share, according to the instrument. A term sheet summarizes proposed transaction terms such as valuation, investment, rights, and other conditions and serves as a basis for formal documentation and negotiation.

Exit refers to an event through which investors and other shareholders obtain liquidity or realize value, commonly through acquisition, merger, or IPO, although the specific mechanism depends on the company, market, and transaction structure. Understanding an exit requires tracing the cap table and associated rights over time: founders created the company; the business developed a product and sought PMF; customers, retention, CAC, LTV, and growth were measured; capital was raised; investors acquired ownership; employees may have received options subject to vesting and cliffs; new rounds changed ownership through dilution; and an exit may allow different holders to realize the value associated with their interests. Financial and ownership concepts are therefore integrated descriptions of how company value is created, financed, allocated, diluted, and eventually realized.

The central learning sequence is not memorization of isolated acronyms but understanding relationships. A startup may identify a problem, define an ICP and market, test an MVP, validate or revise hypotheses, seek PMF, acquire and retain customers, measure CAC/LTV and unit economics, choose a GTM and growth model, monitor burn and runway, decide between bootstrapping and external financing, raise capital from appropriate investors, modify the cap table through investment and employee equity, and eventually provide liquidity through an exit. A statement such as “we're fundraising, have eight months of runway, and are evaluating a SAFE” therefore encodes a concrete financial situation: the company is raising capital, has a defined cash horizon under assumptions, and is considering an instrument in which investment converts to equity later according to contractual terms.

The practical objective is contextual fluency, not specialist mastery. Startup terminology contains many additional terms and dialects, and definitions vary by business, market, and transaction structure. The useful skill is recognizing the context, understanding how the concepts connect, and knowing where an unfamiliar term fits into the larger structure.
