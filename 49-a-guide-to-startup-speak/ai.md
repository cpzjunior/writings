The guide treats startup vocabulary as a foreign language and proposes learning it progressively rather than as a definitive dictionary. Inspired by Código Fonte TV’s Programmer’s Dictionary, it organizes the most useful startup concepts by increasing complexity, emphasizing relationships between terms used in discussions about product, market, growth, economics, capital, and ownership. The definitions are intentionally practical because startup terminology varies by context, and the goal is to enable readers to recognize concepts and follow conversations rather than become specialists. The guide therefore prioritizes a manageable vocabulary that provides enough context to understand unfamiliar terms later.

The first level introduces the basic vocabulary needed to identify what kind of company and participants are being discussed. A startup is generally an organization pursuing a repeatable and scalable business model under uncertainty, rather than merely a new or small company. Founder and co-founder describe the people who established the company, while customer and user may be the same person or different people, particularly in B2B businesses where a company pays and its employees use the product. B2B and B2C distinguish sales to businesses from sales to consumers and affect sales dynamics, decision-making, sales cycles, and distribution. SaaS describes software delivered as a service, usually through recurring payments, while a marketplace connects supply and demand so participants can transact. Together, these terms allow someone unfamiliar with startup terminology to understand a basic description of a company without treating each acronym as an isolated concept.

The second level connects vocabulary into the basic logic of building a startup. The process begins with a problem, a solution addressing it, a value proposition explaining its relevance, and a business model describing how value is created, delivered, and captured. ICP identifies the type of customer with the strongest fit for the problem, potential value, and ability to be efficiently acquired and served. TAM, SAM, and SOM progressively narrow the potential market from the broad addressable opportunity to the portion the company can serve and the portion it realistically expects to capture. An MVP is the smallest product or experiment capable of testing a meaningful hypothesis and generating learning, while validation means obtaining behavioral evidence that supports or weakens that hypothesis rather than relying mainly on expressions of interest. PMF describes a condition in which product and market fit together through evidence of sustained demand, while a moat represents mechanisms such as network effects, technology, data, brand, switching costs, scale, or distribution that make the business harder to replicate or customers harder to lose. A pivot is a significant product or business-strategy change prompted by learning that invalidates an important assumption, while traction is evidence of business progress through metrics such as revenue, customers, users, growth, or retention and is not synonymous with either revenue or PMF. Once this story can be communicated coherently, the pitch and pitch deck organize the problem, solution, market, business model, traction, team, and capital requirements for an audience such as investors.

The third level focuses on what happens after people encounter a product: how they enter, progress through it, experience value, and remain or leave. Acquisition brings users or customers into the product or sales process through channels such as paid media, content, referrals, sales, partnerships, or organic search. A funnel represents the stages through which people progress, while a lead is someone who has demonstrated relevant interest and entered the acquisition or sales process, although this concept varies by business model. Conversion rate measures movement between funnel stages and helps identify friction. Activation is the first behavior through which a user experiences the product’s core value, with the appropriate event differing by product. Retention measures continued usage, while churn measures customer or revenue loss, with the distinction between customer churn and revenue churn being economically significant. Cohorts group users or customers according to a relevant characteristic, commonly the period in which they entered, allowing retention and behavioral changes to be observed over time rather than hidden by aggregate averages. These concepts connect acquisition with downstream behavior: increasing acquisition does not necessarily improve the business if users fail to activate or increasingly poor retention appears in newer cohorts.

The fourth level introduces the economics of growth at the customer or transaction level. CAC measures the cost of acquiring customers, while LTV estimates the economic value generated by a customer over the relationship, with methodology and assumptions affecting the quality of the estimate and economic contribution being more informative than revenue alone. Their relationship helps determine whether acquiring customers creates or destroys value. Unit economics examines the economics of an individual unit such as a customer, order, or transaction, including acquisition cost, economic contribution, margin, and service costs. Payback measures the time required to recover CAC from the customer’s economic contribution, and longer payback generally increases the capital required to sustain growth. Gross margin measures the portion of revenue remaining after costs directly associated with delivering the product or service, so revenue growth can coexist with poor economics when margins are insufficient. In SaaS, MRR measures monthly recurring revenue and ARR annualized recurring revenue; ARR is often simplified as twelve times MRR but is not necessarily equivalent to accounting revenue recognized during the period. Consequently, recurring-revenue growth needs to be interpreted alongside CAC, LTV, gross margin, payback, and cash generation rather than treated as evidence of healthy growth by itself.

The fifth level concerns the mechanisms through which a company reaches the market and grows. Growth can be measured through customers, revenue, active users, transactions, or another metric appropriate to the business model, but the important task is identifying the mechanisms that make the relevant metric increase consistently and economically. GTM, or Go-to-Market, describes how the company reaches and operates in its market, including audience, positioning, distribution, sales approach, and market presentation. Pricing concerns not only the numerical price but also monetization model, charging unit, plans, limits, discounts, and the relationship between price and perceived value. Sales-led Growth places a sales organization at the center of acquisition and conversion and is common in complex or high-ticket products requiring demonstrations, negotiation, or human interaction. Product-led Growth places the product itself at the center of acquisition, activation, conversion, retention, and expansion, without necessarily eliminating sales teams. These approaches are not necessarily mutually exclusive or permanent: a company may begin with founder-led sales, add a sales organization, incorporate PLG mechanisms, or change its strategy. Growth is therefore the interaction of product, market, acquisition, conversion, retention, pricing, and economics, and not simply increased acquisition spending; some companies may instead prioritize margin, cash generation, or slower growth.

The sixth level shifts from growth to the financing capacity behind it. Burn rate measures the rate at which the company consumes net cash, generally tracked monthly, while runway estimates how long available cash can sustain operations under specified assumptions. A company with R$3 million in cash and R$500,000 of monthly consumption has a simplified six-month runway, although real calculations must account for changing revenue, expenses, hiring, and other factors because burn is not necessarily constant. Runway informs decisions about reducing expenses, increasing cash generation, or raising capital, and waiting until cash is exhausted can be problematic because fundraising itself may require months. Bootstrapping means financing the business primarily through founders’ resources and operating revenue, potentially preserving ownership and reducing dependence on investors while constraining growth speed or increasing the need for early positive cash generation. Fundraising involves preparing the investment case and materials, approaching investors, responding to questions, due diligence, negotiating terms, and closing the transaction. External capital can finance hiring, product development, acquisition, geographic expansion, or additional time to reach a milestone, but often involves transferring part of the company’s ownership to investors, making financing simultaneously an operational and ownership decision.

The seventh level introduces investors and financing stages. Angel investors are individuals investing their own money, generally at early stages, and may also contribute experience, contacts, market knowledge, or guidance. Venture capital funds invest institutional capital in companies with high growth potential, using an investment thesis and portfolio model in which significant returns from successful investments are intended to compensate for losses elsewhere. Financing stages are commonly described as Pre-seed, Seed, Series A, and later rounds, but these labels are not universal categories with rigid criteria and can represent materially different situations across companies and markets. Pre-seed generally refers to very early companies that may lack a fully developed product or substantial market evidence; Seed commonly corresponds to companies with some validation that are developing the product and business; Series A generally involves a more consistent body of evidence concerning product, market, and growth capacity. The appropriate investor and stage depend on the company’s actual maturity, evidence, and financing objective rather than the label alone.

The eighth level explains ownership. Equity represents an ownership interest in a company, while a cap table records the distribution of ownership among founders, investors, employees with equity or options, and potentially other rights that may convert into ownership. A simple founding structure can become more complex as investment, employee incentives, and new issuances are introduced. Dilution occurs when new equity issuance or conversion reduces an existing holder’s percentage ownership, but a lower percentage does not necessarily mean lower economic value. A founder whose 50% stake in a R$10 million company is worth approximately R$5 million could hold 40% of a R$20 million company after dilution, representing approximately R$8 million in value under the simplified assumptions. Therefore, ownership must be evaluated through valuation, economic and control rights, transaction terms, and the broader cap table rather than percentage alone. Option pools reserve ownership for future employee or other equity grants, establishing that ownership can evolve over time rather than remaining fixed.

The ninth level describes how an investment round is negotiated. Valuation is an assessment of the company’s value based on factors such as stage, growth, market, risk, traction, comparable companies, and transaction terms. Pre-money valuation is the value before a new investment, while post-money valuation reflects the value after the new capital enters. Under the simplified example of R$20 million pre-money valuation and R$5 million investment, post-money valuation is R$25 million and the investor represents approximately 20% of the post-money company. A SAFE, or Simple Agreement for Future Equity, allows capital to enter before the corresponding ownership is determined, with conversion occurring later according to contractual conditions. A valuation cap establishes a valuation limit relevant to conversion, while a discount provides a reduced price relative to a future financing price under the instrument’s rules. A term sheet summarizes the principal proposed transaction terms, such as valuation, investment, rights, and other conditions, and serves as a basis for formalizing the transaction, so discussions about a term sheet concern specific financing conditions rather than merely the general intention to raise money.

The tenth level connects ownership with long-term incentives and eventual liquidity. Stock options give someone a conditional future right to acquire ownership, while an ESOP organizes such grants for employees. Vesting makes the right accrue progressively over time, linking ownership to continued employment or contribution, and a cliff establishes an initial period during which no ownership is acquired; a common four-year schedule with a one-year cliff provides an example, although actual terms vary. Partnership can have a different meaning in organizations such as investment banks, asset managers, and professional-services firms, where certain executives may also have an ownership interest, rather than referring simply to a commercial partnership between companies. The cap table therefore may encompass existing shareholders, investors, employee options, option pools, and future rights, all of which can change through new grants, financing rounds, and dilution. An exit is an event through which shareholders and investors may realize value, with acquisition, merger, and IPO among the possible forms. These concepts connect the entire startup lifecycle: founders identify a problem, develop a solution, seek PMF, acquire and retain customers, measure economics, grow, raise capital, distribute ownership, modify the cap table over time, and eventually may realize the accumulated business value through an exit.

The end-of-level answers reinforce the intended reasoning rather than introducing new concepts. A newly created B2C SaaS company is not automatically a startup because startup status depends on the pursuit of a repeatable and scalable model under uncertainty; the buyer is the customer and, when the same person uses the product, also the user. An MVP is generally preferable to immediately building the complete product when key hypotheses remain untested, and evidence from early experiments should determine whether a substantial strategic change constitutes a pivot. If acquisition increases while activation and retention deteriorate, the problem may lie downstream in the funnel rather than in acquisition itself. Rapid MRR growth does not establish healthy economics without considering CAC, LTV, gross margin, and payback. A company struggling to grow despite having a good product should investigate GTM before simply increasing acquisition spending, with Sales-led and Product-led approaches depending on product characteristics, ticket size, sales complexity, and value perception. Fundraising should be considered before runway reaches zero because it takes time and depends on growth, expenses, cash generation, investment needs, and market conditions. Very early companies with limited traction may seek angel investors and Pre-seed or Seed financing, although these are not rigid rules. Dilution must be evaluated through both percentage ownership and economic value, as well as rights, transaction terms, and future ownership represented in the cap table. A R$20 million pre-money valuation plus R$5 million investment produces R$25 million post-money and approximately 20% ownership for the new investor in the simplified case, whereas a SAFE defers the ownership determination. Finally, understanding value at an acquisition requires examining the cap table, investor equity, employee options and their vesting, option pools, subsequent dilution, and the rights associated with each ownership interest rather than merely identifying current shareholders.

The conclusion returns to the central purpose of the guide: vocabulary becomes useful when it allows concrete situations to be understood rather than merely memorized. A statement such as “we're fundraising, we have eight months of runway, and we're evaluating a SAFE” describes a company seeking capital, operating within a defined cash horizon, and considering a financing instrument whose ownership consequences may be determined later. Startup Speak is therefore not merely a collection of difficult English expressions but a vocabulary for describing how companies are built, operated, financed, owned, and ultimately capable of realizing value. Familiarity comes through recognizing the relationships among terms and applying them in context, while unfamiliar terminology remains normal and can be understood by identifying its place within the broader conversation. The intended outcome is not fluency in every startup term, but enough contextual understanding to follow discussions and investigate new vocabulary as it appears.
