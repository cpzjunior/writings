# Has DevOps Become Too Big for a Single Profession?

_If Security can be divided into specialties, why does DevOps still need to fit into a single profession?_

**Summary:** The problem with DevOps is not that it has become too big, but that we have turned an operating model into a profession that needs to carry everything it encompasses. Just as Security combines integration with specialization, DevOps can distribute depth across capabilities such as SRE, Platform Engineering, and Security without rebuilding the silos it sought to eliminate. Generalists still make sense when the context requires them, but not as a universal expectation. The better approach is to define roles based on the capabilities the organization needs to build, rather than trying to make the entire model fit into a single person.

---

Some time ago, I wrote about Security and the tension between control and speed in “[The Security Dilemma: Between Control and Speed](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/o-dilema-de-security-entre-o-controle)”. One of the things that caught my attention was precisely the breadth of the domain. Security can involve identity, applications, infrastructure, cloud, data, architecture, privacy, fraud, incident response, intelligence, third parties, and compliance. And, despite all of this being related to the same domain, it is natural to find different roles and specialties working on distinct problems.

After publishing that article, I started looking for other areas of IT that could lead to a similar reflection. Not necessarily to keep talking about Security, but to observe how other domains deal with their own growth. What happens when an area incorporates new responsibilities and starts to involve increasingly different areas of knowledge? At what point does it make sense to divide these capabilities into specialties?

That is how I arrived at DevOps. DevOps emerged as a movement and an approach for bringing Development and Operations closer together. These areas traditionally had distinct responsibilities within the software lifecycle, and the distance between them could create problems with collaboration, speed, and accountability for what reached production. The proposal was to bring these responsibilities closer together, improve the flow between building and operating software, and reduce the barriers between the teams involved in this process.

In this sense, DevOps did not begin as a profession. It was a different way of thinking about the relationship between parts of an organization that needed to work together. Over time, however, the term came to represent much more than this change in operating model. Cloud, infrastructure as code, automation, continuous integration and delivery, containers, observability, and reliability became, in different contexts, part of the universe associated with DevOps. Security also entered this discussion, giving rise to the term DevSecOps. More recently, AI and agents are beginning to introduce new possibilities and new responsibilities for software development and operations.

At the same time, the market saw the emergence and consolidation of DevOps Engineer as a title for professionals responsible for a significant portion of these capabilities. The problem is that, as the universe associated with DevOps grows, so does the expectation placed on this profession.

This is where the discussion becomes interesting. There is an important difference between an operating model becoming broader and a profession needing to incorporate everything that this model comes to encompass. The first can happen naturally. The second creates a difficult question: is there any limit to what we can reasonably expect from a single specialty?

Perhaps the problem is not that DevOps has become too big. Perhaps it is that we are trying to fit an operating model into a single profession.

## When an Operating Model Becomes a Job Title

DevOps did not emerge as a list of technologies someone should know. The original concern was much more related to how Development and Operations worked together. Development and operations had distinct responsibilities within the software lifecycle, and the distance between these areas could create problems with collaboration, speed, and accountability for what reached production. The DevOps proposal was to bring these responsibilities closer together, improve delivery flow, automate activities, and create a more integrated relationship between building and operating software.

This is different from defining a profession. An operating model describes how an organization works. It influences responsibilities, processes, tools, teams, and forms of collaboration, but it does not need to be represented by a specific person. An organization can adopt DevOps practices without having a team called DevOps and can distribute its capabilities across different teams without ceasing to operate according to its principles.

The emergence of the DevOps Engineer is not, in itself, a contradiction. Organizations need to turn needs into roles and need people capable of taking on certain responsibilities. In many contexts, having someone with broad knowledge of infrastructure, automation, and delivery makes perfect sense.

The question begins when the profession's name starts carrying the entire model. From that point onward, everything that participates in the DevOps universe can become another expectation placed on the professional. Infrastructure and automation give way to cloud, CI/CD, containers, infrastructure as code, and observability. Kubernetes enters. Reliability enters. Security enters. Depending on the context, networking, operating systems, scripting, architecture, and development also appear in the job description.

Each of these competencies can be perfectly legitimate. The problem lies in the accumulation. If everything that participates in the operating model is interpreted as the DevOps Engineer's responsibility, the profession grows along with the concept itself. And there is a fundamental difference between these two things.

The fact that different capabilities participate in the same way of working does not mean they need to be concentrated in the same person. An organization may need deep knowledge in several domains without having someone who deeply masters all of them. In fact, that is precisely why specializations exist.

A professional can have a broad view of the system, understand the dependencies between its parts, and know enough about adjacent areas to collaborate with specialists without needing to replace them. They can understand how an infrastructure decision affects security, reliability, or costs without simultaneously being a specialist in Security, SRE, and FinOps.

Having context is not the same as possessing expertise. This distinction is important because perhaps we are trying to solve an organizational problem as if it were a professional training problem. Instead of asking what capabilities an organization needs to possess and how they should relate to one another, we start asking how many of these capabilities a single person can accumulate.

As the organization grows in scale and complexity, the limits of this concentration become more evident. Not because generalists cease to be necessary. On the contrary, they can be extremely valuable. The problem is turning generalism into a universal obligation of a profession whose scope continues to expand.

When this happens, the job title stops describing a specialty and starts functioning as a container for everything the organization has not yet decided where to put.

## And Then We Created DevSecOps

Up to this point, we could argue that the problem is simply one of depth. DevOps grew, new technologies emerged, and the professional needs to keep up with this evolution. It would still be possible to imagine that there is some reasonable limit to what fits within the profession.

Then Security enters. DevSecOps emerges with a proposition that is difficult to dispute: security should not be treated as a later stage of development. It needs to be part of the delivery lifecycle. If DevOps brought Development and Operations closer together, why not bring Security closer as well?

The logic seems perfectly reasonable. But if we accept this logic as a criterion for defining the profession's scope, we have just opened a door that is difficult to close.

If Security enters because security affects software delivery and operations, what do we do with everything else that also affects this same lifecycle? Cloud costs affect operations. Capacity management affects operations. Compliance affects operations. Architecture affects operations. Developer experience affects productivity and, therefore, delivery.

With every new concern, there is a plausible justification for adding another competency to the same professional. And at this point, the problem is no longer just keeping up with the evolution of DevOps tools. The profession begins incorporating entire areas because they have some relationship with software development and operations.

And then we arrive at AI. If AI starts transforming the way software is developed, tested, deployed, or operated, it seems natural that a DevOps Engineer should at least understand how these changes affect their work. If agents begin executing engineering tasks, there is a need to understand how to operate and integrate them. If agents begin communicating with one another, new abstractions, such as A2A, may enter the discussion. And if tomorrow another technology emerges that can once again alter the way systems are built and operated, there will also be a justification for adding it.

The question stops being “what does a DevOps Engineer need to know today?” and becomes “what else can we add tomorrow?” The problem is that this question has no natural answer. Today it is AI. Tomorrow it will be another technology. Then another. Each one can bring a new justification for expanding the profession's scope.

If everything that influences software development and operations can be considered part of DevOps, then the boundary begins to disappear.

And when the boundary disappears, the caricature stops seeming quite so absurd. If everything that impacts software development and operations can be DevOps, then the DevOps Engineer can take care of infrastructure, cloud, security, architecture, observability, reliability, compliance, FinOps, AI agents, budgeting, vendors, hiring, people management, risk, product, marketing, sales, investments, printers, elevators, fire extinguishers, and even the coffee.

And perhaps this is where the paradox appears: if everything is DevOps, then nothing is DevOps. If the DevOps Engineer needs to know everything, we are faced with two possibilities: either we look for a professional so rare that their cost becomes prohibitive, or we describe a professional who, in practice, does not exist.

With each new responsibility, there will be a plausible reason to include it. And that is precisely the problem. We are no longer discussing how much a professional can learn. We are discussing a profession whose definition allows its scope to continue growing indefinitely.

## Security Already Showed Us a Path

This was precisely where Security came back to mind. Security is also a cross-cutting domain. It touches development, infrastructure, identity, data, networks, applications, cloud, and operations. The breadth is large and continues to grow, but it does not seem necessary to solve this breadth by concentrating everything into a single profession.

Throughout its evolution, Security has also become organized around specialties. Application Security, Cloud Security, Identity, Threat Intelligence, Incident Response, GRC, and other areas can have very different knowledge, responsibilities, and objectives. Yet they remain part of the same domain.

Specialization allows different problems to be addressed with the necessary depth without requiring a single person to be an expert in all of them.

This point seems obvious when we talk about Security. An Identity professional does not need to be an Incident Response specialist. An Application Security specialist does not need to deeply master GRC. This does not mean they work in isolation or that they do not need to understand the broader context. It simply means there is a difference between being part of the same domain and practicing the same profession.

And perhaps this is precisely the distinction missing in DevOps. Not because DevOps and Security are equivalent, but because both demonstrate that breadth and specialization can coexist. A domain can be cross-cutting and, at the same time, have different specialties without necessarily destroying its integration.

The most immediate response would be to say that DevOps was born precisely to eliminate silos. But eliminating silos does not mean eliminating specialization. An organization can have different specialists working on different parts of the same system and still require collaboration, shared responsibility, and common objectives. Specialization does not have to mean isolation.

Perhaps the problem is that we have confused integration with concentration. DevOps can continue to be a model that connects different capabilities without requiring all of them to fit within a single person's job description.

The question, then, is no longer whether DevOps needs to be divided. Perhaps the more useful question is this: how can we specialize the capabilities that exist within this universe without rebuilding precisely the silos that DevOps was created to reduce?

## Specializing Without Rebuilding the Silos

Of course, there is a counterargument. If we start dividing DevOps into specialties, we could end up creating exactly the scenario DevOps tried to overcome: an infrastructure team, a security team, a platform team, an observability team, and a reliability team, each with its own backlog, priorities, and interfaces. Developers would then depend on all of them to deliver anything. In that case, we would merely replace one large silo with several smaller ones.

That does not seem to be the goal. Specialization should not mean fragmenting the workflow. A team can have depth in a particular capability without taking on all the work that depends on it. A platform team can build and evolve a platform without operating every application that uses it. A team dedicated to reliability can deepen SRE practices without becoming responsible for the entire operation of products. Security specialists can establish capabilities and act on risks without taking responsibility for every engineering decision.

Responsibility for the product can remain close to those who develop it. What changes is where the necessary depth resides to solve problems that exceed the knowledge reasonably expected of a single role.

This also does not mean that DevOps Engineers should cease to exist or that generalist professionals are a problem. In a small startup, for example, it is perfectly reasonable to have one person responsible for infrastructure, cloud, automation, and delivery. When there are few people, accumulating responsibilities can be an advantage. The generalist may be precisely the person who connects the different parts.

The issue arises when this reality is transformed into a universal definition of the profession. As an organization grows, so do the amount of knowledge, the systems involved, and the responsibilities that need to be addressed. In this context, specialization can be a way to distribute depth without necessarily fragmenting the delivery flow.

A professional does not need to know everything to understand the system. They need to know enough about adjacent areas to make good decisions, recognize dependencies, and work with those who possess the necessary depth.

This applies to architecture and it applies to DevOps. An architect does not need to deeply master every technology present in a solution. They need to understand its implications and be able to connect different specialties into a coherent decision. Perhaps DevOps is reaching a similar point.

And, in a way, the market itself offers signs of this change. SRE and Platform Engineering have come to occupy distinct spaces in many organizations, with more specific responsibilities and competencies. Security has had a similar structure for a long time. It is not necessary to interpret these movements as a replacement for DevOps. They can be seen as signs that some of the capabilities associated with the DevOps universe have come to require their own identity and depth.

This leads us to a distinction that tends to get lost when we use “DevOps” to describe different things. There is the knowledge domain. There is the operating model. There are the teams that assume certain responsibilities. And there are the professions practiced by the people who work on these teams.

These layers do not need to have the same name. DevOps can continue to be an operating model that guides collaboration between different capabilities. SRE can exist as a discipline focused on reliability. Platform Engineering can exist as a specialty dedicated to building internal platforms. Security can continue to be a domain composed of several specialties. And DevOps Engineer can continue to exist when it makes sense for a particular context.

None of this requires one of these things to be the complete representation of the others. The model can span multiple specialties without all of them needing to be concentrated in a single profession.

Perhaps this is the distinction that was missing in the evolution of the term. We do not need to break DevOps into independent pieces. We need to stop confusing the model with the person who participates in it.

Specializing does not need to mean creating new boundaries. It can simply mean putting depth where it is necessary while maintaining integration where it remains necessary.

## Perhaps the Future Is Less About Knowing Everything

This also changes the question we ask when opening a position. Instead of asking which technologies a DevOps Engineer needs to master, perhaps we should start by asking which capability the organization needs to build. Do we need someone to create a platform? To improve reliability? To structure infrastructure? To automate delivery? To work with observability? To address a specific security problem?

The answers may point to different specialties. In some cases, they will point to a generalist DevOps professional. In others, to SRE, Platform Engineering, Security, or another specialty. And that does not need to be a problem.

The organization should define the role based on the problem it needs to solve, rather than defining the problem based on a profession that already exists. This difference may seem small, but it changes how we think about the organization itself. Instead of trying to find one person who carries everything the model requires, we start building an organization capable of executing it, distributing depth where it is necessary and maintaining collaboration where it is essential.

Perhaps this is the answer to the question that gave rise to this reflection: Has DevOps become too big for a single profession? The problem is not that DevOps has become broader, nor the existence of a profession called DevOps Engineer. The problem arises when we treat this profession as if it needed to represent everything the model has come to encompass. At that point, every new technology, discipline, or responsibility added to the world of software development and operations can become another individual requirement, without any clear limit to that expansion.

DevOps can continue to be an operating model. It can continue to guide how different capabilities collaborate to build and operate software. DevOps Engineers can continue to be generalist professionals when the context justifies that breadth. What does not seem sustainable is turning that breadth into a universal expectation for the profession.

In the end, perhaps the problem is not the size of the DevOps Engineer, but the mistake of turning an operating model into a job description. DevOps does not need to become smaller to continue making sense. It simply needs to stop being treated as something that, in its entirety, has to fit inside one person.
