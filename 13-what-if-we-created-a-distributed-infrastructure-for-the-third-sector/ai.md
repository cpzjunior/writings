It is difficult to walk through a large city without encountering information about missing persons, distributed across physical and digital channels in the hope that someone can recognize the person and provide useful information. Unlike commercial information, whose value may depend on attribution, ownership, and capturing the economic value of an interaction, information about missing people illustrates a different logic: its value increases when it reaches more people capable of acting, regardless of which channel produced the connection. The same applies to third-sector needs such as fundraising, volunteer recruitment, food distribution, or community assistance. This suggests the possibility of digital infrastructure specifically designed to maximize the circulation of socially valuable information rather than its exclusivity.

A distributed third-sector infrastructure would need to avoid creating another centralized intermediary while allowing organizations to preserve their existing systems, databases, and autonomy. Information should be published at its source and automatically become discoverable by different applications, avoiding the need to manually reproduce the same information across multiple platforms. Because social information changes continuously, updates, corrections, fulfilled needs, and expired opportunities would also need to propagate. The same information should support different uses by portals, regional applications, organizations, researchers, and other systems without the infrastructure prescribing those uses. Privacy, governance, access control, and organizational responsibility would remain necessary, but the central principle would be a distributed, interoperable, open layer that enables independent systems to communicate. The problem therefore shifts from improving dissemination through another platform to defining a protocol through which independent participants can collaborate without becoming a single system.

Existing infrastructures demonstrate that independent systems can collaborate through common standards without becoming centralized. RSS allows websites to publish standardized representations of their own content so that aggregators can discover and distribute it without requiring individual integrations with every application. Open Finance similarly allows independent financial institutions to retain their own systems and data while exchanging information through regulated common standards; interoperability therefore does not require a common database. Federated social networks and protocols such as ActivityPub provide another example in which independent servers, applications, and communities communicate through shared rules. None of these models should simply be copied for the third sector, because each addresses different requirements, but together they demonstrate an alternative between isolated systems and a centralized platform. For a diverse ecosystem spanning education, social assistance, emergencies, environmental initiatives, animal protection, and employment, the goal would be a common language that permits different systems to recognize one another, exchange authorized information, and track changes while preserving organizational differences and autonomy.

A third-sector organization responding to an emergency normally has to publish information through multiple channels and build its own audience across systems with different rules and communities. A distributed infrastructure could reverse this relationship: the organization would publish information once in its own environment, while independent applications and organizations could discover, contextualize, and distribute it according to their own audiences and purposes. The same campaign or need could therefore appear simultaneously on multiple platforms without duplication being treated as a problem. This differs from commercial platforms, where duplicated information can create competition over traffic, attribution, conversions, and economic value. In a social-impact infrastructure, success would instead be associated with whether information reaches someone capable of acting on it, such as a donor, volunteer, resource provider, or organization able to address a need. Distribution would consequently become part of the impact itself rather than merely a mechanism for capturing value.

Emergency situations make the value of distributed information particularly apparent because many needs, resources, actors, and circumstances change simultaneously, while no participant possesses the complete picture. During events such as the 2024 floods in Rio Grande do Sul or the Brumadinho dam collapse, independent shelters, organizations, volunteer groups, companies, communities, and institutions could publish information about capacity, needs, resources, equipment, locations, and operations from their own systems. Other applications could consume this information without requiring everyone to use a common platform. The critical benefit would be the ability of previously unconnected actors to discover one another: a local group could report a need unknown to a larger institution, a company could identify where its resources are required, or a volunteer team could discover a nearby operation. The infrastructure would therefore not need to maintain a complete centralized picture or determine who should solve each problem; it would need to make information about needs and available resources discoverable so that independent participants could establish useful connections.

A platform concentrates participants and controls the environment in which they interact, whereas a protocol establishes common rules that allow independent systems to communicate. The proposed infrastructure would therefore allow NGOs, portals, universities, community organizations, and other participants to retain their own technologies while sharing a common language for publishing and consuming information. This would allow an ecosystem of applications to emerge without requiring each developer to integrate individually with every organization: one application could map social opportunities, another could search campaigns, another could connect volunteers and organizations, and another could coordinate emergency resources. The model also challenges the assumption that social information derives value from exclusivity, while preserving the need for privacy, consent, security, authorization, and governance; some information could be public, some controlled, and some excluded from circulation entirely. Organizations would remain responsible for their data, while the protocol would reduce the need for ownership of data to also determine who can distribute or use it. The central design question would therefore be which common rules enable collaboration without requiring a single intermediary to control the network.

The internet already provides interoperable infrastructure for transporting information, while commercial platforms provide increasingly sophisticated mechanisms for organizing and distributing it; the proposal is to apply similar interoperability to information whose primary purpose is connecting needs, opportunities, resources, and people capable of acting. A small organization would not need the technological resources or audience of a large institution to make its information discoverable: publishing through its own system could make that information available to applications with their own communities and distribution capabilities. This would separate information production from information distribution, allowing organizations to focus more on their missions while other participants contribute discovery, contextualization, search, and dissemination capabilities. Developers could build applications without first persuading thousands of organizations to join another platform and reproduce their data. The resulting “social layer” would therefore not be another centralized destination but infrastructure through which existing platforms, applications, communities, and organizations could discover and connect independently produced information.

The proposal explicitly rejects both a single global database containing every organization and need and an attempt to standardize the third sector around a common operational model. Organizations working in education, emergencies, animal protection, employment, or other fields have different concepts, processes, indicators, and requirements, and those differences should remain intact. What needs standardization is only the minimum language required for systems to communicate. Organizations could choose what information to expose and continue using their own data models and processes, while applications could choose which information to consume and how to present it. The infrastructure would therefore connect diversity rather than eliminate it: one participant might publish volunteer opportunities, another campaigns, another emergency information, and another community-project data. Its value would come from enabling information produced by different organizations to find new paths of circulation, not from concentrating that information in a single system.

The development of AI agents introduces another potential use for open social infrastructure because agents increasingly can discover information, reason across sources, and eventually perform actions on behalf of people or organizations. A distributed third-sector infrastructure could provide agents with structured access to information about volunteer opportunities, emergencies, organizations, campaigns, needs, and available resources without requiring a single platform to aggregate everything. Standards such as MCP and A2A illustrate a broader movement toward interoperable interactions among models, tools, services, and agents, suggesting that an open information layer could become useful to machine participants as well as human users. An agent could discover a community need, identify organizations and resources in the relevant region, and help connect them, while another could monitor changes over time. However, discovering information and executing actions are materially different problems: actions such as donations, resource commitments, or sharing sensitive data require explicit authorization, trust, and accountability mechanisms. The protocol therefore would not need to dictate how AI works, but could provide an open information layer through which future applications and agents can discover and use socially relevant information without depending on a centralized database or requiring today's designers to predict tomorrow's applications.

The success of such infrastructure would be measured differently from that of a conventional platform: not by concentrated users, proprietary data, or economic value, but by participating organizations, diverse applications, and useful connections enabled between previously disconnected actors. Its network effects would also differ because value could increase while participants remain independent: more organizations would provide more information, more applications would increase distribution capacity, and more successful connections could encourage further participation. No participant would need to own the whole network; organizations could contribute information, developers applications, universities analysis, and communities new uses for the resulting data. This creates a cumulative form of technological infrastructure in which today's contributions may support actors and situations that their creators never anticipated. The resulting legacy would therefore belong not to a platform or company but to a network of contributions that becomes more useful as others build upon it. The proposed distributed infrastructure for the third sector is ultimately an attempt to maximize the propagation of social impact by creating open mechanisms through which information can circulate beyond the individual reach of the organizations that produce it.
