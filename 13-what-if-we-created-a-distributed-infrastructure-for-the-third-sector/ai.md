Information of social interest can increase in value through circulation rather than exclusivity. A distributed digital infrastructure for the third sector could therefore use open protocols to let independent organizations publish needs, opportunities, resources, campaigns, and initiatives from their existing systems while enabling other applications and organizations to discover, redistribute, contextualize, and update that information. The objective is not to create another centralized platform, database, or intermediary, but a common interoperable layer that preserves organizational autonomy and allows information to reach actors capable of turning it into action.

The infrastructure would need to be decentralized, interoperable, open, continuously updateable, compatible with existing systems, and usable by multiple applications for different purposes. Organizations should publish information once at its source rather than manually reproducing it across platforms. Changes, corrections, fulfilled needs, and expired opportunities should propagate as well. Different consumers could use the same information for portals, maps, search engines, volunteer matching, regional discovery, research, or emergency coordination. Privacy, security, consent, authorization, and governance would remain essential: information could have different access levels or be excluded from circulation entirely. Decentralization should apply not only to data storage but also to control over participation and distribution.

Existing infrastructures demonstrate that independent systems can collaborate through common standards without becoming one system. RSS allows publishers to expose standardized content to aggregators without knowing every future consumer. Open Finance allows independent financial institutions to exchange information through common APIs, formats, and regulated sharing mechanisms without requiring a common database. Federated networks such as those using ActivityPub allow independently operated servers and applications to communicate through shared protocols. These examples do not provide templates to copy directly, because each addresses different requirements, but they demonstrate an alternative between isolated systems and centralized platforms: independent participants can preserve autonomy while sharing a common communication language.

For the third sector, this model is particularly relevant because organizational diversity is structural. Organizations working in education, social assistance, emergencies, environmental protection, animal welfare, or employment have different concepts, processes, indicators, and systems. A common infrastructure should therefore standardize only what is necessary for interoperability, not impose a uniform operating model. One organization could expose volunteer opportunities, another campaigns, another emergency information, and another community projects. Each would retain its own systems, data models, processes, and responsibility for its information.

The distribution model reverses the conventional platform logic. An organization responding to an emergency normally has to publish through multiple channels, each with its own audience and rules, requiring it to invest effort both in its substantive mission and in building distribution channels. With distributed infrastructure, it could publish through its own system while applications and organizations independently discover and redistribute the information. The same campaign or need could appear simultaneously in a geographic application, a cause-oriented portal, a partner organization's community, and a search engine. Duplication would not be a failure; it would increase the probability of reaching someone able to act.

The relevant measure of success therefore changes. Commercial platforms often value attribution, audience capture, conversion tracking, and determining which channel generated an interaction. For social information, the primary outcome may instead be that the information reached someone capable of producing a useful result: a campaign found a donor, an opportunity found a volunteer, or a need found someone capable of addressing it. The origin of the connection may still matter operationally, but exclusive ownership of the distribution path need not determine the information's value. Circulation itself becomes part of social impact.

Emergency situations make this logic especially important because information is fragmented, rapidly changing, and distributed among many actors. During a large-scale flood such as the 2024 Rio Grande do Sul disaster, one organization might report shelter capacity, another a need for water in a region, another volunteer availability, a company available vehicles or equipment, and an institution available resources. During a disaster such as the Brumadinho dam collapse, similar coordination challenges arise. A distributed infrastructure could let each actor maintain its own system while making relevant information discoverable by other participants. The network would not need to know in advance which organization would solve a particular problem; it would need to enable information about needs and available resources to find each other. This can create connections between actors that were not part of the original operation and could not have been individually coordinated beforehand.

The central conceptual distinction is between platforms and protocols. A platform concentrates participants inside a controlled environment; a protocol establishes common rules allowing independent systems to interact. The proposed infrastructure should be a protocol or common layer rather than a replacement platform. NGOs, portals, universities, community organizations, developers, and other participants could retain independent technologies and applications while sharing a common language for publishing and consuming information. New applications could emerge without individually integrating with every existing organization. Developers could build maps, search engines, volunteer tools, emergency coordination systems, campaign discovery tools, or resource-matching applications without first creating a new centralized database.

This architecture also changes the economics of social information. Although data can have commercial value precisely because it is exclusive, social information can lose potential impact when exclusivity prevents circulation. Volunteer opportunities are more useful when they find volunteers; campaigns when they find donors; community needs when they find capable responders. This does not imply that all social information should be public or unrestricted. Controlled access, consent, privacy, security, and accountability remain necessary. The infrastructure should support different sharing permissions without allowing control over individual data to become control over the entire network. Organizations should remain responsible for their data while common standards reduce the need to make ownership itself the mechanism of distribution.

This suggests a broader "social layer" of the internet. Existing internet protocols already provide highly effective mechanisms for transporting information and connecting heterogeneous systems. What is comparatively underdeveloped is an interoperable layer optimized for information whose primary purpose is connecting needs, opportunities, resources, and initiatives with actors capable of responding, rather than generating advertising, audience, or revenue. A small organization would not need the reach or technological resources of a large institution: publishing through its own infrastructure could make its information discoverable by applications that already possess their own communities and distribution capabilities. Production and distribution would become separable functions.

The same separation could accelerate application development. A developer interested in volunteering would not need to persuade thousands of organizations to register on a new platform. An application could consume already available volunteer opportunities. Similar applications could be created for social initiatives, emergency coordination, resource matching, regional needs, or campaign discovery. The ecosystem could collectively combine local information, contextualization, discovery, and distribution without any participant controlling the complete network.

The proposal explicitly rejects the need for a global database containing every organization, project, and social need, as well as the need for a company or institution to control the third sector's information infrastructure. It also rejects imposing a single operational model on heterogeneous organizations. What should be common is the minimum language required for systems to communicate. Each organization decides what information to expose; each application decides what it consumes and how it presents it; participants contribute their own distribution capabilities. The network derives value from connecting diversity rather than eliminating it.

Artificial intelligence agents provide an additional reason for such infrastructure. As software agents increasingly discover information, reason over it, and execute actions on behalf of users and organizations, an open social-information layer could become a machine-readable source of context. Agents could search for volunteer opportunities, organizations operating in affected regions, campaigns related to causes, available resources, or unresolved needs across multiple independent sources instead of depending on a single platform.

Protocols such as MCP and A2A illustrate complementary aspects of this emerging ecosystem: MCP provides standardized interaction between AI systems and tools or context sources, while A2A addresses communication between agents. The proposed social infrastructure could provide agents with distributed information about organizations, needs, opportunities, and resources. An agent could discover a community requiring food, identify organizations operating there, find available resources, and help a person or institution determine how to contribute. Another agent could monitor changing information and detect when a need has been fulfilled or a new need has emerged.

However, discovering information and executing actions are distinct problems. Finding a campaign or need may require relatively straightforward discovery and retrieval; donating, committing resources, or sharing sensitive information requires authorization, trust, accountability, and explicit rules governing what an agent may do and on whose behalf. The protocol need not determine how AI should operate. It should provide interoperable mechanisms through which agents, applications, and organizations can discover information and, where authorized, interact with it.

An important benefit of building such infrastructure without prescribing its future applications is that its designers would not need to predict which interfaces, agents, or collaboration models will exist in five or ten years. Open, interoperable, distributed information can support unforeseen uses by people, applications, and agents. Intelligence and application logic can remain at the network's edge while the infrastructure provides discovery, interoperability, and controlled information exchange.

The resulting network effect would differ from that of centralized digital platforms. A conventional social network gains value by concentrating participants in one environment. An open social infrastructure can gain value because participants remain independent: more organizations increase the available information; more applications increase distribution capacity; more successful connections create incentives for additional participation. No participant needs to own the network. Organizations contribute information, developers contribute applications, universities contribute analysis, communities discover new uses, and each benefits from infrastructure created partly by others.

Its value would therefore be cumulative and communal. Information published today could help an unknown organization months or years later. An application created for one purpose could become useful in an unforeseen emergency. Participation would contribute not merely to solving an immediate need but to an infrastructure that others can reuse. The technological legacy would be an increasingly useful shared layer rather than a platform that accumulates users, data, or control.

The appropriate success metrics would consequently be different from those of commercial platforms. Relevant measures could include participating organizations, diversity of applications, successful connections, resources matched to needs, opportunities discovered, and coordination improvements during emergencies. The fundamental measure is the number and quality of relevant connections enabled by the network, rather than the amount of data controlled, users concentrated, or economic value captured. If information reaches someone who needs it, if a small organization accesses resources it could not otherwise find, or if people, organizations, and resources find one another faster during an emergency, the infrastructure has fulfilled its purpose.

The core proposal is therefore to build an open, distributed, interoperable protocol layer for the third sector that separates information production from information distribution, preserves the autonomy and responsibility of individual organizations, enables controlled circulation across independent systems, and can be consumed by people, applications, and AI agents. Its purpose is not to centralize social impact in another platform, but to allow the impact generated by thousands of independent initiatives to propagate beyond the distribution limits of each one.
