# What if we created a distributed infrastructure for the third sector?

_There is information whose social value increases with its circulation, rather than with its exclusivity._

**Summary:** I advocate for the creation of a distributed digital infrastructure for the third sector, based on open protocols that allow organizations to share information without giving up their own systems and autonomy. The idea is to separate production from distribution, allowing needs, opportunities, and resources to circulate across different applications and reach those who can turn them into action. Instead of concentrating data and power in a new platform, this common layer would collectively expand the reach of existing initiatives. Success, therefore, would not lie in owning the network, but in how many relevant connections it makes possible.

---

It is difficult to walk through a large city without encountering some form of information related to missing persons. On the subway, at bus stops, on posters spread throughout the streets, or on social media, photographs and information are shared in the hope that someone will recognize that person and be able to provide information that helps clarify their whereabouts. There are organizations dedicated to this work, as well as different databases that seek to gather and make this information available.

There is something particularly different about this form of communication. When a photograph of a missing person is reproduced in different places, no one should be concerned about determining which channel was responsible for the discovery. If someone recognizes that photograph because they saw it at a subway station, in a post shared by someone, or on a website that gathered information from different sources, the circulation has fulfilled its purpose. The value of that information lies precisely in its ability to reach someone who can help.

This principle seems simple, but it contrasts with an important part of the logic we have built on the internet. Much of today's digital infrastructure was developed around audience, ownership, and capturing value from data. Platforms compete for users' attention, companies seek to concentrate information, and advertising systems try to precisely identify the origin of each interaction. In this model, knowing where a person found information and which platform generated a given action can have significant economic value.

But there is information for which this attribution logic is secondary. A third-sector organization that needs to raise funds should not necessarily be concerned with discovering which platform deserves credit for bringing a donor to its campaign. An organization looking for volunteers should be interested in finding people willing to help, regardless of where they discovered that opportunity. A community initiative that needs food should be interested in reaching those who can contribute, rather than necessarily controlling every place where its need was publicized.

In these cases, information does not become less valuable when it ceases to be exclusive. It becomes more valuable as it manages to circulate. And this leads us to a question: what if we created digital infrastructure specifically designed to facilitate this circulation?

## Infrastructure Characteristics

If the problem is to make information of social interest circulate more effectively, the first question should not be which technology to use, but what characteristics this infrastructure would need to have.

First, it could not depend on a single point of control. Third-sector organizations are independent and have different structures, capabilities, and needs. It would make little sense to solve fragmentation by creating a new centralized system that would concentrate all this information and become, in itself, a new intermediary.

At the same time, organizations should not need to abandon their existing systems. An NGO that already has a website, management system, or database should be able to continue using its own infrastructure. The new layer would need to complement what already exists, rather than require everyone to migrate to a common platform.

It would also be necessary to reduce the work of distribution. If an organization needs to manually register the same information on ten different platforms, we are still dealing with the original problem of fragmentation. Information should be published at the source and, from there, be able to reach different places without requiring the organization to reproduce the same work.

This circulation would also need to be continuous. Social information changes. A campaign may reach its goal, a position may be filled, a need may cease to exist, an organization may update an opportunity, or information may need to be corrected. It is not enough to distribute information just once. Its changes must also be able to circulate.

Another requirement would be to allow different applications to do different things with the same information. A portal might want to display fundraising campaigns. An application might want to show opportunities near a particular region. An organization might want to find other initiatives working in the same area. A researcher might want to analyze aggregated information. The infrastructure should not determine how information will be used. Its role would be to make it available for different uses.

And there is perhaps a requirement more important than all the others: the infrastructure should not turn the circulation of information into a mechanism for concentrating power. If the goal is to expand social impact, it would make little sense to create a new institution that would control who can distribute certain information, which organizations can participate, or which application will have privileged access to the data.

In other words, we would need infrastructure that is distributed, interoperable, open, and capable of preserving the autonomy of those who produce the information.

At this point, the question ceases to be merely how to improve the dissemination of third-sector initiatives. It becomes how to create a common layer that allows independent systems to collaborate in distributing this information without having to become a single system. This is where the idea of a protocol begins to make sense.

## What Other Infrastructures Can Inspire Us

The idea of creating distributed infrastructure for the third sector is not based on the premise that we need to invent a new way for independent systems to collaborate. The history of the internet and other digital infrastructures already offers several examples of participants preserving their autonomy while following common standards for exchanging information.

RSS is perhaps the simplest example. A website can remain responsible for the content it produces while providing a standardized representation of that content so that aggregators and other applications can discover and distribute it. The site does not need to know in advance every place where its content will be displayed, nor does it need to create a specific integration with every interested application. There is a common layer that allows new participants to appear without the entire network having to be redesigned.

Open Finance takes this principle into a much more complex context. Different financial institutions continue operating their own systems, maintaining their own data, and building their own products, but they can participate in a sharing infrastructure based on common standards. The goal is not to turn banks into a single platform, but to allow independent systems to communicate in a predictable and secure manner. In Brazil, this model is regulated by the Central Bank and uses common standards for APIs, data formats, and sharing mechanisms.

The experience of Open Finance is particularly interesting because it demonstrates that interoperability does not mean centralization. The existence of a common language does not require a common database. Participants remain responsible for their own systems and data, while the shared infrastructure establishes the conditions under which certain information can circulate between them.

Another example appears in federated social networks and protocols such as ActivityPub. In this model, different servers can belong to different organizations, use different applications, and serve independent communities, while still being able to communicate through a common protocol. Once again, the network does not depend on a single platform to exist. What enables the connection is the adoption of common communication rules.

These examples are not models that should simply be copied for the third sector. Each solves specific problems and has its own requirements. Their value lies in demonstrating that there is an alternative between two extremes we tend to consider: completely isolated systems or a large centralized platform. Between these two models lies a third possibility, in which different participants preserve their autonomy and yet become part of a shared infrastructure.

This possibility is particularly interesting for the third sector because diversity is a structural characteristic of this ecosystem. We do not want to turn organizations working in education, social assistance, emergencies, the environment, animal protection, or income generation into different versions of the same organization. Nor do we need to determine in advance which information will be most important or which applications will be built on top of it.

We simply need to create conditions under which different systems can recognize one another, exchange information, and track its changes when there is interest and authorization to do so.

This is how these cases serve as inspiration. The challenge would not be to invent a new way of centralizing social information, but to apply the logic already demonstrated by other infrastructures: independent participants can build something greater when there is a common language that allows their parts to connect.

## The Logic of Distribution

Imagine an organization that needs to raise funds to assist a community affected by an emergency. Today, it needs to make that information find its audience on its own. It can publish on its own website, share on social media, seek partners, send messages, and try to reach different communities. Each channel has its own rules, its own distribution mechanisms, and, most importantly, its own audience. The organization ends up needing to invest energy not only in what it does, but also in building the channels necessary for people to discover that it exists and understand how they can help.

A distributed infrastructure could reverse this logic. The organization would continue publishing information in its own environment, but that information could be discovered by different applications and organizations interested in distributing it. An application could present campaigns near a person's location, a portal could bring together initiatives related to a particular cause, an organization could share opportunities with its own community, and a search engine could find initiatives available in a given region. The same information could therefore exist in different places at the same time, reaching audiences that the original organization would hardly be able to reach on its own.

And this would not be a failure of the infrastructure. It would be one of its main characteristics. On the commercial internet, information duplication often represents a problem because different platforms compete for audience, traffic, and attribution. If two platforms present the same information, there is an incentive to determine which one will keep the user and the economic value generated by that interaction. In infrastructure oriented toward social impact, this logic can be reversed. Information does not need to compete for a place. It can occupy several places simultaneously, and each new distribution can represent a new opportunity to produce some result.

The difference lies in what we consider success. On a commercial platform, it may be important to know where each user came from, which content generated a particular conversion, and which channel should receive credit for an interaction. For a social initiative, the most important outcome is often simply that the information reached someone capable of taking action. A campaign found a donor, an opportunity found a volunteer, a community's need found someone capable of addressing it. The exact origin of that connection may be interesting, but it does not need to determine the value of the information.

The more a relevant piece of information can circulate, the greater the chances that it will find someone capable of turning it into action. Distribution ceases to be merely a mechanism for capturing value and becomes part of the very impact that the infrastructure seeks to produce.

## When Information Needs to Circulate During a Crisis

The potential of this infrastructure becomes even clearer in emergency situations, when different organizations need to act simultaneously on a problem that changes rapidly. In a large-scale flood, for example, there is not just one information need. There are thousands happening at the same time: people looking for shelter, locations needing food and water, organizations looking for volunteers, teams seeking equipment, communities reporting newly affected areas, and institutions trying to identify where their resources can be most useful. Each group may possess only part of the overall picture, while the situation as a whole changes continuously.

In a situation such as the one experienced in Rio Grande do Sul in 2024, it would be possible to imagine an infrastructure in which different organizations could publish this information from their own systems and make it available to different applications. A shelter could report that it has a certain capacity. An organization could record that it needs water in a particular region. A volunteer group could report where it is operating. A company could make vehicles or equipment available. An institution could indicate that it has resources available for a particular need. This information could be consumed by different systems without all participants having to operate within the same platform.

The same reasoning could be applied to other types of disaster or emergency, such as the collapse of a dam in Brumadinho. In situations of this kind, the number of actors involved and the speed at which information changes make the ability to share and update information particularly important. The challenge is not only to produce data, but to allow different people and organizations to access the information relevant to making decisions and coordinating actions. A distributed infrastructure could function as a common layer between these different initiatives, allowing each organization to maintain its own systems without turning collaboration into dependence on a single platform.

The advantage of infrastructure of this kind appears precisely when we do not know in advance who the next participant capable of helping will be. Information published by one organization may be useful to another that was not even part of the original operation. A local group may have knowledge about a need that a larger institution is unaware of. A company may have a resource available without knowing where it is needed. A volunteer team may be near a region that needs help. When information can circulate between independent systems, new connections can emerge without anyone needing to individually coordinate each one.

In emergency situations, therefore, the value of distributed infrastructure would not lie only in making information more accessible. It would lie in increasing the ability of different actors to discover one another and coordinate their resources. The network would not need to know in advance which organization would solve a particular problem. It would simply need to allow information about the problem and information about available resources to find each other.

This may be one of the most important aspects of the proposal. We are not trying to build a system that knows everything happening in society. We are trying to create conditions under which what each participant already knows can be discovered by other participants who can make use of that information.

## From Platforms to Protocols

There is a fundamental difference between a platform and a protocol. A platform concentrates participants and defines the environment in which they interact. A protocol establishes common rules so that independent systems can interact without needing to belong to the same platform. This difference is central to the proposal because the goal would not be to replace dozens of existing systems with a new centralizing platform, but to create a common layer that allows them to continue existing independently and still collaborate.

An NGO could continue using its own system, a portal could maintain its own technology, a university could develop a research tool, and a community organization could participate using an extremely simple structure. All could be part of the same network because they would share a common language for publishing and consuming information. The infrastructure would be shared, but the applications would remain independent. This would also allow new participants to build applications without having to establish individual integrations with every existing organization.

This difference creates room for an ecosystem that does not need to be planned in advance. One developer could create a map of social opportunities, another could build a campaign search engine, another could develop a tool to connect volunteers and organizations, and another could create a system for identifying needs in regions affected by emergencies. None of these applications would need to be responsible for gathering all existing information. They could simply use the common infrastructure to find and distribute what interests them.

This also changes the economic logic of information. In many markets, data is valuable precisely because it is exclusive. Whoever possesses a particular set of information can use it to build a competitive advantage, control an audience, or capture part of the value generated from it. When we think about information of social interest, however, there are situations in which exclusivity may be exactly what reduces its potential impact. A volunteer opportunity is more useful when it finds volunteers. A fundraising campaign is more useful when it finds donors. A community's need is more useful when it finds someone capable of addressing it. The value of information lies less in who possesses it than in the ability to connect it with someone who can do something with it.

This does not mean that all information should be public, unrestricted, or indiscriminately replicated. Privacy, security, consent, and governance remain fundamental. Some information needs to have controlled access, some can be public, and some should not circulate at all. A distributed infrastructure does not eliminate these issues. It needs to establish mechanisms that allow different levels of sharing to be possible without turning control over data into control over the entire network.

The difference is that governance would not need to be concentrated on a single platform. Each organization could remain responsible for the data it produces and manages, while the network would establish common standards to allow its circulation. The goal would not be to remove ownership or responsibility for data from organizations, but to reduce the need for ownership to also be the mechanism through which information must be distributed.

This inversion is what makes the idea of a protocol particularly interesting. Instead of asking who should own the infrastructure that connects this information, we can ask what common rules would allow different organizations, applications, and communities to collaborate without having to surrender their autonomy to a single intermediary.

## A Social Layer of the Internet

The internet already has extraordinarily efficient infrastructure for transporting information. Protocols allow pages to be accessed, messages to be transmitted, files to be shared, and different systems to communicate regardless of who built them. On top of these layers, companies and organizations have created applications capable of distributing information to billions of people. What remains relatively unexplored is the possibility of using this same logic of interoperability for information whose primary purpose is not to generate audience, advertising, or revenue, but to connect a need with someone capable of addressing it.

A layer of this kind could function as a common infrastructure for information of social interest. A small local organization would not need to have the audience or technological resources of a large institution to make its information circulate. By publishing a need, an opportunity, or an initiative in its own system, it could make that information available to different applications that already have their own communities, audiences, and distribution capabilities. Reach would no longer depend exclusively on the communication capacity of whoever produced the information.

This would also change how new applications could be built. A developer interested in volunteering, for example, would not first need to convince thousands of organizations to register their opportunities on a new platform and build their own database. They could build an application on infrastructure where this information was already available. The same could happen with maps of social initiatives, search engines, emergency coordination tools, campaign discovery systems, or applications that connect available resources with existing needs.

The result would be a separation between producing information and distributing it. Today, an organization often needs to do both. It needs to carry out its work while simultaneously building the channels necessary for people to discover that work. A common infrastructure could allow each organization to focus more on what it does, while different network participants contribute their own capacity for discovery, contextualization, and distribution.

Perhaps this is one of the greatest opportunities of infrastructure of this kind. It is not merely about giving the third sector another communication channel, but about creating a structure in which thousands of organizations can collectively benefit from one another's distribution capabilities. A small initiative could produce local information, one application could contextualize it, another could find it by region, and a community could turn it into action. None of these parts would need to control the entire network for the combination to work.

In this sense, a social layer of the internet would not be a new platform for concentrating existing initiatives. It would be precisely a layer that would allow them to be discovered and connected by different platforms, applications, and communities. The infrastructure would not need to know what the final use of each piece of information would be. It would simply need to make it possible for that information to reach someone who could do something with it.

## The Goal Is Not to Centralize or Standardize the Third Sector

One of the most important things about this proposal is precisely what it does not intend to create. We do not need a large global database containing every organization, every project, and every social need. Nor do we need a company responsible for concentrating the information infrastructure of the third sector, or a single system to which all organizations must adhere.

It would also make little sense to try to standardize the third sector as if all its organizations, areas of activity, and needs were the same. An organization working in education has different needs from one working in emergencies, just as an animal protection initiative operates in a completely different way from an organization focused on employment generation. Each area has its own concepts, processes, indicators, and ways of working. A common infrastructure should not erase these differences in the name of false uniformity.

What needs to be common is only what allows different systems to communicate. Just as the internet does not need to determine how every website should function in order to allow them to communicate, a distributed infrastructure for the third sector would not need to determine how each organization should organize its work. Each participant could continue using its own systems, data models, and processes, sharing only the information that makes sense to make available and following a minimum set of conventions so that it can be discovered and used by other participants.

This distinction is fundamental. The proposal is not to create a single model for the third sector, but to create a minimum language that allows different models to coexist. One organization could participate by sharing volunteer opportunities, another could provide campaigns, another information about emergencies, and another data about community projects. It would not be necessary for all of them to work in the same way or even share the same types of information.

Each organization would remain responsible for its own data. Each application could decide which information it wants to consume and how it intends to present it. Each participant could contribute its own distribution capabilities. The infrastructure would exist precisely to connect this diversity, not to eliminate it.

The network would be larger than any of its participants because its value would not lie in concentrating all information in one place, but in allowing information produced by different organizations to find new paths of circulation. The diversity of participants would not be a problem to be solved by the infrastructure. It would be one of the main reasons for its existence.

## An Infrastructure Prepared for AI Agents

There is another reason why an open infrastructure for social information may become particularly relevant in the coming years: the evolution of artificial intelligence agents. If the first phase of the internet was built for people to find and consume information, we are beginning to build systems in which software agents can also discover information, make decisions, and execute actions on behalf of people and organizations.

In this scenario, a distributed infrastructure for the third sector could cease to be useful only for websites and applications and also become a source of context for AI agents. An agent could look for volunteer opportunities in a given region, identify organizations working in an area affected by an emergency, find campaigns related to a particular cause, or discover resources available to address a need. Instead of relying on a single platform that had previously gathered all this information, it could query different sources participating in a common infrastructure.

This changes the possible scale of this type of collaboration considerably. Today, a person interested in helping after an emergency needs to search for information across different websites, social networks, and organizations. An agent could perform this search much more broadly, relating information produced by different participants and presenting the user with the most relevant possibilities. The infrastructure would remain responsible only for making the information available and trustworthy within its own rules. Interpretation and interaction could take place on a completely different layer.

This is where initiatives such as MCP and A2A become interesting as references. MCP allows AI models to interact in a standardized way with tools and sources of context, while A2A seeks to establish a means of communication between different agents. Both point toward an important change in how software can interact with the world: instead of each application needing to know in advance every system with which it may work, common standards allow new participants to be discovered and used more dynamically.

An open infrastructure for information of social interest could occupy a complementary position in this ecosystem. It would provide agents with a layer of information about needs, opportunities, organizations, and resources that would not necessarily be concentrated on a single platform. An agent could discover that a particular community needs food, find organizations operating in that region, identify available resources, and help a person or institution decide how to contribute. Another agent could monitor the evolution of that situation and detect when the need had been met or when a new demand had emerged.

This also reinforces the importance of not turning the infrastructure into a large centralized database. AI agents are likely to become increasingly capable of working with multiple sources and services. The value of an open network lies precisely in allowing them to find information distributed across different organizations, each maintaining responsibility for its own data. Intelligence can exist at the edge of the network, while the infrastructure provides the mechanisms necessary for that intelligence to find relevant information.

There is, however, an important difference between allowing agents to discover information and allowing them to execute actions. The first step can be relatively simple: finding a campaign, a need, an opportunity, or an organization. The second requires mechanisms for authorization, trust, and accountability. An agent should not simply make a donation, make a resource available, or share sensitive information without clear rules determining what it can do and on whose behalf it is acting.

This distinction may be important for the future of the proposal. The protocol would not need to decide how artificial intelligence should work. It would simply need to create infrastructure in which agents, applications, and organizations can discover information and, when appropriate, interact with it in a controlled manner.

Perhaps this is one of the most interesting consequences of building open social infrastructure today. We do not know what applications will be built on top of it five or ten years from now. We do not know which agents will exist, which interfaces will be used, or which new models of collaboration will emerge. But we can build infrastructure that does not depend on predicting these applications.

If information is available in an open, interoperable, and distributed manner, people, applications, and agents alike will be able to find new uses for it. And, in this scenario, the network would not merely be a more efficient way to distribute information among organizations. It could become infrastructure on which a new generation of intelligent systems could discover needs, find resources, and help connect them.

This would take the original idea one step further. First, we built an internet that allows people to find information. Then we built platforms that try to organize that information for them. Now we can begin to imagine an infrastructure in which people, applications, and agents can find, share, and transform information into action without a single platform needing to control the entire process.

## A Legacy Built on a Network

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of this proposal is that its success would not need to be measured by the size of a platform, the number of users concentrated in a single place, or the economic value of a company. An open infrastructure could grow in another way: through the number of organizations that participate in it, the diversity of applications built on top of it, and, above all, the connections that become possible between people who previously could not find one another.

This type of infrastructure could also produce a different network effect from the one we know from large digital platforms. In a social network, value increases when more people are concentrated within the same environment. In an open infrastructure, value can increase precisely because participants remain independent. The more organizations publish information, the more relevant content becomes available. The more applications participate, the greater the capacity to distribute that content. The more people find opportunities to help, the greater the incentive for new organizations to participate.

This creates a dynamic in which no one needs to own the entire network for it to be useful. One organization contributes information. Another contributes distribution capacity. A developer creates an application. A university develops a new form of analysis. A community finds a new way to use that data. Each participant adds a small part of the infrastructure and, at the same time, benefits from what others have built.

Perhaps that is precisely where the most important dimension of this proposal lies: its value could be cumulative and communal. An organization that participates today would not merely be addressing a current need. It would be contributing to an infrastructure that could tomorrow be used by other organizations it does not even know. A developer who creates an application may be creating a tool that will be used by an institution in a situation that does not yet exist. Information published today may find someone months or years from now capable of turning it into action.

This is a different kind of technological legacy. It is not the legacy of a platform that grows until it becomes indispensable, nor of a company that accumulates users and data. It is the legacy of an infrastructure that becomes more useful as other people build on top of it.

Perhaps some of society's most important problems require exactly this kind of construction. Instead of concentrating information, audience, and distribution capacity among a few actors, we can create mechanisms that allow thousands of independent organizations to collectively expand their reach. Instead of competing over who owns the information, we can create conditions for it to circulate. Instead of asking which platform deserves credit for an action, we can ask whether the action was possible because the information reached those who needed it.

In the end, perhaps this is the simplest measure for an infrastructure of this kind: not how much data it possesses, how many people are inside it, or how much economic value it captures, but how many relevant connections it has made possible.

If information found someone who needed it, the network fulfilled its role. If a small organization managed to access resources it would never have found on its own, the network fulfilled its role. If, during an emergency, people, organizations, and resources managed to find one another more quickly, the network fulfilled its role.

And perhaps the greatest success would be that, after a few years, no one would know exactly who built all of it. Because truly community-driven infrastructure does not need to have a single author. It can be the accumulated result of thousands of small contributions, each seemingly limited, but capable of building something greater than any participant could build alone.

That is the idea behind a distributed infrastructure for the third sector: not to create yet another platform to concentrate social impact, but to build an open infrastructure so that the impact of thousands of initiatives can propagate far beyond the limits of each one.
