Introduction: The text frames itself as a retrospective exercise rather than literal time travel, addressing the author's 20-years-younger self based on the specific life he actually lived. The advice is explicitly limited by his family, circumstances, resources, choices, and incomplete life experience, so readers are encouraged to disregard what does not fit their reality. The author nevertheless considers concrete, experience-based examples more useful than generic advice, while acknowledging that some lessons remain unfinished because his journey is ongoing. The text emerged from reviewing two decades of successes, mistakes, and practical lessons that he believes are rarely taught directly, with the intention of presenting them honestly rather than making universally valid or marketable claims.

Mental health and well-being: The author argues that achievement should not come at the expense of mental health and recommends deliberately cultivating restorative activities and experimenting with hobbies or skills until something genuinely fits, while avoiding the opposite extreme of allowing entertainment to consume opportunities and responsibilities; he uses RPGs, guitar, friends who neglected college, and his own decision to uninstall Ultima Online as examples. He recommends balancing rest and work, being less self-critical, accepting mistakes, and using Pareto as a practical framework: roughly the first 80% of competence often requires relatively little effort, while the final 20% toward exceptional performance requires disproportionate dedication, making breadth across several useful skills often more valuable in ordinary life than extreme specialization in one. He qualifies this by advocating a T-shaped profile, with broad competence across multiple domains plus genuine depth in at least one, so breadth and specialization complement rather than exclude each other. Frustration should be treated as part of the path rather than evidence that a goal is unsuitable; his experiences with university admission, academic performance, programming, graduate degrees, and unsuccessful entrepreneurship illustrate how setbacks redirected him rather than invalidated the underlying pursuit. When uncertain about the future, he recommends examining one's history for recurring interests, fulfilling activities, natural problem-solving preferences, and lessons from failed attempts, while explicitly rejecting the idea that this implies predetermined destiny; his long-standing interests in computing and music and eventual choice of computing illustrate this reasoning. He advises judging people primarily by accumulated actions rather than promises, because behavior under inconvenience or pressure reveals more than stated intentions. Relationships that consistently drain rather than support should be reconsidered; although he has not reached what he regards as a successful outcome in this area, he distinguishes painful loneliness from chosen solitude and concludes that preserving values and self-respect is preferable to shrinking oneself to obtain acceptance. He describes several attempts at therapy that did not work well for him and found writing more therapeutic, but explicitly does not generalize this experience against professional help; instead, he argues that people should genuinely seek and test approaches until they find something effective and should not ignore mental health because of stigma. He recommends avoiding excessive alcohol and other substances that reduce lucidity, emphasizing cumulative effects on cognition, decision-making, relationships, and physical functioning rather than only immediate consequences. He also recommends humility, emotional intelligence, diplomacy, patience, and the ability to disagree without attacking people, arguing that arrogance reduces responsiveness to corrective signals and that some problems require time, context, or other people's maturation rather than force. Responsibility for children, family, or employees should be taken seriously because one's decisions can affect an entire network of dependents; he describes consciously accepting responsibility for his family after graduation. Finally, he identifies faith as personally important during periods when rational analysis did not provide sufficient answers, while distinguishing reliance on faith from abandoning responsibility or action; for him, faith provided support during the interval between doing what he could and awaiting outcomes beyond his control.

Physical health and nutrition: The author recommends establishing healthy dietary habits early, minimizing ultra-processed foods, and treating intermittent fasting cautiously rather than following trends blindly, while emphasizing personal responsibility for the consequences of neglecting one's body. He describes gaining substantially more weight than was healthy during a difficult period in which routine, anxiety, and personal problems interacted, and argues that maintaining a healthy weight is primarily about preserving energy, mobility, and long-term quality of life rather than appearance. Regular exercise, at least three times per week, is recommended in any practical form, including home weights, push-ups, or running; he acknowledges that exercise may initially feel tedious but argues that cumulative effects matter and cites his own post-pandemic sedentary habits and subsequent consequences as evidence of the cost of delayed correction. He recommends adequate hydration and gives approximately 35 ml/kg/day as a commonly used reference while explicitly noting that actual requirements vary with diet, activity, climate, and other factors; for a 100 kg person, he calculates approximately 3.5 liters and suggests using a large bottle and refill markings to make tracking easier. Sleep is treated as foundational because deprivation can simultaneously impair productivity, mood, and memory; he recommends investing in a good mattress and pillows, reducing light and noise with blackout curtains, masks, earplugs, or sound insulation, avoiding mentally demanding activity for roughly 30 minutes before bed, reducing nearby electronic stimulation, and considering a humidifier or air purifier where climate or pollution makes them relevant. He also mentions inexpensive appearance-related habits, such as sleeping with a cloth bonnet to reduce hair friction and periodically using a professional barber, framing these not as vanity but as ways of influencing one's presentation and self-esteem.

Education and personal development: The author recommends developing self-directed learning early because future knowledge requirements cannot all be anticipated or supplied by formal education, and argues that the durable benefit is partly the discipline of learning independently rather than merely acquiring particular content. He suggests using free time to develop professionally relevant skills such as investing, languages, communication, and management, noting that his own lack of English fluency caused him to miss opportunities. He advocates pursuing certifications consistently, suggesting that even one per semester from the beginning of college can establish a durable learning habit, while acknowledging that accessible online platforms now reduce historical constraints of time and money. He cautions against devoting excessive time and energy to student organizations or collective causes before establishing a personal foundation, despite valuing his own student-association experience, because earlier investment in one's capabilities can have greater long-term leverage; he uses the principle of putting on one's own oxygen mask first to express this priority. The same limitation applies to helping friends or others: he describes unrepaid loans and eventually closing a volunteer social project because maintaining paid and unpaid work simultaneously harmed his health, while clarifying that this does not invalidate generosity but means help should be given within sustainable limits of time, money, and energy. He recommends gratitude without dependence on receiving gratitude in return, arguing that people may respond negatively when someone who repeatedly helped them eventually refuses, and that assistance should therefore be understood as voluntary rather than as a mechanism for creating enforceable moral debt; even loans or expected repayments should, in his view, be emotionally released rather than continuously carried as an expectation.

Financial planning and career: The author recommends establishing an emergency fund before investing, targeting roughly three months of essential expenses in an accessible, low-risk vehicle, while acknowledging that some experts recommend six months or a year and stating his view that such larger reserves can be difficult in a Brazilian inflationary environment. The purpose of the reserve is resilience rather than return, protecting against events such as unemployment, health problems, or family emergencies and reducing the need to sell investments during unfavorable conditions or assume expensive debt. After basic financial resilience, he recommends prioritizing investment in one's own earning capacity through courses, certifications, graduate education, and continuous professional learning, particularly early in a career, because increasing future income can have greater impact than directing small initial surpluses into financial markets. Only after this foundation does he recommend focusing seriously on investments, beginning with an explicit thesis about why an asset is attractive, what evidence would confirm the thesis, and what would invalidate it; because the investor bears the eventual loss, he argues that relying completely on advisers or influencers gives away responsibility for one's own wealth. He acknowledges that concentrating wealth in one asset is risky and that diversification generally provides balance according to life stage and risk tolerance, but describes his own two-stage strategy of relatively aggressive accumulation through significant concentration in an asset for which he has strong conviction, followed later by greater diversification to protect accumulated wealth; he explicitly presents this as a personal strategy rather than a recommendation and notes that concentration requires a sound thesis, genuine conviction, and sufficient time to tolerate volatility, otherwise becoming merely a bet. He distinguishes an unrealized decline in portfolio value from a realized loss, arguing that a price decline alone should not automatically trigger selling; instead, investors should assess whether the original thesis and fundamentals remain intact, while recognizing that severe declines still represent real reductions in wealth and can be psychologically difficult. Career decisions should also be strategic: he generally recommends remaining in a job for at least a year to avoid credibility problems and to distinguish persistent structural problems from temporary difficulties, but explicitly excludes harassment, discrimination, and serious threats to physical or mental health, for which no minimum tenure justifies staying. If the environment remains unproductive after that period, he suggests beginning a search, potentially during vacation, while also warning that genuine stability and recognition can be valuable if they do not become a form of confinement; physical and mental health should remain decision criteria, and major decisions should not be made during crises. He argues that working in financial markets, including technology roles within financial institutions, can expose professionals to technological developments in automation, AI, and data infrastructure and provide useful insight into both career and personal finance; he considers his own current environment particularly educational regarding entrepreneurship, innovation, and technology. Entrepreneurship should be considered but approached with planning and financial prudence: he cites Sebrae studies as indicating that around half of Brazilian startups fail in their early years, particularly because revenue may not arrive before cash is exhausted, and therefore recommends realistic cash-flow planning, market validation before large commitments, and a personal reserve capable of supporting the founder while the business remains unsustainable; failure without those safeguards can consume resources that might otherwise support future attempts.

Mindset for the future: The author recommends building genuine professional and personal connections because opportunities often circulate among people already participating in relevant conversations, even though this can appear unfair. He describes himself as socially reserved and acknowledges concrete opportunity costs from avoiding events and introductions, arguing that networking should begin before it is needed because relationships formed during urgent need can appear transactional and generally provide less value than relationships developed without an immediate request. He emphasizes negotiation and communication because opportunities often require explicit advocacy rather than allowing work results to speak for themselves; his own difficulty selling ideas led him to recognize that a poorly presented strong idea can lose to a well-defended weaker one, and he recommends deliberate practice through direct communication feedback, studying effective negotiators, and practicing in low-risk contexts. He advises monitoring trends before they become obvious, since opportunities can lose value once broadly recognized, and considers international travel useful not merely for tourism but because exposure to foreign business models, products, and consumer behavior can reveal developments that may take longer to appear locally. Finally, he recommends taking opportunities to change environments, including moving cities or living somewhere different, because regional differences can alter the availability of opportunities and demands; he describes delaying his own departure from his hometown because what seemed like security was later understood as fear of the unknown, while difficult experiences after moving, including shared housing until financial stability was achieved, contributed substantially to his maturity. He concludes that comfort and growth often do not coexist during the same stage and that people must determine which one they need more at a given moment.

Conclusion: The author reiterates that the advice is neither a guaranteed formula nor universally applicable because it derives from specific successes, failures, circumstances, and an unfinished life, and estimates that perhaps half of it may not fit a given reader's situation. The intended common pattern is to protect physical and mental health before consequences become severe, maintain independent learning, choose relationships and jobs that contribute rather than drain, and manage time and money deliberately because apparently small impulsive decisions can have larger long-term costs. He reduces the broader lesson to the claim that the distance between one's current and potential selves is produced less by isolated major decisions than by small choices repeated consistently over a much longer period than people would prefer to wait; twenty years earlier he lacked patience to believe this, while now he still lacks patience but has greater conviction that the pattern is true.
