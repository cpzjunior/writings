# Advice I Would Give My 20-Years-Younger Self

_If I could talk to the younger version of myself, what message would I give him?_

**Summary:** I learned that a well-built life depends less on getting everything right the first time and more on taking care of what sustains the long term: health, learning, relationships, responsibility, and autonomy. I want to develop breadth without giving up depth, learn from frustrations without treating them as signs to give up, and make decisions with greater clarity, patience, and prudence. At work and in finances, I prefer to build capability and wealth before pursuing quick results, maintaining my own theses and assuming the risks of my choices. In the end, my advice is simple: know your own journey, preserve what matters, and keep moving forward, even when the path does not turn out as planned.

---

This text is not about time travel. There is no machine, portal, or multiverse here. It is a simpler and, at the same time, more honest attempt: to look back and write what I would say to myself if I could, even knowing that I cannot.

Each person has their own journey, shaped by a unique combination of family, context, resources, and decisions. That is why what follows was not written as advice for just anyone aged 15 to 20, but specifically for my 20-years-younger self, someone whose life I know in detail because it was the life I lived. That difference matters. Advice born from my experience carries the limits of my experience, and some parts of this text probably will not apply to whoever is reading it, because they stem from choices and circumstances that existed only on my path.

Even so, I decided to write and publish this because I believe concrete examples, even when they do not fit perfectly into someone else's life, are worth more than generic advice that applies to everyone and, for that very reason, says nothing specific to anyone. If something here does not make sense for your reality, ignore it without guilt. If some part fits, even partially, I consider it already worth writing. If something seems incomplete, it is because I have not finished my journey yet: I have half my life ahead of me, or so I hope.

The idea was born from a simple question: if I could talk to the younger version of myself, what message would I give him? I spent the last twenty years getting many things wrong and right, learning things that no one told me before I had to learn them in practice and that, to this day, I do not see being told. This text is the result of bringing those lessons together in one place, without the filter of what sounds beautiful or sells well and without the pretense that they are valid outside the context in which they were born.

## Mental health and well-being

Success is not worth it if it costs you your mental health. Learn to relax and find activities that take you out of your routine without turning into an escape. Treat the search for a new hobby or skill like a free gym or English class. Learn the basics, test it, put in the effort, and see whether it clicks and you develop a taste for it. If it does, great; if not, move on to the next attempt. In my case, RPG games and playing guitar still serve that purpose today.

On the other hand, there is a balance to this story that few people talk about. Just as it is not worth sacrificing everything in the name of success, it is also not worth going overboard with hobbies to the point of letting opportunities that will never come back slip away. Ecclesiastes sums it up better than I could: there is a time for everything. A time to rest and a time to work, and confusing one with the other comes at a high cost. I saw several friends repeat college courses because they played or partied too much, thinking there would always be time to make up for it later. I myself had to uninstall Ultima Online in my teenage years because I realized the real risk of letting it take over my time and hurt my grades and my future. Both your personal and professional time should be balanced, without excesses.

Be less self-critical. Striving for excellence is great, but demanding perfection from yourself is a game no one wins. Making mistakes is part of it, and progress matters more than the perfect result. A principle that helped me a lot, and that almost no one teaches, is to think in terms of Pareto: the first 80% of results in any area usually come with a relatively small share of the effort. It is the easy stretch, where learning is fast and the return is immediate. The final 20% of results, the ones that separate the good from the exceptional, require a disproportionate share of the effort, often years of almost exclusive dedication. This means that, most of the time, being reasonably good in several areas at once delivers more practical value in life than insisting on being exceptional at a single thing. No one teaches you this because culture in general celebrates the specialist, the record holder, the best in the world at what they do. But, for most people, in a real life with bills to pay and decisions to make, the combination of several average-to-good skills tends to open more doors and solve more problems than a single skill taken to the extreme.

This does not mean never developing depth in anything, which would be an error of the opposite magnitude. The model that comes closest to the right balance is that of the T-shaped professional, a concept that became known through the hiring handbook of Valve, a game company famous for documenting its own internal culture. The idea is simple: the horizontal bar of the T represents broad competence across several areas, enough to talk, collaborate, and understand the context of different teams, while the vertical stem represents real depth in at least one of those areas, enough to be a reference in it. Pareto explains why it is worth building the horizontal bar relatively quickly. The T-shaped professional explains why, even so, it is worth choosing at least one area where you will go beyond the first 80% and pursue an exceptional result. The two ideas do not compete; they complement each other: breadth across several areas, real depth in at least one.

Frustration is part of the journey, and what separates those who go farther is not having fewer frustrations, but not giving up because of them. I did not get into the university I wanted most, nor did I graduate with the highest GPA in my class, but I graduated from an institution of excellence that welcomed me in a way others did not. I was never the best programmer anywhere I worked, but I was always one of the most versatile, the person they turned to in the most critical moments or when facing the most complex problems, when it was necessary to quickly understand the situation, find a solution, and execute it carefully. My first graduate degree was an important experience, but I realized that its focus did not fit as well with the kind of work I was looking for day to day. The second, on the other hand, broadened my perspective and aligned much more closely with how I wanted to work, because it encompassed a more complete perspective on problems and solutions. I was not successful in my first attempts at entrepreneurship, but I still believe the next one will be different. In each of these cases, it would have been easy to stop right there, at the point when the result did not turn out as planned, and use the frustration as proof that that path was not for me. But none of these stumbles canceled the entire path; each one simply redirected me toward a different version of what I was seeking, often better than the original. That is how I want you to see yours: not as a sign to stop, but as part of the route that only makes sense when viewed afterward, from farther away.

When you do not know where to go next, look backward before trying to see what lies ahead. Our journey is rarely a sequence of random events. The choices we made, the opportunities that appeared, the doors that closed, the things that excited us, and even what frustrated us carry clues about who we are and about the path that may make sense for us. This does not mean that there is a predetermined destiny or that every event has a single interpretation, but that our own history contains information that we often ignore when we are anxious to decide on the next step. Ask yourself what repeats throughout your journey, which activities have always sparked your interest, in which situations you felt fulfilled, what problems you naturally enjoy solving, which experiences made you change direction, and what you learned from what did not work out. Perhaps the answer you are looking for about the future is already, in part, hidden in your past. I, for example, have liked computing since I was 10 and music since I was 15. Both have always awakened something in me and been part of my journey, but, because there are few opportunities for musicians in Brazil, I chose computing. Today, looking back, I realize that this choice did not happen by chance. It was the result of interests that were already present in me, the opportunities I encountered along the way, and the circumstances that led me to choose among different possibilities. And I still feel good about that choice today. This applies both to a teenager trying to decide which career to pursue and to an adult who realizes they need to change professions, start over, or simply take a different direction. You do not need to be certain about the entire path. Sometimes, it is enough to look carefully at the story that brought you here.

Do not trust words or promises too much, no matter how beautiful and convincing they may seem. The Sermon on the Mount contains an idea that I consider especially valuable: people should be recognized by the fruits they produce. Words can reveal intentions and promises can express good intentions, but actions reveal character. Observe what happens after the promise, especially when keeping it stops being convenient. It is easy to say what we will do when everything is under our control; it is harder to maintain the same willingness when obstacles, costs, or temptations to do otherwise arise. Over time, accumulated actions say much more about someone than any promise. So learn to listen to words, but trust the fruits.

Be careful with relationships that drain more than they build, whether romantic or friendships. I have been involved with people who did not reciprocate the care I offered, some of whom even seemed intent on hurting or harming me. I still have not achieved what I would consider a good outcome in this area of life, and perhaps you will also be at that point when you read this. Even so, there is a lesson I learned, although I have not reached the happy ending: it is better to face solitude than to make yourself smaller to fit into the reality of someone who does not care about your well-being, much less about what you feel. I make a point of separating solitude from loneliness because they are different things. Loneliness is the pain of being alone against your will, waiting for someone who may never arrive or, worse, accepting any company just to avoid feeling that emptiness. Solitude is something else: it is the space you occupy peacefully, by choice, while you wait for someone who truly deserves to share your life. The first wears you down. The second strengthens you. I learned that it is better to go through a period of solitude, preserving my values and self-respect, than to shrink myself to be accepted by someone who treats my feelings as something dispensable. I am still on this journey and do not have the final answer, but I stopped sabotaging myself and allowing other people to pull the rug out from under me. That is already real progress, even before the outcome I still hope to achieve.

I tried therapy several times, with genuine effort, without giving up after the first session. Even so, to be honest, it never worked well for me. Not because therapy is bad, far from it, but because every person is different and there is no silver bullet that works for everyone in the same way. In my case, I discovered that writing about what I am going through works in a more therapeutic way than talking to a specialist. That does not make me a better or worse person; it simply means I found the tool that works for me after trying an approach that was not as effective as I expected. I mention this not to discourage anyone from seeking professional help, but to reinforce that each person needs to make a genuine effort before dismissing a path and should keep looking until they find what actually works, even if that means moving beyond the most obvious option. What cannot happen is sweeping mental health under the rug and pretending it does not exist, nor allowing society's prejudice around the subject to be a reason not to seek any help at all.

Avoid excessive use of alcohol or anything else that takes away your lucidity. Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant, and its effect on the brain is concrete: less ability to think clearly, plan, memorize, and even move precisely. The problem is not only the next day, but also the cumulative effect over the years, which gradually erodes the same mental clarity you need to make good decisions, sustain relationships, and keep your body functioning well. You do not have to overdo it every day to feel this cost; it is enough for excess to become a habit rather than an exception. If you are trying to build something that requires focus and discipline, whether a career, a family, or any long-term project, this works against you in a silent way, because the effects rarely appear all at once; they appear slowly and, for that reason, are easy to ignore until the damage has already been done.

Cultivate humility and emotional intelligence. Arrogance precedes a fall, almost always, since those who think they are superior stop listening to the signals that would indicate when it is time to change direction. The most arrogant person in a room is usually the last to realize they are wrong and, by the time they realize it, they have already paid a much higher price than they would have paid if they had listened earlier. Diplomacy accomplishes more than direct confrontation, not because confrontation is always wrong, but simply because most people close themselves off under pressure and open up when treated with respect. Disagreeing with someone without attacking that person is a skill that can be practiced and that produces more results in the long term than winning a single argument. Patience solves things that no other strategy can solve at first; after all, some problems depend on time, context, and other people's maturity to be resolved, and no amount of haste changes that equation. Learning to wait for the right moment, without becoming complacent, is one of the rarest and most useful skills there is.

Take seriously the responsibility that life places in your care, whether it is your children, your family, or your employees. I became responsible for my family shortly after graduating and consciously accepted that role. Since then, I have understood one thing clearly: when you take on this kind of responsibility, your choices stop being only yours. They begin to require discipline, prudence, and consistency, because one mistake on your part does not affect only you; it brings down an entire network of people.

Finally, faith played an important role in my journey, especially during the most difficult moments. I do not write this to convince anyone of anything, but because it would be dishonest to tell this story and leave this part out. For me, trusting in something greater than my own plans helped me get through periods that reason alone could not explain, moments when no rational analysis offered a sufficient answer and the only thing sustaining me was the belief that there was a greater purpose behind what I was experiencing. This does not mean giving up responsibility or stopping action while waiting for everything to resolve itself. It means having something to lean on when you have already done your part and the result has not yet appeared, which, in my experience, happened more often than I would like to admit. It was in these intervals, between what I had already done and what was still beyond my control, that faith made the greatest difference.

## Physical health and nutrition

Take care of your diet as early as possible. The earlier you adopt healthy habits, the less work you will have to do to correct course later. Avoid ultra-processed foods whenever possible, prioritizing foods with less industrial intervention. Intermittent fasting can help, as long as it is done with knowledge and does not become an obsession; just because something has become the latest fitness trend does not mean it works for everyone, much less that you can blindly consume it without studying its consequences. Remember that you are the only person responsible for your own body: no one else will bear the consequences of your own laziness and neglect; perhaps the people who depend on you will, but as a result of your incapacity or absence.

I ended up gaining considerably more weight than was healthy for me during a particularly difficult period, when my routine, anxiety, and personal problems became intertwined and my body paid the bill first. Staying within a healthy weight range is not about aesthetics; it is about preserving energy, mobility, and quality of life in the long term, and about not allowing your body to become yet another obstacle on top of everything that is already difficult to manage in adult life. Our bodies have a life expectancy of more than 70 years, so we need to prepare ourselves not to suffer too much in the final stage of our lives; some people, in fact, do not even make it that far because they end up having a heart attack or stroke beforehand.

Exercise regularly, at least three times a week. It does not have to be a gym: weights at home, push-ups, a run around the block already make a difference. It may seem boring at first, but the cumulative effect over the years is significant, because our bodies have something called muscle memory. If you choose to neglect this advice, the bill comes due one way or another: today I deal with direct consequences of years of being more sedentary than I should have been after the pandemic, the kind that only appear long after the bad habit has already become established, when correcting it takes much more effort than prevention would have required.

Drink plenty of water. A frequently used reference is around 35 ml of fluids per kilogram of body weight per day, although actual needs vary depending on diet, level of physical activity, climate, and other factors. To simplify, think of a person weighing 100 kg: using the 35 ml/kg reference, that would be about 3.5 liters per day. That is approximately 14 250-ml glasses or almost 12 300-ml bottles. That is quite a lot. In a world as busy as ours, it can be difficult to remember to refill a small bottle several times throughout the day or constantly go to the kitchen to get more water. A larger bottle can make this habit much easier. A 1-liter bottle would need to be refilled only about 4 times to reach 3.5 liters; an 800-ml bottle, about 5 times. Fewer trips to the kitchen, fewer interruptions, and a simpler way to keep track of how much you have already drunk during the day. In addition, to track your daily consumption, you can mark the bottle with a pen, making a small line each time it is refilled. That way, it is easy to know how many times you have already filled it throughout the day, without having to do mental calculations. At the end of the day, just clean it with alcohol or with the material most appropriate for cleaning your bottle.

Sleep well. Sleep deprivation affects productivity, mood, and memory, all at the same time, and it is one of the few habits that, when adjusted, improves almost every other area of life without requiring additional effort in any of them. Investing in a good mattress and comfortable pillows is not a luxury; it is basic maintenance. The same goes for small adjustments that make a disproportionately large difference for their cost: a blackout curtain or a sleep mask to block light, and earplugs or some type of sound insulation for those who live in a noisy place. Avoid any activity that requires mental effort during the thirty minutes before going to sleep. If possible, move or silence electronics near the bed and replace the screen with light reading, something that relaxes you instead of keeping your attention locked in until late at night without realizing it. If the climate of the city where you live is very dry, buy a humidifier; there are some the size of a cup that are very good and turn themselves off after a while. If your city has high pollution levels, buy an air purifier.

Some simple habits also help with your appearance over time and cost almost nothing compared with the return they provide in self-esteem. Sleeping with a cloth bonnet reduces friction between your hair and the pillow, which helps reduce hair loss over the years. Having your beard done by a professional barber from time to time changes your appearance more than any expensive product can change it on its own. None of these habits are about vanity; they are about presenting yourself to the world the way you would like to be seen, without depending on luck or genetics for that.

## Education and personal development

Study on your own from an early age. The habit of self-directed learning creates an advantage that lasts a lifetime, because most of what you will need to know ten years from now does not even exist today, and no one will teach it to you in a classroom. Even when the content seems useless at the moment, what matters is not the content itself, but the discipline muscle you build by repeatedly training this habit. Those who learn how to learn on their own never again depend entirely on someone else to improve, and that is a form of freedom that few people realize they are building while they build it.

Use your free time to develop skills that genuinely matter for professional growth: investments, languages, communication, management. None of these things is usually taught in depth in formal courses, and that is precisely why mastering one of them creates a real advantage. My lack of fluency in English, for example, has already made me miss good opportunities that I only recognized after they had passed. Some doors closed before I could even fully understand what was being offered on the other side.

Pursue certifications consistently, not just from time to time. Since the beginning of college, trying to earn at least one certification per semester already puts you ahead of a large portion of people, not because the certification itself is worth that much, but because the habit of constantly pursuing credentials signals, both to yourself and to those observing from the outside, that learning is part of your routine and not an exceptional event. Today there are accessible and comprehensive online platforms for this, which eliminates much of the excuse of lack of time or money, which was more valid in the past.

Be careful about how much time and energy you give to student organizations or collective causes before you have established a solid foundation of your own. I say this with a mixture of affection and regret: I greatly enjoyed the experience I had with my student association, and the sense of community and collective purpose was real, but today I see that part of that time could have been invested directly in my future, at a stage of life when each hour still yielded proportionally more than it will later, when responsibilities accumulate. The principle is simple and well known, but rarely applied: put on your own oxygen mask before trying to help the person next to you, because helping from a fragile foundation tends to harm both sides, not just one.

The same applies to friendships and to helping others in general, and this is a lesson I learned in a harder way than I would have liked. I have lent money that was never returned, trusting people I considered close, and I even shut down a social project I chaired as a volunteer because my own health could not handle the pace of two simultaneous lines of work, one paid and the other purely out of conviction. This does not mean stopping helping people; generosity is still something I value. It means understanding that there is a right time for it and that helping before the right time, without your own reserves of time, energy, or money, can cost the helper far too much, to the point of compromising their own ability to continue helping in the future.

Be grateful, but do not expect gratitude from other people. I have witnessed many cases of people who helped someone dozens of times but, the first time they say no, whether because they gave up or for some other reason beyond their control, end up receiving back an even greater hostility than they would have received had they done nothing. Not everyone understands the value of gratitude, and that is fine; we have no right to force someone to understand it, just as no one can force us to have an obligation to help. We do it because we want to, because it is part of our set of beliefs and moral values. Therefore, if you help someone, even if it is only a loan or there is an expectation of repayment, let it go and forget about it. Do not perpetuate that debt even with God or the universe; let the harvest come as it must, without expectation or anxiety.

## Financial planning and career

Before making any investment, build an emergency fund. It is basic advice, but rare in practice, especially in Brazil, where a large portion of people live paycheck to paycheck and treat any unexpected event as a crisis. Save enough to cover about three months of essential expenses, in a place that is easily accessible and low risk, even if the return is modest compared with other options. This money is not there to generate returns; it is there to exist when you need it most, because no one knows what might happen tomorrow: a layoff, a health problem, a family emergency. Without this foundation, any unexpected event forces you to sell investments at the worst possible time or, worse, take on expensive debt just to stay afloat. The emergency fund is not the most interesting investment you will make, but it is the one that supports the possibility of all the others. Experts sometimes talk about six months or even a year, but, honestly, I think that is too difficult in a reality like ours, where inflation quickly erodes everything we have.

With that foundation in place, the next step is not necessarily to invest heavily; it is to invest in yourself. Many people take an influencer's advice too seriously and, still in their first job, already start putting everything left over into the financial markets, as if the next logical step after having any surplus money were always to invest more. But the job market is demanding, and that demand does not stop at a college degree. It continues to require continuous learning from professionals, year after year, until that person becomes senior and, in fact, a reference in their own field. Investing in courses, certifications, and graduate degrees, especially early in your career, usually yields a higher return per real invested than any financial investment, because it directly expands your future income-generating capacity, and it is that higher income that will later support larger and more consistent contributions to the market.

Only then is it worth turning your attention to investing itself, and the first step there is also not choosing where to invest; it is building a thesis. Understand why you are investing in it, what you would expect to see happen to confirm that the thesis is correct, and what would make you change your mind. This is essential for a simple reason: in the end, no investment advisor and no influencer will take responsibility for the loss you incur. The decision is yours, the money is yours, and the risk is yours alone, so relying entirely on someone else's opinion means giving up the only real control you have over your own wealth.

As a general rule, concentrating everything in a single investment is risky, no matter how strong the thesis may seem at the time, and a more balanced strategy tends to combine different asset classes according to each person's stage of life and risk tolerance. In my case, however, I think of a two-phase strategy: first, I prioritize more aggressive accumulation, concentrating a significant portion of my investments in a specific asset in which I have strong conviction, built from my own study and a long-term horizon; later, I intend to diversify more evenly, protecting the accumulated wealth. This is simply how I think about my own strategy, not an investment recommendation, and it does not mean it is the best approach for someone else. I do not consider concentration and diversification to be opposing principles, but different phases of the same strategy. The risk, however, is real: concentration only makes sense when there is a solid thesis, genuine conviction, and enough time to withstand volatility; otherwise, it is simply a bet.

A concept that makes a big difference, especially during periods of crisis, is understanding the difference between a decline in the value of an investment and an actually realized loss. As long as the position remains in the portfolio, a decline in price represents a loss in wealth, but the loss only becomes definitive when you sell the asset for less than you paid for it. This does not mean ignoring the market or pretending that steep declines do not hurt; they do. It simply means that a price decline, by itself, does not need to trigger an immediate decision. In moments of greatest turbulence, the most important thing is to keep a cool head, assess whether the original thesis still makes sense, and understand whether there has been a real change in fundamentals or merely a decline in price. If the thesis remains valid and the investment horizon remains the same, there may be no reason to sell at that moment. Selling in panic can turn a temporary loss in the value of the position into an actually realized loss, also eliminating the possibility of recovering that investment.

Be strategic in choosing jobs. Not every job is worth the effort it demands. Even so, a practical rule I adopted is to stay in a job for at least one year, even when it is far from ideal, because very quick exits hurt your credibility in the market and rarely give you enough time to assess whether the problem is the environment or a passing phase. This rule applies to ordinary discomfort, boredom, stagnation, a difficult boss, and unproductive tasks, not to environments that put your mental or physical health at real risk, such as harassment or discrimination. In those cases, no minimum amount of time justifies staying, and leaving as soon as possible is the right decision, without exception. If, after that period, the environment still does not develop you, leaving is usually the right decision, and a practical time to start looking for the next opportunity is during vacation, a period that allows you to rest, regain the energy to update your résumé and professional networks, and create real space for an active search without the strain of doing it alongside your work routine. On the other hand, if a company recognizes your value and offers genuine stability, it is worth thinking twice before giving that up, as long as that stability does not become a prison disguised as comfort. Use your physical and mental health as a decision criterion, always, and make that decision only with a clear head, never in critical moments. At the end of the day, a company can replace you at any time. You cannot replace your own life.

Getting involved with the financial market, even if your field is not finance, opens doors. Few people realize it, but the financial market is currently one of the technological vanguard sectors in Brazil, competing directly with major technology companies for the best professionals and investing heavily in automation, artificial intelligence, and data infrastructure, because speed and precision in this sector translate directly into money. Working with technology within a financial institution, for example, is a practical way to be at the forefront of this transformation and understand how the system works from the inside, using that knowledge to your advantage both in your career and in your own financial decisions. In my case, the environment where I work today has taught me more about entrepreneurship, innovation, and technology than any previous job and recognizes that work fairly.

Consider entrepreneurship, but with prudence and a well-defined plan before taking the first step. I am not trying to discourage anyone, quite the opposite: when it works, the sense of accomplishment is enormous, and few professional experiences compare with seeing something you built from scratch actually work. The point is that starting a business without preparation means taking on a greater risk than most people imagine. According to studies by Sebrae, around half of Brazilian startups do not survive their first years of operation, mainly because of difficulties generating enough revenue before their cash runs out. This is not a reason to give up on the idea; it is a reason to enter it with a realistic cash-flow plan, market validation carried out before investing time and money on a large scale, and a personal reserve that can support you while the business is not yet self-sustaining, the same emergency fund mentioned at the beginning of this section, now serving a second purpose. When it goes wrong without this preparation, the feeling is that you have spent time and wealth that could have been used to increase the chances of success in the next attempt, instead of compromising the foundation on which that next attempt could have been built.

## Mindset for the future

Build genuine connections. The right networking opens doors that talent alone does not, and this bothers many people because it seems unfair, but that is how the world works in practice: opportunities circulate first among those who are already part of the conversation. I have always been more socially reserved, avoided events, preferred to solve everything on my own, and I know that this cost me concrete opportunities along the way, not abstract ones. Meetings I did not attend, introductions I did not make, people I met too late for the moment when they could have made a difference. It is something I am still working on, and I recommend that you start before you need it, because networking built when you are in need almost always sounds self-interested and yields less than networking built in advance, without asking for anything in return.

Learn to negotiate and communicate well. Knowing how to express an idea clearly and defend a point of view changes the course of both your career and personal life, because most opportunities do not come to you; you have to argue for them. I have historically struggled to sell ideas, always trusting that the results of the work would speak for themselves, and I learned, more than once, that this is rarely enough. A good idea that is poorly presented loses to a mediocre idea that is well defended in most real-world meeting rooms. Improving this remains an ongoing goal, not something that is solved once and for all, and it involves deliberate practice: asking for direct feedback on how you communicate, observing who negotiates well and understanding what that person does differently, and practicing in low-risk situations before you need to negotiate something that really matters.

Pay attention to trends before they become obvious to everyone. Spotting an opportunity slightly before most people is a real advantage because, when everyone can already see the same opportunity, it has already lost much of its value. Traveling, especially abroad, helps a lot with this, not because of tourism itself, but because it exposes you to business models, products, and consumer behaviors that will still take time to reach here, and arriving early with that information is worth more than it seems.

Take advantage of opportunities for change when they appear. Moving to another city, living somewhere different expands possibilities in a way that is difficult to replicate by staying put, because customs, opportunities, and demands vary from region to region, and what is impossible in one place is sometimes trivial in another, just a few hours away. I took longer than I should have to leave my hometown, holding on to a comfort zone that, at the time, seemed like security and that I now recognize was simply fear of the unknown in disguise. The changes I made afterward, even with real difficulties at the beginning, such as sharing housing until I became financially stable in a new place, contributed more to my maturity than any period of comfort I would have had by staying where I was. Comfort and growth rarely coexist in the same stage of life, and at each moment you need to choose which of the two you need more.

## To conclude

None of this advice is a guaranteed formula, and I already said that at the very beginning: these are real experiences, with successes and mistakes that only make sense because they actually happened in my life, not in anyone else's. Perhaps half of what I wrote here will not apply to your current situation, and that is fine, because that was never the point. What I hope remains, even where the details do not fit, is the pattern behind them: take care of your body before it sends you the bill, take care of your mind without being ashamed of it, learn without depending on anyone to keep learning, choose relationships and jobs that add rather than drain, and spend time and money with the same clear head, knowing that hasty decisions cost more than they seem to at the moment they are made.

If I could sum it all up in a single sentence, it would be this: the distance between who you are today and who you can become is not made up of isolated major decisions, but of the sum of small choices, repeated consistently, over much more time than anyone would like to wait. Twenty years ago, I did not have the patience to believe that. Today, I still lack patience, but I already have the conviction that it is true.
