# What Senna and Churchill Can Teach Us About Resilience

_Resilience is not about never giving up, but about adapting to adversity without losing sight of what matters._

**Summary:** I argue that resilience is not simply about never giving up, but about knowing what is worth preserving when reality stops cooperating. Senna and Churchill show that conviction does not mean insisting on the same path: we need to prepare, adapt our means, recognize the limits of our control, and seek help when necessary. We can lose a battle, change strategy, or admit a mistake without abandoning what truly matters. Ultimately, being resilient means continuing to work while there is still something important to build, even without any guarantee that the outcome will come.

---

Ayrton Senna was my childhood hero. I was born in 1990 and, therefore, did not follow his entire career. Even so, I grew up surrounded by the images, stories, and memories of that Brazilian driver who had become one of the country's greatest idols. Like so many children of my generation, I built an almost mythical image of Senna, of that driver who seemed capable of doing things that others simply could not. To this day, watching documentaries about him moves me. Not only because of the victories, overtakes, or footage of his races, but because of the combination of talent, intensity, discipline, and, above all, the way he faced difficulties.

Strangely, I feel something very similar when I watch Winston Churchill's speeches during World War II. Perhaps because, over the years, I have watched so many documentaries about the war, the rise of Nazi Germany, the fall of France, Dunkirk, and the possibility of a British invasion. Knowing the context makes those words even more impressive. Churchill was not speaking at a time of tranquility. He was speaking when circumstances were terrible, when a British defeat seemed possible, and when the consequences could have been catastrophic. His words take on a different force when we remember that, at that moment, he did not know how that story would end.

There is something about these two men that interests me more than their achievements. I do not believe Senna or Churchill should be treated as saints or as absolute examples of virtue. Both had flaws, controversies, mistakes, and behaviors that can and should be criticized. We do not need to turn a person into a moral hero to recognize an extraordinary characteristic they possess. Perhaps it is precisely because they were real, contradictory, and imperfect men that the two are such interesting examples.

What interests me is conviction. The ability to look at an adverse circumstance, recognize its magnitude, and still decide to continue. Not because victory is guaranteed. Not because suffering is insignificant. Not because believing is enough to transform reality. But because there is something that, for us, is greater than adversity itself.

There is, however, an important difference between conviction and obstinacy. Continuing is not necessarily a virtue. Sometimes we need to change strategy, acknowledge a mistake, ask for help, abandon a path, or even give up a battle. A resilient person is not someone who refuses to change. It is someone who can distinguish what truly matters from what is merely one of the possible ways of getting there.

Perhaps that is where resilience begins: not in the ability to endure any suffering, but in the ability to know what is worth enduring.

## Life Is Not Fair

There is an idea deeply present in our culture that, if we do everything correctly, things should work out. We study, work, prepare, make plans, and expect effort to produce some proportional result. There is almost a kind of implicit contract in this way of thinking: if we do our part, reality should do its part. Sometimes that happens. Often it does not. Extremely competent people fail. Honest people are treated unfairly. Disciplined people miss opportunities. Carefully planned projects go wrong. Relationships end despite all the effort invested. Companies go bankrupt. Careers do not unfold as we imagined. People get sick, accidents happen, and, at times, events completely outside our control permanently alter the course of a life.

Perhaps this is one of the hardest truths to accept as adults: reality has no obligation to be fair to us. The world does not know our efforts, does not keep track of our good intentions, and does not distribute outcomes according to what we believe we deserve. This does not mean that effort, competence, discipline, or character are irrelevant. They matter enormously. It simply means that there is no guaranteed relationship between what we do and what we receive in return. We can do everything right and still lose. We can do everything within our power and discover, in the end, that it was not enough.

This realization can produce two very different responses. The first is resignation. If the world is unfair and we cannot control everything, then perhaps it is not worth fighting. The second is harder: accepting reality without allowing it to determine who we are or what we are willing to defend. Recognizing that we do not control the outcome does not mean giving up responsibility for our response. On the contrary. Perhaps it is precisely when we understand the limits of our control that we begin to exercise better control over what remains within our power.

It is important, however, not to confuse resilience with passivity. A resilient person is not someone who simply accepts everything that happens to them and silently endures any suffering. Resilience is also not a kind of cult of pain, as though suffering were, in itself, a virtue. There are situations in which we need to change strategy, abandon a path, admit a mistake, or recognize that a particular battle is no longer worth the price we are paying. Persisting indefinitely in the same direction, regardless of the evidence, may simply be obstinacy.

Resilience lies somewhere else. It lies in the ability to distinguish what is essential from what is circumstantial. We can lose a battle without abandoning what we are trying to build. We can change paths without abandoning the destination. We can recognize that we were wrong about the means without concluding that the objective has lost its value. A resilient person is not necessarily someone who never changes direction, but someone who knows what they are unwilling to abandon.

This distinction between objective and path is important because, when circumstances become adverse, we often do not have only two options: continue or give up. There is a third possibility: adapt the way we are trying to get there. We can seek help, change strategy, acquire a new skill, find other resources, or accept that what worked before has stopped working. Resilience does not require loyalty to the original path. It requires enough clarity about the destination to know when the path needs to change.

This is where conviction becomes fundamental. To continue in the face of adversity, we need some reason that predates it and is stronger than it. If our willingness to fight depends on favorable conditions, any significant change in circumstances can destroy our will. If, on the other hand, there is something we consider deeply valuable, we can accept that the path will be harder than we imagined without concluding that what we seek is no longer worth pursuing.

This also changes the way we see suffering. Suffering does not become good simply because we are suffering. Pain is not proof of virtue, and difficulty is not evidence that we are on the right path. What can give suffering meaning is what we choose to do with it and, above all, what we are willing to endure it for. There is an enormous difference between suffering because we have found no alternative and accepting suffering as part of the price of something we consider greater.

Therefore, perhaps the most important question is not “how much suffering can I endure?” That question still places suffering at the center. The question that really matters is “what is important enough to me that I am willing to endure suffering to defend it?” This is a much harder question because it forces us to identify our own convictions. And it is also a question that inevitably arises when circumstances stop cooperating.

This is when resilience ceases to be a beautiful word and becomes a concrete necessity. When planning fails, when talent is not enough, when effort does not produce the expected result, when the body reaches its limit, or when reality seems to have decided against us, something needs to remain. It may be a person, a family, a responsibility, a profession, a faith, a project, or simply the conviction that there is still something worth fighting for.

Senna and Churchill interest me precisely because their stories allow us to observe this dynamic in completely different situations. One inside a Formula 1 car, trying to extract performance from a machine that does not provide ideal conditions. The other facing a threatened nation, trying to sustain a political decision when military circumstances offered little reason for optimism. There is obviously no comparison possible between a war and a race. The consequences, responsibilities, and moral dimensions of the two situations are incomparable. The comparison lies elsewhere: in the way two men responded when what they faced became much more difficult than they had expected.

Neither of them completely controlled what was happening. Both, however, had some ability to decide what to do next. They could prepare, adapt, seek resources, change strategy, and, when necessary, bear the cost of continuing. And perhaps this is the first foundation of resilience: not the certainty that we will win, but the conviction that it is still worth fighting.

## Blood, Toil, Tears, and Work

On May 13, 1940, just days after taking office as prime minister, Winston Churchill delivered his first speech to Parliament in a desperate military situation. Germany had begun its offensive in the West, France was being defeated, and the British army was in an extremely vulnerable position. The possibility of a British defeat was not a distant hypothesis. It was a concrete possibility. Churchill took over the government precisely when Britain's future was deeply uncertain.

Faced with that scenario, it would have been understandable for a new prime minister to try to reassure the country. He could have minimized the risks, emphasized the possibilities of victory, or simply asked for trust. Churchill did something very different. He chose to tell the British what awaited them: “I have nothing to offer but blood, toil, tears and sweat.” There was no promise of comfort. There was no guarantee that the sacrifice would be small. There was only recognition of the price the situation demanded.

In that same speech, Churchill also made clear what his policy would be: to wage war “by land, sea and air, with all our might and with all the strength that God can give us.” And when he presented the objective, he was equally direct: “Victory. Victory at all costs, victory in spite of all terror, victory however long and hard the road may be.” The most important word may not even be “victory,” but “in spite.” The objective did not depend on favorable circumstances. It had to be pursued despite them.

A few weeks later, on June 4, Churchill would return to Parliament after the evacuation from Dunkirk. The operation had succeeded in evacuating hundreds of thousands of British and Allied soldiers from the continent, but it had not been a military victory. France was falling, much of the British equipment had been abandoned, and the possibility of an invasion of the British Isles hung over the country. Even so, Churchill refused to treat the withdrawal as a reason for surrender, stating in his speech, in words that decades later would be immortalized at the opening of Iron Maiden's Aces High: “We shall fight on the beaches. We shall fight on the landing grounds. We shall fight in the fields and in the streets. We shall fight in the hills. We shall never surrender.”

But the speech did not end there. Churchill took the idea even further. Even if Britain were invaded and much of its territory occupied, resistance would continue. And, if necessary, the struggle would continue beyond the Atlantic, until the “New World,” with all its strength and power, came to the aid of the Old World. The message was clear: defeat in a battle, loss of territory, or even occupation of the island would not be enough to end the fight.

This makes the speech particularly interesting in relation to the idea of resilience. Churchill was not claiming that Britain would be able to control circumstances. He was claiming that circumstances alone would not determine the moment when resistance would end. If one strategy failed, there would be another. If territory were lost, the fight would continue somewhere else. If British resources ran out, it would be necessary to seek help. The objective remained; the means could change.

What stands out in these speeches is not only the rhetorical force. Churchill was not trying to convince the British that adversity was small. He was doing the opposite: recognizing its magnitude and, nevertheless, asserting that there was something that needed to be preserved. But there is another aspect of his wartime leadership that helps us better understand this idea. Churchill did not confuse conviction with loyalty to a specific method.

For decades, he had been a fierce critic of Soviet communism. Even so, when Hitler invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, Churchill immediately supported cooperation with Stalin against Nazi Germany. This did not mean he had changed his opinion of the Soviet regime. The alliance was an instrument for confronting a threat he considered more urgent.

Something similar happened in the relationship with the United States. Britain needed weapons, ships, financial resources, and supplies to continue the war. Churchill turned to Franklin Roosevelt and, in September 1940, the two countries reached an agreement that transferred fifty American destroyers to the British in exchange for rights to bases in the British Atlantic and Caribbean. In 1941, Britain's financial situation had deteriorated further, and Lend-Lease allowed the United States to provide war materiel to the British without requiring immediate payment.

The lesson here is not that Churchill always chose his allies or strategies correctly. It is something else: he was willing to change the means when the existing means were insufficient to achieve what he considered essential.

This may seem like a small distinction, but it has important consequences outside politics and war. Someone building a career may discover that the specialization initially chosen does not offer the expected opportunities. It may be necessary to learn another skill, change companies, move to another field, or temporarily accept a position different from the one imagined. Continuing to believe in a company does not mean continuing to sell exactly the same product, to the same customers, and in the same way.

The same applies to a personal project. We may spend months or years trying to achieve a particular result and, at some point, realize that the original strategy was wrong. In that case, abandoning the method does not necessarily mean abandoning what we wanted to build. It may be precisely the condition required to continue. This is the difference between obstinacy and conviction. Obstinacy says, “I will keep doing this.” Conviction says, “I will keep pursuing that.” The first can trap us in our own mistake. The second allows us to change strategy without turning every change into a personal defeat.

It is relatively easy to defend our convictions when everything is working. When we have resources, recognition, support, and results, almost any conviction seems firm. The real test begins when defending it starts to cost something: when losses, uncertainty, exhaustion, failure, or the need to admit that what we were doing does not work arise.

It is important, however, not to turn Churchill into a morally perfect figure in order to recognize the strength of these moments. His political career was marked by controversial positions, especially regarding the British Empire, as well as mistakes and decisions that continue to be the subject of legitimate criticism. His leadership during the war does not erase these contradictions.

The point here is more specific. Faced with the threat of a defeat that could mean the loss of British independence, Churchill did not consider surrender an acceptable alternative. At the same time, he did not interpret this conviction as an obligation to remain tied to a single strategy. He could negotiate, form unlikely alliances, ask for help, accept external resources, and modify his approach as circumstances changed.

It is this combination that makes the example useful in everyday life. We will probably never have to decide the fate of a nation. But we may spend years trying to build a career, start a company, change professions, support a family, or get a project off the ground. In all these cases, moments will come when reality does not respond as we expected.

The market may reject our product. A promotion may not happen. A company may fail. A project may consume more time and money than we imagined. A career may take a different direction from the one planned. We may discover that a strategy that worked for years has stopped working.

In these moments, resilience does not mean insisting on the original plan until exhaustion. It means identifying what we are really trying to build and separating that from the way we imagined getting there. If the objective still makes sense, perhaps it is time to change the path. If the path still makes sense, perhaps it is time to persevere. And if we discover that the objective itself has lost its meaning, abandoning it may be a demonstration of lucidity, not weakness.

Conviction, therefore, does not serve to make us incapable of changing. It serves to give us a criterion for deciding what to change.

## Senna and Preparing for Adversity

Senna offers a much more personal version of the same idea. Churchill had to decide how a nation would respond to circumstances he could not control. Senna had to do so inside a Formula 1 car. He could not control the rain, the behavior of his competitors, track conditions, or the mechanical reliability of the equipment. What he could control was how prepared he would be when those things happened.

Perhaps that is precisely why his career is so interesting when we think about resilience. Senna's public image is that of almost supernatural talent, but behind that talent was an obsessive relationship with preparation, discipline, and improvement.

Rain is one of the best examples. Senna realized early on that, if he could not eliminate a difficulty, he would need to learn to deal with it better than others. A wet track was not something he could control. What he could do was prepare for when the rain came. Over time, what could have remained a vulnerability became one of his greatest competitive advantages.

The same logic appeared in his physical preparation. In his very first year in Formula 1, in 1984, Senna realized that his body was not yet prepared for the demands of the category. From then on, his physical preparation became increasingly rigorous. Talent made it possible to control the car, but the body had to be prepared to withstand the forces, duration, and concentration required by a race.

This characteristic would appear repeatedly throughout his career. When he encountered a limitation, Senna sought to determine whether it could be worked on. If his body could not withstand a particular load, he trained his body. If rain was a difficulty, he trained in the rain. If the equipment had limitations, he sought to understand how to extract the maximum from it. Adversity ceased to be merely something to endure and became a problem to study.

This also helps us understand the beginning of his Formula 1 career. In 1984, Toleman was not a team capable of regularly competing for victories. Senna started with limited equipment and had to build his reputation from it. The car established concrete limits, but it did not completely determine what he would be capable of doing.

Perhaps this is one of the most concrete forms of resilience: not waiting for ideal conditions to start acting. First do more with the resources available. Then turn that performance into an opportunity to obtain better resources. Material reality establishes constraints, but it does not have to predetermine the size of what we can build.

The logic applies far beyond the racetrack. At the beginning of a career, we almost never have the position, influence, knowledge, or resources we would like to have. Someone starting a business may begin with little capital, a small team, an imperfect product, and customers who do not yet trust the company. Someone building a career often has to demonstrate competence before receiving authority and assume responsibilities before having the corresponding title. Waiting for ideal conditions can mean waiting indefinitely.

This is the context in which the 1991 Brazilian Grand Prix takes on a meaning greater than that of a simple victory. Senna had already tried to win at home several times, but victory continued to elude him. In 1990, he finally seemed to have found the perfect opportunity: he started from pole and was leading the race when an incident with backmarker Satoru Nakajima damaged the front wing of his McLaren. Senna had to make an additional pit stop and returned to the track far behind the leaders, finishing the race in third. The long-awaited home victory continued to elude him.

In 1991, Senna returned to Interlagos as a two-time world champion. He again started from pole, built a comfortable lead, and this time seemed on his way to achieving what he had pursued for years.

Then the car began to develop problems. First, Senna lost fourth gear. Then fifth. In the final laps, only sixth remained. For someone unfamiliar with a Formula 1 car, this may seem simply like a loss of performance. The problem was much greater. The gears allow the engine's revs to be matched to the car's speed. In a slow corner, for example, the driver downshifts to a lower gear to slow the car and then accelerates again strongly on the exit. Gear selection is part of the very way the car is driven.

With only sixth gear, Senna lost that possibility. He had to take even slow corners using a gear ratio that would normally be unsuitable for them. If he slowed down too much, the engine's revs could drop to the point of stalling. If he maintained enough speed to keep the revs up, he would enter the corners too quickly and have much less margin to control the car.

This completely changed the driving technique. Senna had to brake, steer, and accelerate differently, trying to keep the engine running without compromising control of the car. Each corner required a new balance between speed, revs, and grip. At the same time, the car became harder to drive and the advantage he had built began to disappear.

The problem, therefore, was not simply that the car was slower. One of the main tools Senna had for controlling the car's behavior had stopped working. The machine no longer responded as it should, and he had to adapt his technique to its new limitations.

Meanwhile, Riccardo Patrese was rapidly closing in. Senna's lead, which had reached around forty seconds, fell to just a few seconds. What had seemed like a straightforward victory once again became a battle.

And then it started to rain. The rain was brief, but it made it harder for Patrese to close the gap and helped Senna preserve the lead. The driver who had spent years turning wet tracks into one of his greatest specialties now found himself in circumstances that unexpectedly worked in his favor.

There is something almost poetic about this. For years, Senna had prepared himself to master one of the conditions that could most hinder a driver. That day, when the car was already deeply compromised and his body was beginning to reach its limit, the rain that could so often have been an adversity became a small source of help.

Senna reached the final lap with a minimal lead. He had to keep the engine running, control a car with only one available gear, and endure the accumulated physical effort. When he finally crossed the finish line, he won by 2.9 seconds. The celebration became one of the best-known images of his career. Senna shouted over the radio and practically collapsed from exhaustion. Cramps and muscle spasms made movement difficult, and he needed help getting out of the car. On the podium, he could barely lift the trophy he had pursued for so many years.

It is difficult to reduce that scene to “Senna never gave up.” That is true, but it is insufficient. The car was having problems, his lead had almost completely disappeared, and his body was at its limit. He had to continue working within circumstances that were no longer under his control.

And there is a particularly interesting irony in this story. Senna had spent years preparing himself to master precisely one of the conditions that could hinder a driver. At Interlagos, when everything seemed to be slipping from his hands, the rain appeared and ended up helping preserve the victory he had pursued for so long.

We can explain this through physics, strategy, and the circumstances of the race. There is no need to turn a weather phenomenon into a supernatural intervention. But, knowing who Senna was, it is difficult not to see a symbolic dimension in this coincidence as well.

Senna was deeply Christian. He read the Bible, spoke openly about God, and interpreted his own life through a Christian worldview. His faith did not guarantee that he would win, did not prevent the car from developing problems, and did not replace training or competence. But it could provide an answer to what was beyond his control: a reason to continue even when the outcome no longer depended entirely on him.

Perhaps that is why the rain at Interlagos has such powerful symbolic force. Senna had done everything within his power. He had prepared his body, developed his skill, studied the equipment, and built a career capable of putting him in that position. After that, something he could not control happened. It started to rain.

Perhaps it was simply coincidence. Perhaps, for a deeply religious man, it meant something more. We have no way of knowing. What we can observe is the boundary between preparation and circumstance: we do what is within our power, but we never completely control the outcome.

The same distinction appears in professional life. We can study for a job and still not be selected. We can build a company and discover that the market has changed. We can work for years toward a promotion that never comes. We can execute a project correctly and still see it fail because of factors beyond our control. Preparation increases our ability to respond to these situations, but it does not turn uncertainty into a guarantee.

And that is precisely where Senna's story becomes more interesting. His response to adversity was not simply to endure more suffering. It was to prepare before it appeared and, when it did appear, adapt to what was still possible to do.

This interpretation also allows us to talk about Senna without turning him into a saint. His same intensity produced behaviors that deserve criticism. His rivalry with Alain Prost repeatedly crossed the boundaries of healthy competition. In 1989, the collision between the two at Suzuka ended in a controversy involving Senna's disqualification. In 1990, again at Suzuka, Senna deliberately did not yield space at the first corner, knowing that a collision would secure the championship.

His determination was extraordinary, but the way he expressed it was not always admirable. This matters because resilience is not an isolated virtue capable of turning someone into a morally superior person. Determination can produce greatness, but also obstinacy. Conviction can sustain someone in the face of adversity, but it can also cause them to persist when they should retreat.

Senna was a contradictory man. He was deeply religious and extremely competitive. He could demonstrate generosity and humility, but he could also be aggressive and ruthless. We do not need to erase these contradictions to recognize what was extraordinary about him. The interest lies precisely in observing how a real person, with virtues and flaws, developed an exceptional capacity for preparation, adaptation, and persistence.

Perhaps this is the best way to understand his trajectory. The physical preparation, mastery of rain, years at Toleman, inferior equipment, defeats at Interlagos, and 1991 victory are not isolated stories. In different situations, the same pattern appears: identify a limitation, work on what can be improved, and adapt when something happens that cannot be controlled.

It is a lesson that applies to anyone trying to build something. We will not always have the best equipment, the best resources, the necessary knowledge, or ideal conditions. We can, however, work to increase our capacity before difficulty appears. And, when it does appear, we can decide what is still within our reach.

This does not mean insisting indefinitely. Some difficulties need to be worked around. Others require help. Some force us to abandon a strategy that has stopped working. And some simply need to be accepted. Senna's lesson is not to suffer more than others, but to take seriously what we want to build and prepare ourselves for the difficulties that will inevitably appear along the way.

Faith enters this story at another level. It does not replace preparation, competence, or adaptation. It can, however, provide a conviction for what remains when these tools reach their limits. Preparation increases our possibilities; adaptation allows us to respond to circumstances; faith, for those who have it, can give meaning to what we cannot control.

Perhaps the rain helped. Perhaps it was simply chance. Perhaps, for a deeply Christian man, it was something more. We cannot know. But we can understand what came before it: Senna did what was within his power, adapted when circumstances changed, and continued while there was still something he could do.

This is a more interesting view of resilience than the image of someone who simply never gives up. Resilience does not mean controlling everything, always winning, or always being right. It means preparing for what can be anticipated, recognizing what cannot be controlled, adapting the means when circumstances change, and continuing to work while there is still something important that can be done.

And perhaps that is exactly why Interlagos 1991 remains so moving. Senna spent years trying to win at home. That day, the car began to fail, the lead disappeared, and his body reached its limit. He no longer controlled all the conditions of the race. But he still controlled how he would respond to them. And he kept driving.

## What Remains When Circumstances Are Adverse?

Perhaps that is why Senna and Churchill are such interesting examples of resilience. Not because they were perfect men, nor because they were always right. Both had mistakes, contradictions, and behaviors that can be criticized. What matters here is something more specific: in moments of enormous adversity, both managed to sustain a conviction without confusing it with an obligation to always follow the same path.

Churchill could not control the German army, the fall of France, or the possibility of an invasion. Senna could not control the rain, his competitors, or the car's reliability. What was within the reach of both was the response: prepare, adapt, seek resources, change strategy, and continue working while the outcome remained uncertain.

This distinction is important because resilience does not mean enduring any suffering or insisting indefinitely. It means knowing what we are trying to preserve or build and recognizing that the means may need to change. We can abandon a strategy without abandoning an objective. We can admit a mistake without abandoning an ambition. We can ask for help without abandoning our responsibility. We can even change direction without losing what truly matters.

It is relatively easy to have convictions when circumstances cooperate. The real test begins when comfort disappears, plans fail, and we discover that effort and competence do not establish a contract with reality. The world does not owe us an outcome proportional to what we have done. Even so, we need to decide what we will do with what remains within our control.

This choice appears far beyond wars and racetracks. It is present when someone decides to start a business without knowing whether the company will survive, chooses a difficult career without any guarantee of recognition, persists with a project after a failure, or has to rebuild their trajectory after a decision that did not work. In all these cases, resilience is not blindly insisting on the original plan. It is knowing what we want to build, having the flexibility to change the means, and being willing to continue working while the outcome remains uncertain.

Churchill did not know whether he would win the war. Senna did not know whether he would make it to the end of Interlagos. Neither controlled the outcome. But both had a reason to continue.

Perhaps that is what being resilient really means: not believing that adversity will be small, nor that effort will necessarily be rewarded. It is knowing what is worth building and being willing to keep trying when the path changes, resources diminish, and certainty disappears.
