I have a certain addiction to watching System Design videos, particularly those explaining how large companies solved problems at scale. Seeing an architecture after its major decisions have been made makes the components and their purposes appear clear and often obvious, whereas designing from scratch is much harder because most problems admit many technically reasonable solutions. Requirements such as availability, volume, latency, cost, security, compliance, and evolvability should narrow the possibilities, but often leave dozens of viable alternatives. In practice, I tend to rely on AWS services and patterns I already know because familiarity makes risks and limitations easier to estimate, even if this introduces some conservatism and reduces experimentation. New technologies, clouds, or architectural approaches can be technically attractive while creating operational difficulty, requirement gaps, or technical debt, and responsibility for those consequences remains with the architect. The consequences of architectural choices frequently emerge months or years later, after data, integrations, and dependencies make supposedly reversible decisions expensive to change. The central problem, therefore, is not merely recognizing that alternatives to defaults exist, but determining which alternatives actually deserve consideration. Modern technology makes implementation increasingly easy: cloud turns infrastructure into services, SaaS turns capabilities into APIs, managed platforms remove operational work, and AI lowers the cost of implementation and experimentation. This creates a paradox in which building solutions becomes easier without making the decision about which solution to build equally easier. The relevant architectural challenge has consequently shifted toward choosing among alternatives rather than merely making one alternative possible.

Complexity has not disappeared; it has moved to another layer. Abstraction allows engineers to stop worrying about implementation details, and cloud platforms have extended this principle by turning servers, storage, networking, replication, capacity, and disaster recovery into consumable services. This drastically reduces implementation effort, but reducing complexity in one layer does not necessarily reduce system-wide complexity: instead of implementing each component, architects must understand how many independently consumable components interact, including behaviors that may be difficult to predict when composed. The knowledge required from architects therefore changes from detailed implementation knowledge toward understanding the properties that matter for decisions, such as guarantees, limits, failure models, costs, and interactions between components.

The paradox of choice applies strongly to solution architecture because the abundance of services and architectural combinations can make decision-making harder rather than easier. Historically, architecture was constrained by what could realistically be built with available resources; increasingly, the problem is choosing among many technologies capable of satisfying the same requirement. Decisions also interact: choosing a database influences data modeling, integration, backup, observability, operational costs, and subsequent technology choices, causing the decision space to expand as alternatives are combined. Experience is therefore valuable partly because it enables architects to eliminate possibilities without deeply evaluating every one. Familiar AWS technologies serve this purpose by reducing the decision space and allowing attention to focus on consequential decisions, but this heuristic becomes problematic if familiarity turns into an unconscious reflex that produces architectural debt. The challenge is thus not to know every possible alternative, but to develop criteria that allow irrelevant alternatives to be discarded deliberately and efficiently.

Abstraction can conceal not only implementation details but also dependencies and common points of failure. A diagram containing S3, Lambda, or DynamoDB represents their visible responsibilities, but does not necessarily expose the infrastructure, shared resources, replication mechanisms, limits, or dependencies beneath those abstractions. This does not mean architects need to understand every internal mechanism, but they must recognize when hidden properties become relevant to decisions involving availability, disaster recovery, or multi-cloud strategies. Systems that appear distributed across services, regions, or clouds may still depend on common underlying components, and different providers may themselves share suppliers or technological dependencies. The architectural implication is that surface-level diversity does not guarantee independence, and abstraction is useful only when the hidden details being ignored remain irrelevant to the decision. The architect therefore needs sufficient knowledge of an abstraction to recognize when its limitations or dependencies can materially alter the architecture.

Artificial intelligence intensifies the same phenomenon because it further reduces the cost of implementation and experimentation while expanding the solution space. The growing number of models, providers, versions, capabilities, costs, latencies, context limits, and behavioral differences makes even apparently simple model-selection decisions increasingly complex. AI solutions also introduce additional architectural choices involving inference strategies, retrieval, vector databases, prompting, tools, agents, and integration patterns, multiplying the possible compositions. The cost of experimentation can now fall dramatically, allowing prototypes that once required days to be produced in hours, but the cost of understanding their architectural consequences does not necessarily fall at the same rate. This creates the possibility of reaching implementation before understanding, with systems entering production before their costs, limitations, dependencies, and behavior under different conditions are sufficiently understood. AI therefore illustrates the broader phenomenon particularly clearly: the cost of turning an architectural decision into working software is decreasing faster than the cost of making a sound architectural decision.

The consequences of poor architectural decisions often appear only when the assumptions underlying them change. A choice may initially produce a perfectly functional system while simultaneously introducing vendor coupling, difficult-to-migrate data structures, operational dependencies, scaling constraints, or specialized skill requirements. As the system grows, reversing the decision becomes increasingly expensive because new data, contracts, integrations, training requirements, rewrites, and operational dependencies accumulate around it. Technical debt can therefore be understood as more than postponed work: it is the increasing cost of maintaining or correcting a decision that has become inappropriate. Implementation cost and reversibility cost are distinct dimensions; a managed service may solve a problem in hours while the architecture built around it persists for years. Consequently, architectural decisions should be evaluated not only by their initial implementation cost but also by the cost of changing them, the assumptions supporting them, and the ease with which they can be reversed. As technology lowers the barrier to adoption, the temptation to defer consideration of exit costs becomes correspondingly greater.

The problem with existing frameworks is not that they are useless, but that they address different portions of architectural work without necessarily forming a comprehensive decision-making discipline. The author's project-management background creates an expectation that a field can consolidate a body of knowledge capable of organizing concepts, practices, and decisions despite changing tools. TOGAF is valuable but deliberately addresses Enterprise Architecture rather than System Design, focusing on organizational capabilities, governance, processes, and strategic alignment. arc42 is closer to software architecture and provides a pragmatic structure for documenting context, quality requirements, decisions, building blocks, runtime, and deployment, but documenting an architecture is not identical to structuring the reasoning that produced it. ADRs, C4, and ATAM similarly address decision recording, architectural representation, and trade-off analysis respectively, yet leave architects to determine how these practices should be connected, which criteria should be evaluated, how trade-offs involving cost, risk, operational complexity, team capability, reversibility, and vendor dependency should be handled, and when decisions should be revisited. In practice, architecture is therefore often represented directly through cloud components in diagramming tools, with much of the reasoning remaining implicit. The resulting diagrams show what was selected but may not reveal why it was selected, which alternatives were rejected, what assumptions support it, or what reversal would cost. The missing element is therefore not another technology-selection framework, but a body of knowledge capable of structuring and communicating architectural reasoning without depending entirely on individual experience.

The idea of a “PMBOK for Solution Architecture” is intended as a body of knowledge that provides language, principles, and structures for organizing architectural decision-making without prescribing specific technologies or architectures. It would not determine which database, cloud, or pattern to choose, because the technological environment changes too rapidly and architecture is inherently contextual. Instead, it would help architects structure problems, make assumptions explicit, communicate reasoning, and preserve judgment. Existing approaches such as arc42, ADRs, C4, and ATAM already address important parts of this objective, but the proposed gap is the lack of a structure connecting them into a comprehensive process without requiring each architect to construct that process independently. A highly prescriptive methodology would be undesirable because architectural decisions rarely have objectively correct answers and excessive proceduralism could replace judgment with compliance. Nevertheless, some architectural knowledge can be systematized, just as PMBOK organizes project-management knowledge without dictating which projects to pursue or what individual decisions to make. The challenge would be finding a level of abstraction stable enough to survive technological change while remaining concrete enough to support real decisions, focusing on durable decision-making structures rather than continuously incorporating every new technology.

Modern systems are therefore becoming easier to build and harder to design because cloud, managed services, SaaS, and AI have reduced the cost of implementation and experimentation while shifting the bottleneck toward judgment. Architects increasingly need to decide what should be built, which alternatives should be eliminated, which trade-offs should be accepted, and which future consequences are acceptable. Knowing more technologies remains useful, but as the solution space expands, consciously reducing that space becomes increasingly important; architectural expertise includes knowing which alternatives can safely be discarded without discarding the right ones. The proposed need for a “PMBOK for Solution Architecture” is consequently not a demand for another technology framework, but for a more consolidated discipline around architectural decision-making in a rapidly changing environment. Architecture ultimately concerns more than successfully building a solution: it concerns making sound decisions about what is worth building while preserving, as far as possible, the ability to change those decisions when their underlying assumptions cease to hold.
