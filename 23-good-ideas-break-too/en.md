# Good Ideas Break Too

_How strategy, execution, and the cash cycle determine business sustainability_

**Summary:** A good idea does not, by itself, guarantee the sustainability of a business. Value must be transformed into revenue, revenue into cash, and cash into the capacity to sustain the next cycle, because decisions that appear correct in isolation can, together, create an economically unviable operation. Financial and operational metrics help reveal when assumptions are no longer holding—and, most importantly, when there is still time to correct the trajectory.

---

A few days ago, I read an analysis by CB Insights that made me return to a question I find quite interesting: why do businesses with good ideas, relevant products, and apparently competent teams end up shutting down? The study, published in March 2026, examined 431 venture-backed startups that had ceased operations since 2023. In 70% of the cases, running out of capital appeared among the reasons for the shutdown. The number is striking, but what interested me most was what lies behind it. The analysis itself points to factors such as product-market fit problems, inadequate timing, and unsustainable unit economics. This raises a different question: are we confusing the moment when an organization ceases to exist with what actually led it there?

There is an interesting analogy with aviation. After an accident, the investigation does not merely seek to identify the moment when the aircraft crashed. The black box helps reconstruct what happened beforehand: what the conditions were, what decisions were made, what signals were available, what events followed one another, and how different factors contributed to the outcome. The objective is not to find a single cause, but to reconstruct the sequence that led to the accident so that it is possible to understand where the system failed and how to prevent a similar combination from producing the same result again.

Perhaps it is useful to look at companies that cease operations in a similar way. When the cash runs out, that is the most visible event, but it is hardly the complete story. Before it, there is a sequence of decisions about product, market, pricing, growth, people, technology, capital, and operations. Some assumptions may have proven incorrect. Some signals may have been ignored. Some individually reasonable decisions may have produced, when combined, an unsustainable result. That sequence is what interests me. Not only why a company failed, but how it got there and what signals could have made it possible to identify the problem earlier.

It is interesting to observe how, in the business environment, we tend to separate strategy, operations, and finance as though they were distinct disciplines. We talk about market, product, technology, sales, and growth in one language; revenue, costs, working capital, and liquidity in another. In the day-to-day functioning of a business, however, these dimensions are permanently connected. A pricing change affects margin and demand. A commercial decision changes acquisition costs and collection periods. A new feature may increase retention but also raise infrastructure costs. An expansion may require financing long before it generates a return. Even the decision to offer better payment terms may, in practice, represent a choice to finance the customer.

For this reason, a good idea is not necessarily a good business. Solving a real problem and delivering a desired product are important conditions, but they are not sufficient. There must be an economically sustainable way to capture part of the value created. This involves understanding how much it costs to acquire a customer, how much they generate over the course of the relationship, how much it costs to serve them, what margin is produced, how long it takes to recover the initial investment, and when revenue actually turns into cash. The difference between creating value and being able to capture it is one of the most important boundaries between an interesting product and a sustainable organization.

This logic does not belong only to the startup world. A traditional company can face the same dilemmas on a different scale. A nonprofit organization also needs to finance people, infrastructure, and operations in order to continue producing impact, even if its objective is not to distribute profit. Likewise, a company may deliberately choose to operate with lower margins, invest aggressively before obtaining a return, or use debt to accelerate an expansion. None of these decisions is inherently wrong. What changes is the need to understand the assumptions supporting each choice and the ability to absorb a potential change in circumstances.

Perhaps there is an important gap in the training of many managers. I do not need a PhD in economics to run an organization. I also do not need to replace the work of a controller, accountant, treasurer, or capital markets specialist. But I need to understand enough to interpret the questions the numbers are trying to answer. I need to know, for example, what CAC means, why MRR and ARR do not necessarily represent available cash, how long it takes to recover the investment made to acquire a customer, how the collection period affects working capital, and why a rapidly growing operation may need more financing precisely when it appears to be doing better.

This does not mean putting finance above product, technology, people, or the market. It means recognizing that every management decision consumes resources and changes the conditions for subsequent decisions. A strategy can be excellent, execution can be competent, and the product can be desired by customers, yet there can still be an economic combination incapable of sustaining the whole. Understanding how this combination works, therefore, should not be a competency restricted to the finance function. It should be part of the basic repertoire of anyone making decisions about an organization's future.

## A Good Idea Is Not Necessarily a Good Business

There is an important difference between an idea that creates value and a business capable of capturing that value sustainably. A product can solve a real problem and still have no market willing to pay enough for it. The price may be below the economic value delivered, or above what the market accepts. The company may acquire customers but spend so much to acquire them that every new contract increases revenue while simultaneously increasing the loss. It may also find a promising market but discover that the operation required to serve it costs more than the model can support.

This is where the concept of unit economics comes in: the economics of each unit of the business needs to make sense before scale can turn that model into a sustainable company. Growth does not necessarily correct an economically poor operation. In some cases, it merely causes it to consume resources more quickly.

Some metrics help us see this dynamic. CAC, Customer Acquisition Cost, indicates how much it costs to acquire a customer. MRR, Monthly Recurring Revenue, shows the recurring monthly revenue generated by the operation, while ARR, Annual Recurring Revenue, annualizes that recurring revenue and makes it possible to track the size of the business over time. These are particularly useful indicators for companies with recurring revenue models, but none of them, in isolation, tells us whether growth is producing value.

A company can increase its MRR while CAC grows even faster. It can report an impressive ARR and discover that its customers remain for only a short time. It can acquire thousands of new contracts and require successive capital injections because the capital invested in acquisition takes too long to return. It can even show rapid revenue growth and simultaneous deterioration in its financial position.

Therefore, the more interesting question is not simply “are we growing?” but “how much does it cost to produce this growth, how much economic value does each new customer generate, and how long does it take to recover the capital invested to acquire them?” This shift in perspective transforms growth from an isolated objective into a question of economic efficiency.

## Strategy Is Also a Financial Decision

Every strategy involves resource allocation. When a company decides to enter a new market, hire a team, build a platform, open a store, reduce prices, extend payment terms, or invest in customer acquisition, it is doing more than choosing a direction: it is committing resources today based on an expectation about the future.

This means that every strategic decision carries a financial dimension. Not because the finance department should decide on product, technology, or market, but because none of these decisions happens without consuming some type of resource. People need to be hired, systems need to be built, campaigns need to be funded, inventory needs to be purchased, and operations need to be maintained until the return appears. Every choice, therefore, has an opportunity cost: the resource used in one initiative is no longer available for another.

This also means that a decision does not need to generate an immediate return to be good. A company may deliberately operate at a loss for a certain period to gain market share, invest in technology before monetizing it, or expand its capacity before demand has fully materialized. It may use debt, investor capital, or receivables financing to fund this interval. The point is not to require every decision to produce an immediate return, but to understand which hypothesis is being financed, how much capital it requires, how long it may take to prove itself, and what conditions need to be true for the investment to make sense.

This is where strategy and financial discipline meet. A strategic decision is, to some extent, a bet on the future. The greater the capital committed, the more important it becomes to understand the assumptions behind that bet and the room available for those assumptions to be wrong. A company with substantial cash can afford to experiment for longer. Another, with little liquidity and significant obligations, may need much stronger evidence before making the same commitment.

The question, therefore, is not to eliminate financial risk. That would be incompatible with virtually any significant business decision. It is to understand what risk is being assumed, how much it costs to assume it, what returns can justify it, and, most importantly, what will happen if the assumptions do not hold. That last question is particularly important. Strategy is not only about deciding where we want to go. It is also about understanding how much we are willing to lose to discover that we were wrong.

## The Cash Cycle

This is where the concept I consider most useful for thinking about the subject comes in: the cash cycle. Every organization needs to transform resources into some form of result and, to continue operating, needs part of that result to return to finance the next cycle. The starting point may be shareholder capital, debt, supplier credit, or resources already generated by the operation itself. These resources finance people, technology, inventory, infrastructure, and customer acquisition. The operation, in turn, produces goods or services that are sold, generate revenue, and create receivables. From there, another journey begins: collecting, receiving, correctly identifying the amounts, reconciling the transactions, and transforming what was sold into available cash. That cash returns to the operation to pay suppliers, salaries, taxes, and debt, remunerate capital, and finance new investments.

The cycle seems simple when presented this way, but each transition has its own risks. The product may not find a market. The price may not adequately capture its value. CAC may grow faster than the revenue generated by new customers. The sale may happen, but the customer may cancel before generating a sufficient return. The billing may contain errors. Payment may be delayed. Money may come in without the company being able to identify it correctly. A transaction may remain pending reconciliation. The average collection period may increase while the operation's obligations continue to come due normally. A change in interest rates may increase the cost of financing the interval between paying and receiving.

It is therefore possible for all the most visible indicators to appear positive while some part of this cycle begins to deteriorate. Sales are growing, MRR is increasing, new customers are being acquired, and revenue is breaking records. Even so, cash may be coming under increasing pressure. The problem is that each stage occurs at a different time and with different degrees of predictability. The money spent to acquire a customer happens today; revenue may be recognized over months or years; billing may occur before collection; and available cash needs to sustain the entire operation while this process takes place.

This is why looking only at sales volume provides an incomplete view of the business. The more interesting question is not only how much we are selling, but how long it takes to turn that sale into cash, how much it costs to make that journey, and how much of that value actually returns to finance the next cycle. At its core, this is what the cash cycle seeks to reveal: not only how much value an organization can create, but how efficiently it can transform that value into the capacity to continue operating and growing.

## Growth Is Not Just Revenue

This distinction becomes even more important in companies that are growing rapidly. Growth is often treated as an unequivocal indicator of health: more customers, more revenue, more MRR, greater market share. But growth also requires resources, and the speed at which a company expands its operation may be greater than the speed at which it can recover the capital invested to produce it.

Imagine a company that increases its MRR by 30% in one year. The number looks excellent. But suppose that, to produce this growth, CAC increased by 60%, customers began paying over longer periods, and the structure required to serve them became significantly more expensive. Revenue grew, but the capital required to produce each new unit of revenue grew even more. In this scenario, the business may be expanding its operation while reducing its financial capacity.

This is why concepts such as unit economics are important. Before asking only how much the company is growing, we need to understand whether the economics behind that growth work. How much does it cost to acquire a customer? How much does that customer generate over the course of the relationship? What margin is associated with that revenue? How long do they remain? How long does it take to recover CAC? And how much capital needs to be committed before that return occurs?

These questions help distinguish healthy growth from growth that continuously depends on new resources to sustain itself. A company with healthy unit economics may have an excellent reason to seek capital and accelerate its expansion. If each new customer tends to generate more value than the capital required to acquire and serve them, increasing scale can be an efficient way to create value.

The problem appears when scale merely multiplies poor economics. If each new customer requires more capital than they are likely to return, acquiring more customers does not solve the problem. It merely increases its size. In that case, external capital can buy time, but it does not change the fundamental mathematics of the business.

Therefore, it may be more useful to talk about growth quality. It is not enough to know how much a company has grown. We need to understand how much capital was required to produce that growth, what return that capital can generate, and how long that return takes. Speed matters, but the economics behind it matter even more.

## Revenue Is Not Cash

Perhaps this is one of the most basic and, at the same time, most important distinctions for any manager: revenue and cash are different things. A sale can represent revenue for the company without representing money available at that moment. A company can sell today, issue the invoice, and only receive payment 30, 60, or 90 days later. During that interval, salaries continue to come due, suppliers need to be paid, taxes are collected, and infrastructure continues consuming resources. The business needs to finance this difference between the moment when it creates and recognizes revenue and the moment when it actually receives the money.

This financing can come from different sources. The company can use its own cash, draw on a working capital facility, take bank credit, receive a shareholder contribution, finance receivables, or negotiate longer terms with suppliers. Each alternative has a cost, a limit, and a level of risk. The greater the distance between paying and receiving, the greater the amount of capital required to keep the operation running. This is why two companies with the same revenue can have completely different cash requirements.

This difference also explains why growth can increase financial pressure. If a company needs to invest to acquire new customers today, recognizes revenue over the course of the relationship, and only receives payment weeks or months after billing, each new sale may require capital before it begins returning that capital to the operation. The faster the growth, the greater this financing need may be. A company can therefore be increasing revenue and consuming cash simultaneously.

The reverse is also possible. An organization can have cash available without that meaning its economic model is healthy. An investor contribution or new debt increases financial availability, but does not necessarily improve the profitability of the operation. The money is in the bank, but it still needs to be transformed into a business capable of generating enough return to sustain the next cycles.

This is why profit and cash answer different questions. Economic results help us understand whether the operation is creating value after considering revenues and costs. Cash flow helps us understand whether there is sufficient liquidity to meet obligations when they come due. A company can be profitable and face liquidity difficulties; it can also have plenty of money available and be destroying value.

This distinction does not require every manager to master accounting. It simply requires them to understand that a sale does not end when the contract is signed, nor when revenue appears in the report. There is still a path between the value sold and the money available, and the ability to travel that path predictably is a fundamental part of the sustainability of any business.

## Collections Are Part of Strategy

Collections are often placed at the end of the chain, almost as an administrative consequence of a sale that has already happened. But, looking at the complete cycle, they are much closer to the monetization of the business itself. Before there is anything to collect, the company has already invested in product, technology, people, marketing, and sales. Someone found the customer, negotiated the commercial terms, signed the contract, and delivered what had been promised. When the invoice is issued, all this effort finally becomes a financial obligation of the customer. Collections is the process that helps transform that obligation into payment.

If the customer does not pay, part of the value created has not yet been captured. The company has performed practically all the work necessary to produce that revenue, but it still does not have the corresponding financial resources to finance the next cycle. The greater the volume of receivables and the greater the delay, the greater the amount of capital that remains trapped outside the operation.

Therefore, collections should not be viewed merely as a function for recovering overdue amounts. It is also a source of information about revenue quality. If customers delay payment because they do not recognize a particular charge, there may be a commercial or contractual problem. If payments are hindered by recurring invoice errors, there is an operational failure. If a particular segment has significantly higher delinquency than the others, there may be a problem with the credit policy, the profile of acquired customers, or the terms offered. If good customers begin delaying payments after a product or pricing change, the problem may be much closer to strategy than to the collections process.

There is also an important relationship dimension. Collecting effectively does not simply mean collecting more. An aggressive policy may increase short-term recovery while simultaneously damaging relationships with strategic customers. Likewise, indiscriminately tolerating delays may preserve relationships in the short term while compromising the operation's liquidity. The issue is finding a process that can balance recovery, financial predictability, and the quality of the commercial relationship.

In the end, the sale is not the endpoint of the economic relationship with the customer. There is a difference between selling something and capturing the value of that sale. Collections occupies precisely this space between the two. It transforms a promise of payment into actually available resources and, at the same time, reveals what is happening with revenue after the contract has been signed.

Collecting, therefore, is not just about recovering money. It is about completing monetization and discovering whether what the company believes it is selling is, in fact, turning into cash.

## Reconciliation Is Financial Observability

Reconciliation may seem even further removed from strategy. At first glance, it is merely an operational activity: comparing what should have happened with what actually happened. How much should have been received, how much came in, from which customer, related to which charge, into which account, and whether the recorded amount corresponds to what actually occurred. When there is a difference, it is necessary to understand its origin and determine whether it represents an error, a delay, a legitimate exception, or some problem in the process.

But it is precisely in this comparison between expectation and reality that reconciliation becomes important. An organization constantly works with different representations of the same event: the contract says one thing, the billing system records another, the bank presents a transaction, and the accounting system needs to reflect the result. When these representations cease to match, there is information that deserves investigation.

In technology, we use observability to understand a system's behavior based on the signals it produces. It is not enough to know that an application is working; we want to identify latency, errors, unavailability, and anomalous behavior before they turn into larger problems. Reconciliation performs a similar function in the financial environment. It creates a mechanism to compare different sources of information and identify where reality has ceased to correspond to what the processes expected.

An unidentified payment may indicate a failure in the collections process. A recurring difference between billing and receipts may point to an integration, master data, or contractual problem. Frequent manual adjustments may reveal a structural deficiency in the process. Growing receivables may indicate deterioration in payment terms. A discrepancy concentrated in a particular product, customer, or channel may reveal a problem that would not appear by looking only at the consolidated result.

These signals are not necessarily financial problems in themselves. They are evidence that something deserves to be understood. And the earlier they are identified, the greater the possibility of correcting the cause before it produces a significant impact on the business.

Therefore, reconciliation should not be treated merely as a closing step or an operational obligation of the finance function. It is a form of observability over the company's economic cycle. It helps answer whether what the systems say should have happened corresponds to what actually happened and, when there is a difference, creates an opportunity to discover why. In the end, control does not simply mean knowing the result. It means being able to explain how we arrived at it.

## Capital Can Accelerate the Cycle, but It Cannot Fix the Business

No company needs to grow exclusively with its own capital. Loans, credit facilities, receivables financing, investor contributions, and other forms of financing are legitimate instruments for accelerating an operation, financing working capital, acquiring equipment, expanding capacity, or bridging the gap between investment and expected return. In certain models, relying on external capital may even be more efficient than waiting for the operation itself to generate all the resources required to finance the next stage of growth.

The problem is not using external capital, but failing to understand what that capital is financing. An investment may be made to accelerate an operation that already shows signs of viability, to explore an opportunity whose potential return justifies the risk, or simply to finance a predictable interval between expenditure and receipt. Another situation is using successive capital injections to sustain an operation whose fundamental economics continue not to work. In the first case, financing accelerates value creation. In the second, it merely extends the time until the problem has to be confronted.

This distinction becomes even more important when debt is involved. An investor who puts capital into a company assumes part of the business risk and, in certain structures, may lose the entire investment. A creditor has a different relationship: there is a payment obligation, normally with a defined term and cost, regardless of whether the strategy produces the expected return. The greater the leverage, the smaller the operation's margin for error tends to be.

Retail provides good examples of this dynamic. Operating with low margins is not necessarily a problem; in many models, the logic is precisely to combine high volume, high turnover, and operational efficiency. The difficulty arises when narrow margins are accompanied by a high working capital requirement and a significant debt structure. In this scenario, there is little safety margin between normal operation and the inability to meet obligations.

As long as demand, costs, collection periods, and financing conditions remain close to the original assumptions, the model may work. But a decline in sales, an increase in costs, deterioration in delinquency, an extension of the collection period, or an increase in the cost of debt can quickly consume that buffer. Not all assumptions need to be wrong. It is enough for some of them to deteriorate simultaneously.

This is where leverage stops being merely a growth tool and becomes a source of fragility. The greater the commitment assumed to finance the operation, the smaller the ability to absorb a shock without drastically changing the plan.

Therefore, the question should not be only how much capital a company can raise. It should be how much capital makes sense to commit given the quality of the business, the expected return, the time horizon required to produce it, and the ability to withstand a scenario in which the assumptions do not hold.

Debt can accelerate a healthy business. But it can also accelerate an unviable business toward collapse. Sometimes, a catastrophe is not necessary to discover this. A hiccup is enough.

## Every Good Idea Needs to Be Financially Sustainable

When we talk about financial returns, it is easy to associate the concept directly with profit. But that is not necessarily the issue. A traditional company needs to generate a return compatible with the capital employed. A startup may deliberately prioritize growth and reinvest practically all its revenue in expansion. A nonprofit organization may aim to maximize social impact rather than distribute returns to its funders. The objectives are different, but there is a condition common to all of them: the organization needs to be able to financially sustain the operation required to achieve them.

An NGO seeking to expand services to a particular population needs to finance people, infrastructure, technology, suppliers, and all the other activities necessary to execute its mission. A startup seeking to capture a market needs to finance product, sales, and operations before collecting all the expected return. A traditional company deciding to expand its capacity needs to commit capital today in the expectation of obtaining results in the future. In all these cases, there is a relationship between resources consumed and expected results.

This also means that consuming resources without generating an immediate return is not necessarily a problem. Investments can take years to produce results. An organization may accept operating temporarily at a loss to build capacity, develop a technology, or expand its impact. The point is to know how much is being consumed, how long this strategy can be sustained, and what evidence indicates that the expected result is becoming more likely.

Financial sustainability, therefore, does not mean maximizing profit or accumulating cash indefinitely. It means having the capacity to continue financing what the organization exists to do. The result may be profit, growth, social impact, capacity expansion, or any other form of value defined by the organization itself. What cannot be ignored is the financial constraint that exists between ambition and the capacity to execute it.

In the end, a good idea needs more than people willing to believe in it. It needs sufficient resources to turn the idea into reality and a model capable of sustaining that transformation over time. Without this, even an initiative capable of producing significant value may simply end before it has the chance to produce it.

## When Numbers Help Strategy

I do not need a PhD in economics to run a company. I also do not need to replace the work of a CFO, controller, accountant, or treasurer. But I need to understand enough to ask the right questions and comprehend the economic consequences of the decisions I make.

How much does it cost to acquire a customer? How much does the customer generate? What is the margin? How long does it take to recover that investment? How long does it take to collect? How much is outstanding? How much actually entered the cash balance? These questions should not be restricted to the finance function. Business sustainability is a consequence of decisions made across the entire organization.

This is where strategy, execution, and control stop looking like separate subjects. Strategy defines where resources will be allocated. Execution converts those resources into products and services. The market turns those products into revenue. Billing, collections, and receipts convert revenue into cash. Reconciliation verifies whether what should have happened corresponds to what actually happened. And metrics help transform these signals into decisions.

This is also a learning cycle. CAC, churn, margin, collection period, and cash are not merely numbers that describe the past. They are signals that help determine whether the assumptions behind the strategy remain valid. When these signals begin to deteriorate, there is an opportunity to correct the trajectory before the problem becomes irreversible.

This is where I return to the black box. When an aircraft crashes, the impact is merely the final event in a sequence. The investigation seeks to understand what happened beforehand: what the conditions were, what decisions were made, and what signals were available. The objective is not only to explain that accident, but to learn from it. Companies also leave signals along the way. Good management consists, in part, of knowing how to interpret them while there is still time to change the trajectory.

A good idea is only the beginning. To survive, it needs to create value, capture that value, and transform the result into the capacity to continue operating, learning, and growing.

Because good ideas break too.
