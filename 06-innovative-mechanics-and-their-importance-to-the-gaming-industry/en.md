# Innovative Mechanics and Their Importance to the Game Industry

_Maybe the future of video games does not lie in bigger worlds, but in new ways of interacting with them_

**Summary**: I argue that innovation in video games does not lie in creating bigger worlds or adding more content, but in expanding the player's possibilities for interaction. The most interesting mechanics are those that transform familiar systems into new ways of playing, making the world react to decisions and allowing knowledge, preparation, and experimentation to replace some of the traditional formulas. For this reason, I believe the next leap in games lies less in accumulating systems and more in questioning conventions such as XP, HUDs, fast travel, dialogue, and linear progression, creating worlds in which experiences emerge from the rules and choices of the player themselves.

---

I have noticed something strange in recent years: more and more often, I start a game with the feeling that I am facing something new and, a few minutes later, I have the feeling that I am playing something I have known for years. I am not talking about learning the controls or recognizing the structure of a genre. I am talking about something deeper. I may be in a medieval world, a futuristic city, a distant galaxy, or a post-apocalyptic setting. I may control a warrior, an assassin, a soldier, or a mage. I may be facing a story completely different from anything else I have ever played. Even so, at some point, I realize that I am doing what I have done so many times before: following a marker on the map, reaching a location, defeating some enemies, collecting resources, opening a chest, receiving XP, improving my equipment, and moving on to the next objective. The aesthetics change, the narrative changes, the characters change, but the way I interact with that world remains familiar.

Perhaps the most curious thing is that this does not happen because games are necessarily worse. The industry's ability to create virtual worlds has never been more impressive. We have the technology to build enormous environments, extremely detailed characters, sophisticated physics systems, complex artificial intelligence, and experiences that would have been unimaginable a few decades ago. The problem is that all this technical evolution seems to coexist with a certain convergence in design. Major releases increasingly share the same vocabulary: open world, XP, skill tree, loot, crafting, minimap, fast travel, rarity-tiered equipment, points of interest, side quests, and dozens of systems designed to tell us that we are progressing. Each of these mechanics can be excellent when used in the right context. What bothers me is noticing how they have stopped feeling like choices and started feeling like requirements. There is not necessarily a formula written somewhere saying that a major game needs to have all these things, but it is enough to look at the highest-budget releases to realize that there is a formula being followed.

This leads me to a question that seems simple, but that I consider fundamental when thinking about the future of video games: what, after all, makes a game fun? A good story certainly helps. Interesting characters, striking art direction, a memorable soundtrack, and high-level audiovisual production can also transform an experience. But there is a fundamental difference between video games and other forms of entertainment: we play them. We are not merely spectators of a story, but participants in a system. We need to make decisions, understand rules, experiment with possibilities, deal with consequences, recognize patterns, and discover what is possible to do within that world. It is precisely this interaction that makes video games such a particular medium. A story can be extraordinary and still rest on an uninteresting game. In the same way, a relatively simple narrative can support a memorable experience when the game's own rules are capable of producing situations the player did not expect.

That is why, when I think about innovation in the game industry, I think less about graphics, amount of content, or map size and more about mechanics. Mechanics are the language through which the player interacts with the game. They determine what we can do, what we need to observe, what decisions we need to make, and what consequences our actions can produce. If this language remains limited, adding content does not necessarily solve the problem. We can build a gigantic world and fill it with hundreds of activities, but if all those activities essentially use the same set of interactions, the experience remains small. There is an enormous difference between having more things to do and having more ways to play. A larger map is not necessarily a deeper system. One hundred missions do not necessarily mean one hundred different experiences. Replacing a forest with a desert, a castle with a city, or a sword with a rifle can completely change a game's presentation without significantly changing what the player does.

This is where I believe the discussion about innovation needs to be somewhat broader. A mechanic does not need to be completely unprecedented to be innovative. If innovation meant merely inventing something that absolutely no one had ever done before, practically any evolution in game design would simply be a variation of something earlier. The history of video games is built precisely through the reuse, combination, and transformation of ideas. A familiar mechanic can acquire a completely different function when placed in another context. Two old mechanics can produce new behavior when combined. An interface convention can become a property of the world. What matters is not necessarily the novelty of the mechanic in isolation, but the novelty of the possibilities it creates for the player.

## The history of games is also a history of innovation

When we look at the history of video games, it becomes clear that some of the most memorable experiences of each generation did not become references merely because they were technically impressive. They stood out because they introduced new possibilities for the player. In many cases, what seemed like a small mechanical innovation ended up influencing an entire generation of games. What we now consider natural in a video game often began as a design decision that, at the time, seemed strange, risky, or simply different.

The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time is one of the clearest examples. The game did not create the concept of a three-dimensional world, but it showed how to adapt a design language originally built for two-dimensional environments to a 3D space. The focus-targeting system, for example, solved a fundamental problem of combat and exploration in three-dimensional environments, allowing the player to control the camera and character without turning every encounter into a struggle against the interface itself. The same logic appeared in the way the world was constructed, in the relationship between exploration, combat, and puzzle solving, and in the use of items as tools that progressively expanded the player's possibilities. Ocarina of Time's importance lies not only in having been a great game for its time, but in helping establish a language for an entire generation of three-dimensional games.

Majora’s Mask took this philosophy even further by turning time into a central mechanic. The player did not simply advance through a story while the clock ran. The world had its own routine, with characters who had schedules, trajectories, and behaviors determined by that temporal cycle. The knowledge acquired allowed the player to anticipate events, discover opportunities, and change how each cycle was used. Time stopped being merely a measure of the duration of the experience and became part of the system the player needed to understand. This difference is important: the game did not need to create hundreds of different activities to make its world interesting. It was enough to make the same people, the same places, and the same events take on different meanings as the player better understood how that world worked.

The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim presents a different but equally relevant approach. Part of its strength lies in the attempt to turn progression into a direct consequence of the player's actions. Instead of requiring the player to choose a rigid class in advance and follow a predetermined progression tree, the game allows skills to evolve as they are used. This creates a more direct relationship between what the player does and what their character becomes. Progression stops being merely a sequence of rewards distributed by the game and also becomes a record of the player's behavior. If you spend much of the journey using a particular skill, it becomes part of that character's identity. The mechanic is not merely saying that you have become stronger. It is saying who you have become.

Age of Empires II is another interesting example because it turns economic, military, and territorial decisions into an interdependent system. The player is not simply building units to defeat the opponent. They need to manage resources, decide when to expand, choose which technologies to research, determine where to build, protect routes, and adapt their strategy to the opponent's decisions. A choice in one part of the system affects several others. Investing excessively in an army can compromise the economy. Expanding quickly can increase access to resources, but also create new areas that need to be defended. Depth does not necessarily arise because there are hundreds of isolated rules, but because the existing rules interact in ways that produce different situations in each match.

The Sims may be an even more radical example because it practically removes the traditional structure of game objectives. The player does not necessarily need to save the world, defeat an enemy, or reach the end of a campaign. The premise is to manage a virtual life, build a house, establish relationships, develop a career, and deal with the consequences of one's own decisions. The game transforms seemingly mundane activities into interactive systems. The fun is not necessarily in achieving an objective defined by the developer, but in observing what happens when different systems begin to interact. A poorly planned house affects the routine. A career influences available time. A relationship alters behaviors. A seemingly small choice can produce unexpected consequences. The game creates space for the player to establish their own objectives.

These examples are different from one another, but there is something they have in common. None of them depends exclusively on the amount of content to create depth. Ocarina of Time found new ways to control and explore a three-dimensional world. Majora’s Mask transformed time into a system the player needed to understand. Skyrim connected progression and behavior. Age of Empires II created depth through the interaction between economy, territory, and strategy. The Sims transformed everyday life itself into a set of systems capable of generating emergent stories. In all these cases, the central question does not seem to have been merely “how much content can we put into the game?”, but “what can we allow the player to do?”.

This distinction helps explain why certain mechanics remain relevant decades after their games were released. Graphics age. Resolution increases. Models become more detailed. Lighting systems are replaced by more advanced technologies. But a good mechanic continues to work because its importance does not depend on the technical capability used to implement it. It depends on the possibilities it offers the player. This is why we can still return to old games and discover depth in systems that, visually, belong to another era. Technology can make a world more beautiful, but rules determine what we can do within it.

This also helps put innovation into a different perspective. Not every innovation needs to create a new genre or completely revolutionize the industry. Sometimes it is enough to find a different way to solve a known problem. In other situations, innovation lies in connecting systems that would normally function independently. In others, it is simply about giving the player a freedom that previous games did not offer. What makes these ideas important is not merely the fact that they were new when they were released, but that they expanded the space of possibilities within the game.

Perhaps this is exactly the kind of innovation we should be looking for today. Not necessarily a mechanic that no one has ever imagined, but a mechanic that makes us realize there is a different way to play. If the great games of the past were able to transform camera, time, progression, economy, social relationships, and exploration into systems that the player needed to understand and master, perhaps the next step is not to add more systems to current games, but to question the systems we already consider indispensable.

## The open world does not need to be a map full of icons

The concept of the open world may be the clearest example of the transformation of a design possibility into an industry expectation. The promise of an open world is freedom. The player can choose where to go, what to explore, and which activities to undertake. In practice, however, many open worlds function as large task maps. The player opens the screen and finds dozens of icons indicating missions, camps, collectibles, resources, towers, dungeons, and other points of interest. There is freedom to choose the order in which these activities are completed, but little freedom over what to do within them. The map is large, but the possibilities remain relatively small.

An alternative would be to think of the open world less as a collection of content and more as a set of systems capable of producing new content from the player's actions. Imagine, for example, a dungeon that initially exists simply as a dangerous location that needs to be explored. After being conquered, its location could make it ideal for establishing a trading outpost. The player could invest money to build a workshop, establish a caravan route, or turn that region into a small economic center. The dungeon would not simply be marked as completed on the map. It would have altered the structure of that world. A combat activity could generate an economic consequence that, in turn, could alter a trade route, increase the importance of a city, and create new conflicts.

The same logic could be applied to the economy. Instead of money existing primarily to buy weapons, armor, and consumables, the player could invest in businesses, properties, caravans, workshops, or expeditions. These investments could generate income, resources, or advantages over time, but would also involve risks, maintenance costs, and different opportunities. The player would need to decide whether to use their resources immediately or give up a present advantage to build something that might benefit them in the future. Money would stop being merely a number that increases because the player completes missions and decreases because they buy equipment. It would become a tool capable of modifying the world.

This change would also alter the player's relationship with exploration itself. In a traditional open world, finding a new region generally means finding more content that the developers previously placed there. In a system-driven world, discovering a region could mean discovering a new possibility. A mountain pass could be important because it connects two cities and allows a trade route to be established. A river could be strategic because it facilitates the transportation of certain resources. An apparently useless area could become valuable after the player discovers a particular raw material or realizes its importance to an economic activity. The value of a place would not need to be indicated by an icon on the map. It could be discovered by the player through the relationships between that world's systems.

In this model, exploration stops being a search for points of interest and becomes a search for possibilities. The player does not travel across the map because a marker says there is something waiting for them, but because there is a reason to believe that a particular place may be important. This creates a fundamental difference between content and discovery. Content is what the game places in a particular location. Discovery is what the player realizes they can do with what they found. A truly interesting open world does not necessarily need something new hidden behind every mountain. It needs to make the player want to discover what can happen if they interact with what they find.

Perhaps this is precisely the main difference between a large map and a large world. A large map has a lot of space. A large world has many relationships between its elements. If a city depends on a particular trade route, if a route depends on a safe region, if that safety depends on the presence of a garrison, and if the garrison depends on resources produced in another region, then a change to any of these elements can produce consequences in several others. The world begins to have its own structure, and the player stops simply consuming activities distributed across the map and starts participating in a system that can react to their decisions.

## Fast travel can be immersive

Fast travel is one of the most common mechanics in modern games and also one of those we most easily associate with breaking immersion. We open the map, select a known point, and a few seconds later we are somewhere else. The mechanic solves a real problem: after traveling the same path several times, repeating that journey can stop being fun and become merely a way of artificially extending the experience's duration. The problem may not be the possibility of traveling quickly, but the way this possibility is usually implemented. When the player simply selects a destination on the map, the journey ceases to exist as part of the world. They are not traveling. They are simply jumping from one point to another.

Ultima Online offers an interesting perspective because forms of transportation can exist as part of the world's own infrastructure. Moongates are an example of how fast travel can be incorporated into the logic of the universe rather than existing merely as an interface function. The player is not simply pressing a button to ignore distance. They are using a structure that exists in that world and has a function within it. The difference seems small, but its consequences can be significant. A transportation system can create strategic points, concentrate trade, increase the importance of certain regions, and even produce disputes between players. Transportation stops being merely a way to save time and becomes part of that world's geography.

This idea could be taken even further in a modern game. Routes could be built or controlled by characters and organizations. Caravans could depend on the safety of certain roads. Bridges could be destroyed, rebuilt, or controlled by factions. Ports could connect distant regions and become commercial centers. An isolated city could gain importance after the construction of a new road, while another could lose relevance when an alternative route becomes more efficient. The player would still be able to travel quickly, but their ability to do so would depend on the infrastructure existing in the world. Fast travel would stop being a function external to the experience and become a consequence of the very organization of that universe.

This would also allow travel to become a strategic decision. Instead of simply choosing between walking or teleporting, the player could evaluate which route is safer, which is faster, how much it costs to use, and what risks exist along the way. A caravan could be cheap but slow. A portal could be extremely fast but available only in certain locations. A sea route could allow the transportation of large quantities of resources but depend on political relations between ports. A passage controlled by a faction could be safe while relations with them remain good, but become inaccessible after a political change. Fast travel would remain possible, but the player would need to understand the world to make use of that possibility.

This approach would also create an important consequence for exploration. In many games, discovering a new fast-travel location simply means unlocking another point to which the player can transport themselves in the future. In a more integrated system, discovering a new route could mean discovering a new economic, political, or strategic possibility. Finding a passage between two regions could reduce the time needed to transport goods. Discovering a source of magical energy could allow the construction of a new portal. Establishing a relationship with a particular community could open access to an exclusive transportation network. Discovery would not merely serve to facilitate the player's movement, but to expand the infrastructure available within the world.

The most interesting part is that this makes it possible to solve the problem of repetition without simply removing the importance of space. The player does not need to cross a forest twenty times to prove that the forest exists. After learning the region, they can use a route that makes travel more efficient. The difference is that this efficiency does not need to come from an arbitrary button placed on the map. It can be a consequence of knowledge, economy, politics, and structures that the player helped build or discovered during their journey. Convenience stops being the enemy of immersion when it makes sense within the rules of that universe.

In this model, fast travel stops being a tool for removing gameplay and becomes a mechanic capable of creating gameplay. The player can travel quickly, but needs to build, discover, control, or use the structures that make that journey possible. The world stops being merely the space between two points and becomes a network of relationships connecting those points. Distance continues to exist, but the player learns ways to overcome it. And perhaps that is precisely what makes fast travel more interesting: not eliminating the world's geography, but turning the ability to cross it into a consequence of the player's own experience.

## Experience should come from learning, not grinding

Few mechanics are as widespread as XP. The player defeats enemies, completes missions, discovers places, and receives experience. After accumulating a certain amount, they level up and unlock new abilities. The system works very well because it turns progress into an objective and easily understood metric, but it has also created an almost automatic association between evolution and numbers. The character necessarily becomes more powerful because they accumulated enough points, regardless of what they actually learned during the journey. In many games, experience stopped representing knowledge and became merely the amount of activity the player completed.

Outer Wilds shows a particularly interesting alternative because much of the progression happens inside the player's head. The character does not need to receive a new ability for every important discovery. What changes is what the player knows. Information acquired at a certain moment can completely transform the way they interpret a place they have already visited. The world remains practically the same, but the experience changes because the player has changed. In this case, learning does not mean accumulating points. It means understanding a rule that was previously unknown and being able to use it when necessary.

This idea could be applied to many other systems. An enemy does not necessarily need a health bar for the player to know that it is injured. Its behavior, posture, movements, and breathing can communicate that information. A character does not need a reputation bar if the player can perceive that their social standing has changed through the way people react to their presence. A region does not necessarily need a marker if the environment provides enough information for the player to learn how to navigate it. In all these cases, some of the information normally transformed into numbers can be transferred to the world itself.

This also creates an important distinction between character skill and player skill. In a traditional system, when the character levels up, they receive a new capability because the game has decided that the time has come to unlock it. In a learning-based system, some of these capabilities can emerge simply because the player has finally understood how the world works. An experienced player does not necessarily need an additional ability to recognize a particular pattern, anticipate an enemy's behavior, or realize that an apparently dangerous situation can be solved in another way. The character did not become more powerful. The player got better.

This type of progression is particularly interesting because it allows knowledge to be treated as a resource. Information can open a passage, reveal a weakness, change the interpretation of a character, or turn an apparently useless location into an opportunity. The player may even forget or lose access to certain physical resources, but acquired knowledge remains. This creates a form of progression that does not need to be displayed on a bar and cannot easily be converted into a number. The game does not need to say that the player gained 500 XP for discovering something. It only needs to allow that discovery to have consequences.

This logic can also transform how we think about grinding. If experience represents learning, repeating an activity dozens of times should produce actual knowledge or improvement, not simply fill an abstract bar. Defeating the same enemy twenty times can make sense if the player is learning its patterns, discovering new strategies, or developing mastery over that mechanic. But if the only consequence is receiving enough points to unlock an ability that could have been granted in any other way, the system is transforming invested time into numerical progression. The player is not necessarily learning. They are simply accumulating.

This does not mean XP needs to disappear. An objective metric can be extremely useful for representing certain types of progress, especially when the game deals with training, professional experience, or skill development. The problem is turning XP into a universal language for representing practically everything. Combat, exploration, diplomacy, discovery, knowledge, and personal growth do not necessarily need to use the same system. Different forms of learning can produce different forms of progression, and some of them may not even appear as numbers to the player.

This idea also allows us to think about progression in generational terms. In a generational legacy system, learning could depend on the character's age. Younger characters could learn faster, while this capacity would gradually decrease throughout life. In old age, the character might stop progressing at the same speed, but take on another role: becoming a mentor to their descendants. Knowledge accumulated during a lifetime would be passed on to the next generation, accelerating their development.

Progression would then stop being an individual line. The player would not simply be evolving a character from level one to level one hundred. They would be building a lineage in which each generation receives part of the experience accumulated by those before them. A character could spend decades developing a particular skill and, before dying, pass some of that knowledge to their children. The new generation would begin with its own limitations, but also with an advantage that did not exist at the beginning of the journey. Progress would stop being merely a matter of accumulating numbers and come to represent something closer to what the word experience actually suggests: what was learned, practiced, and passed on over time.

Perhaps this is one of the greatest limitations of traditional XP. It transformed an extremely human idea into a mathematical unit. Experience should mean what we learn by facing situations, understanding systems, making mistakes, and developing knowledge. In video games, however, it frequently means merely a bar that needs to reach the next milestone. If we want to create more interesting progression systems, perhaps the question should not be “how much XP did the player gain?”, but “what do they know now that they did not know before?”.

## The HUD does not need to be visible all the time

The same need to question conventions appears in the interface. The traditional HUD constantly displays health, ammunition, map, objectives, available abilities, enemy indicators, and various other pieces of information. This is extremely efficient, but it also means that the game frequently translates the world for the player before they have an opportunity to interpret it. Instead of observing an enemy's behavior to understand whether it is injured, the player looks at a bar. Instead of learning to recognize a region, they follow a marker on the map. Instead of noticing that their character is reaching their physical limit, they check an indicator. The interface does not merely inform the player. It often interprets the world for them.

An alternative would be a contextual HUD. The interface would remain hidden by default and appear only while the player held a particular button. During that period, they could check health, ammunition, map, objectives, and other relevant information. When they released the button, the information would disappear again. The mechanic would continue to provide exactly the same data, but would completely change how that data is presented. Information would stop permanently occupying the field of view and become a tool the player consults when they consider it necessary.

The advantage is not simply making the screen cleaner. The more interesting aspect is changing the relationship between information and attention. The player stops constantly receiving all available data and starts deciding when they need it. This allows the world itself to take on some of the responsibility for communicating information. An enemy can demonstrate that it is injured through its posture, movement speed, or the way it reacts to attacks. The environment can indicate a direction through its architecture, lighting, vegetation, or geographic features. The character can demonstrate fatigue through breathing, movement, and increasing difficulty performing certain actions. The information continues to exist, but does not always need to be presented as a number on the screen.

The goal would not be to make the game artificially harder by hiding everything. Anyone who wants to use the HUD constantly could do so. The difference would be allowing the player to choose the level of abstraction through which they experience that world. One player may prefer to receive all objective information at all times, while another may choose to consult the interface only when truly necessary. The important thing is that the choice is not between having information or not having information, but between constantly receiving the system's interpretation or learning to interpret part of it independently.

This approach also creates a different form of progression. At the beginning of the journey, the player may depend heavily on the interface because they do not yet know the world. Over time, however, they begin to recognize patterns. They learn to identify when an enemy is close to being defeated, understand which landscape features help with navigation, and notice changes in characters' behavior. Gradually, the HUD becomes less necessary not because the game became easier, but because the player became better at interpreting it. The progression happens in the person behind the controller, not only in the character's attributes.

This could be taken even further if different information were communicated in different ways depending on context. A character might not need a permanent reputation indicator because merchants, guards, and residents would treat them differently as their influence increased. An objective might not need a marker because other characters could provide directions, geographical references, or information about the route. A city could be recognized by its architecture, sounds, or customs before the player even consults the map. The more capable the world is of communicating its own rules, the less the interface needs to occupy that role.

The interface would then stop being merely an information tool and become an optional layer of assistance. The player does not become better because they unlocked an ability that increases their perception. They become better because they learned to observe. They do not gain a new point in a talent tree because they discovered how to navigate. They learn to read the environment. They do not receive XP for realizing that an enemy is vulnerable. They develop knowledge of its behavior. Progression stops existing exclusively in the systems the game presents to the character and also begins to exist in the player's ability to understand the world in which they are situated.

## The inventory can be a preparation problem, not a math problem

Traditional inventories often turn item management into an exercise in logistics. There is a weight limit or a limited number of slots, the player finds new objects, and needs to decide what to discard. This system can be interesting in certain games, especially when resource management is an important part of the experience, but it often ends up creating only frequent interruptions to organize a list. The player spends part of their time deciding whether to carry another sword, how many potions still fit, or which low-value item needs to be abandoned to make room for another.

A dimensional inventory could solve this problem differently. The character would have a practically unlimited magical bag, eliminating the need to constantly choose what can or cannot be carried. The limitation would lie in access. Only items directly equipped on the body, such as weapons, potions, tools, and objects attached to the belt, could be used quickly. To access anything else, it would be necessary to open a real-time catalog without pausing the game. The character could carry dozens or even hundreds of objects, but only a few would actually be available at that moment.

This creates an important difference between possessing an object and being prepared to use it. The player could carry fifty items, but would need to decide beforehand which ones would be immediately accessible. In a dangerous situation, opening the bag to search for something could be risky. A tool that would be extremely useful in a particular situation might be stored deep in the inventory and require a few seconds to find. The problem stops being the character's physical capacity and becomes their preparation for what might happen.

This change also allows the inventory to participate more directly in strategy. Before entering a dangerous region, the player can decide which tools they want to keep accessible. Before a long journey, they can prepare a different equipment set. On an expedition, they can prioritize survival resources. In combat, they can keep certain weapons and consumables within reach. The inventory stops being merely a list of accumulated objects and becomes a representation of the character's preparation for a particular situation.

The question would then stop being “how much can the character carry?” and become “what are they prepared for?”. This difference seems small, but it completely changes the nature of the problem. Instead of spending time calculating weight and space, the player needs to anticipate situations, assess risks, and decide which resources they want available. The inventory stops being merely a logistical issue and becomes a strategic one.

## Dialogues do not need to be choice trees

One of the most established conventions in RPGs is the idea that talking means choosing a response. The character speaks, the game presents several alternatives, and the player decides which one best represents what they would like to say. In more sophisticated games, these choices can alter relationships, open different paths, or produce different consequences. Even so, there is a structural limitation to this approach: the player continues choosing among phrases that were previously written by the designer. The dialogue tree can be small or enormous, but it remains a tree. If the solution the player imagined is not among the available options, it simply does not exist.

This does not mean dialogue trees are necessarily bad. They are an efficient tool for controlling narrative, character personality, and scene pacing, as well as allowing the player to participate directly in story decisions. The problem is confusing the quantity of options with freedom of communication. A game can offer dozens of different responses and still limit the player to possibilities anticipated during development. Variety increases, but the principle remains the same: the player chooses what to say within a closed set of possibilities.

The Elder Scrolls is a good example of how this idea can be expanded without necessarily abandoning the traditional model. Games such as Skyrim allow the player to develop different skills, have different relationships with characters, and use their reputation or knowledge to access new possibilities. Conversations can produce different results depending on context and character traits. Even so, when it is time to speak, the player remains limited to the responses the game has placed on the screen. The system recognizes different conditions, but communication itself remains based on predefined choices.

But what if conversation with an NPC were actually a mechanic? Instead of choosing a ready-made sentence, the player could define an intention, a tone, and a short phrase. The system would interpret these elements considering the character's abilities, the context of the conversation, the NPC's knowledge, and the existing relationship between the two. The goal would not be to allow the player to write anything and expect the game to produce a perfect response, but to turn communication into a systemic interaction with its own rules.

A threat could make the NPC call the guards, attempt to negotiate, or retreat, depending on their personality, relationship with the player, and the credibility of that threat. An attempt at negotiation could work better depending on the character's reputation, persuasion ability, and what they have to offer. A rude statement could damage a relationship, while a respectful approach could build trust. An attempt at flirting could be received differently depending on the level of intimacy between the characters, the NPC's personality, and the context of the situation. The same text could produce different consequences when used with different people.

In this model, the character's abilities would remain important, but would not need to determine exactly what the player can say. A character with high persuasion ability could obtain better results when negotiating, while someone without that skill could struggle even when choosing an apparently appropriate approach. Likewise, knowledge acquired during the adventure could alter the possibilities of a conversation. Knowing that a particular NPC lost a family member, discovering compromising information, or knowing about a rivalry between two families could offer the player new ways to approach a situation without the game needing to present an option called “use information about the NPC's past”.

The point would not simply be to generate infinite dialogue through artificial intelligence. The goal would be to turn communication into gameplay. The player would have to decide what they want to achieve, assess who they are talking to, choose an approach, and accept the risk that the approach may produce an unexpected consequence. Just as in a combat system the player does not need to choose from a closed list of possible attacks, but can combine movement, positioning, equipment, and timing to construct their own action, a conversation could operate through intentions and rules that allow different outcomes.

This would also reduce dependence on traditional dialogue trees. Instead of choosing among four sentences written by the designer, the player could express an intention and observe how the system reacts. The difference is fundamental. In a traditional tree, the player discovers which possibilities the designer prepared. In a more open system, the player tries to discover which possibilities exist within the rules of that world. The question stops being “which of these responses should I choose?” and becomes “how can I convince this person to do what I want?”.

This would also create more room for creativity and experimentation. If the player knows that NPCs have their own goals, personalities, relationships, and information, they can begin testing different approaches to discover how they react. The same situation could be resolved through negotiation, threats, persuasion, bribery, manipulation, or simply by building a relationship of trust over time. The game would not need to present all these alternatives as explicit options. It would only need to build a system in which they are possible.

The mission would then stop being a sequence of instructions and become an open-ended problem. The NPC would not need to tell the player exactly what to do. They would have a problem, goal, or need, and it would be up to the player to discover how to deal with that situation. The difference between a traditional mission and a systemic mission would not necessarily lie in the amount of written content, but in the number of solutions that can emerge from the game's rules. Instead of asking which option the designer imagined for that situation, the player could ask what they themselves can do.

## The protagonist does not need to be born important

Another very common convention in games is the idea that the protagonist has some form of special importance from the beginning. Even when they start as an apparently ordinary person, it does not take long for them to discover that they are descended from someone important, possess a rare power, belong to a forgotten lineage, or are connected to a prophecy. The narrative needs to find a justification for why that particular character deserves to be at the center of the story. This structure works and can produce excellent stories, but it also creates a curious relationship between the player and the world: before doing anything, the character already has a destiny reserved for them. Their importance is a premise of the story, rather than a consequence of their actions.

An alternative would simply be to have no destiny. The player could start as a completely ordinary person, with no special lineage, prophecy, title, or privileged position. They could be a peasant, merchant, low-ranking soldier, artisan, or any other person of no particular importance within society. At the beginning, no one would have any reason to know them or care about their decisions. Their journey would begin exactly where most people in that world begin: with few resources, little influence, and a limited set of possibilities. The difference is that, unlike a protagonist destined to become important, this character would need to build their own importance.

This construction could happen through different systems that reinforce one another. Their achievements could generate reputation. Reputation could facilitate business. Business could generate wealth. Wealth could allow investments in properties, workshops, caravans, or expeditions. These investments could increase their economic influence and create new political opportunities. The player could conquer territory, finance soldiers, establish alliances, take on administrative responsibilities, or simply become so important to a particular region that their decisions began to affect other people. Over time, they could go from being unknown to becoming an owner, merchant, magistrate, adviser, baron, or lord. Not because a mission determined that this would be their next title, but because their actions produced the conditions necessary for that title to make sense.

This would allow social progression to work similarly to economic progression. The player would not simply receive a reward for completing a mission. They could gradually build a position within society. A merchant who became wealthy could finance a caravan and gain control of an important route. A soldier who gained recognition in conflicts could be invited to command troops. An owner who invested in a region could acquire enough political influence to participate in local decisions. An explorer who discovered a valuable area could establish a settlement and turn that discovery into a permanent source of wealth. The same action could generate consequences across different systems, making the character's rise seem like a natural consequence of their trajectory.

The narrative could also recognize this transformation without constantly announcing it. The game would not need to interrupt the story to inform the player that they received ten more reputation points or reached the level required to become a noble. The inhabitants themselves could begin treating them differently. Merchants could offer better terms because they know who the player is. Nobles could request their presence at meetings. Rulers could seek their support. Guards could recognize their authority. Ordinary people could ask for help or appeal to their influence to solve problems. Enemies could begin to consider them a political threat. The most important change would not be on an attribute screen, but in the way the world reacts to the character's presence.

This would also create an interesting difference between fame and power. A character could be extremely famous without possessing wealth or political authority. Another could control large properties and trade routes while being practically unknown outside their region. A third could have little wealth but exercise enormous influence over a particular faction. The player could choose what kind of importance they want to build, rather than simply following a single progression path. Social advancement would stop being a staircase with predefined steps and become a combination of reputation, resources, relationships, territory, and power.

This approach would also allow failure and loss to be part of the journey. A character who accumulated wealth could lose their businesses during an economic crisis. A noble could lose territory after a war. A family could break an important alliance. A political decision could destroy a reputation built over years. This would not necessarily mean the player lost all progress, but that their position within the world is something that needs to be preserved and managed. If advancement is a consequence of the player's actions, decline can be as well. The world stops automatically recognizing the protagonist's importance and starts requiring them to continue producing reasons to be important.

This progression could also transform the narrative itself. At first, perhaps the player is concerned only with earning enough money to survive. Then they begin managing a business. Later, they need to protect their properties, negotiate with authorities, and resolve conflicts between people who depend on their decisions. Eventually, they may become involved in political disputes that would have been completely inaccessible to someone without their previous trajectory. The story does not need to tell the player that they have advanced to a new chapter. The set of problems they have begun facing demonstrates that their position has changed.

The most interesting part is that different players could finish the same experience in completely different positions. One could become a powerful landowner. Another could build a fortune through trade. Another could gain military prestige and assume a command position. Another could remain relatively poor, but build enough relationships and influence to become a respected figure in their community. None of them would need to be the chosen one. None would need to fulfill a prophecy. They could all simply have built different lives from the possibilities offered by the world.

The protagonist would not be important because the story decided they should be. They would be important because the world would have changed in response to what they did. This difference seems small, but it completely changes the relationship between player, character, and narrative. Instead of following the story of someone who was destined to change the world, the player would have the opportunity to discover how far their own decisions can take them.

## The rival can be another protagonist

One of the most interesting characters to consider for this idea is Blue, from Pokémon. He is not simply an enemy who appears to stop the player. He is a rival. Like the protagonist, he wants to become the best trainer, catches Pokémon, challenges gyms, explores the world, and seeks recognition. The difference is that he is doing all of this at the same time as the player. When the two meet, there is a sense that they are following parallel paths toward the same goal. The player is not facing someone who exists merely to be defeated. They are competing with another person who has ambitions of their own.

This idea could be taken much further in an open world. A dynamic rival could function as a second protagonist, with their own goals, resources, relationships, and opportunities. They would have their own trajectory and grow in the same world as the player. If the player ignored a threat, the rival could solve the problem. If a dungeon had an important reward, they could arrive first. If a region were politically unstable, they could take advantage of the situation and seize control. If a commercial opportunity arose, they could invest before the player. Their achievements would not be determined by the protagonist's progress, but by the same conditions that exist for the player.

This would create an important difference from traditional rivals. In many games, the rival appears only when the narrative needs to create a confrontation. They exist because the script needs someone to face the protagonist, and their progress is usually controlled by the story. A truly systemic rival would need to have their own agenda. They could make decisions, make mistakes, miss opportunities, build alliances, accumulate resources, and change strategy according to what happens in the world. The player would not necessarily know where they are or what they are doing. They could simply discover, some time later, that this character was involved in events the player themselves chose to ignore.

The competition would not need to happen only through combat. The rival could compete for businesses, properties, trade routes, discoveries, political positions, territories, and prestige. They could try to buy a property the player wanted, establish a workshop in a strategic city, or win the support of an influential family. They could discover a region before the player and become known for that discovery. They could finance an expedition, take control of a trade route, or build a military reputation that made the protagonist's rise more difficult. Competition would stop being a series of mandatory battles and start existing in practically every system available to the player.

The most interesting part is that the rival would not necessarily need to be better. They could simply be different. Perhaps they are more aggressive and take risks the player avoids. Perhaps they are a better merchant but a worse fighter. Perhaps they have excellent political relationships but few resources. Perhaps they make a disastrous decision and lose much of what they built. What matters is that their decisions have real consequences. If they have access to the same opportunities as the player, then their success or failure needs to be the result of their own choices, rather than a narrative function determining when they should appear.

This would create a rare feeling in games: that the world truly continues to function without the protagonist. The player could ignore an opportunity without it remaining frozen until they are ready to take advantage of it. Someone else could arrive first. A rival family could gain a position the player wanted. A character could become known for a discovery the player did not make. A company could grow because the player chose to invest somewhere else. A region could change politically while the protagonist was occupied elsewhere on the map.

At some point, the rival could even become more famous and admired than the protagonist. They could be celebrated for an achievement the player missed, receive a title the player wanted, or become an important figure within society. The player could encounter people telling stories about them, hear merchants discussing their businesses, or discover that a particular region is now under their influence. This would be especially interesting because it would prevent the protagonist from being treated as the inevitable center of that universe.

The relationship between the two would also not need to be permanently hostile. The rival could be a friend, a respectful competitor, an occasional ally, or someone whose relationship with the protagonist changes over the years. At certain moments, they could compete for the same opportunity. At others, they could cooperate against a greater threat. They could exchange information, do business, or even recognize each other's merits. The rivalry would be a consequence of the fact that both have similar goals, not a justification for turning one character into an enemy.

At its limit, the rival could function as a kind of mirror of the player. After dozens of hours, it would be possible to look at their trajectory and realize that another character made completely different decisions when faced with the same opportunities. Perhaps the player built wealth while the rival built political influence. Perhaps one conquered territories while the other became famous for discoveries. Perhaps both started from practically the same place and ended in completely different positions. The comparison would stop being merely a scoring mechanic and become part of the world's own emergent story.

That is what makes the concept so interesting. The rival does not need to exist to defeat the player. They can exist to prove that the player is not the only protagonist in that world. Just as Blue is following his own journey while the player follows theirs, a systemic rival could transform an open world into a place where other people also have ambitions, make decisions, and accomplish things. For the first time, missing an opportunity would not simply mean leaving a mission incomplete. It could mean that someone got there first.

## The calendar can also be a mechanic

Many games have day-night cycles, seasons, or some representation of the passage of time, but relatively few use the calendar to create a society that truly has routine, memory, and continuity. In many cases, time exists primarily to alter lighting or control certain systems. The world dawns, night falls, and eventually the next day arrives, but almost nothing really changes because a day has passed. An alternative would be to turn the calendar into a social mechanic, making certain events exist independently of the player's presence.

Weddings, harvest festivals, fairs, religious ceremonies, funerals, tournaments, and celebrations could take place on specific dates every year. The player could participate, ignore, or influence some of these events, but they would continue to exist because they are part of that society's culture. A city could have an annual festival that attracts merchants from different regions. A family could hold a traditional ceremony at a certain time of year. A tournament could bring together warriors from various territories. The player would not be responsible for making the event exist. They would simply live in a world where these things already happen.

This would create an important difference from traditional game events. Instead of a festival existing because the player arrived in the city and activated a quest, the player would arrive in the city and discover that the festival is taking place at that moment. If they were in another region, the event would happen without them. If they decided to return the following year, they could find a different celebration because of the consequences of previous events. The calendar would stop being merely a tool for organizing the player's content and begin to represent how society functions.

This continuity would also allow the creation of a collective memory. A wedding held two years earlier could still be remembered by the inhabitants. A war could change the way a particular celebration takes place. A family could stop participating in a festival after a tragedy. A city could create a new tradition after surviving a major crisis. A character could comment that a certain ceremony was different when their parents were young. Small changes like these would make the world seem to have a history that did not begin when the player appeared.

The player could also become part of this memory. Participating repeatedly in a tournament could cause their name to become associated with the competition. Financing a festival could alter its scale. Preventing a war could change a celebration that, in another timeline, would have been marked by mourning. Building a property could cause a particular family to start frequenting a region where they had not previously been present. The calendar would not merely be something the player observes. Their actions could modify what happens when a particular date returns.

This would also allow time to have consequences that do not depend on missions. An opportunity could exist only during a certain time of year. A particular species could appear during a specific season. A trade route could become impractical during winter. A ceremony could bring together characters who are normally never in the same place. A political event could happen while the player is occupied in another region. The value of a date would come to depend on what happens during that period, rather than merely on the number displayed on the calendar.

Natural disasters could follow a similar logic, but looking to the past rather than the future. In many games, earthquakes, floods, eruptions, and fires appear as scripted events, usually used to create spectacle or temporarily alter the environment. A different approach would be to have major catastrophes already occurred before the player's arrival and left permanent marks on the world. The disaster would not need to happen before the player's eyes to be important. Its consequences could be present everywhere.

An earthquake could have created a mountain range and separated regions that were previously connected. A flood could have destroyed a city and turned its ruins into a navigable area. An eruption could have permanently altered the soil, created new volcanic regions, and displaced trade routes. A fire could have completely transformed a forest, leaving burned areas where new species later emerged. A region could be rich in certain resources precisely because a catastrophe altered its geology decades or centuries earlier.

This turns geography into a kind of historical record. The player does not merely find mountains, rivers, forests, and cities. They find the consequences of events that occurred before their arrival. Ruins can indicate a destroyed civilization. A valley may exist because an ancient flood changed the course of a river. A forest may have grown over an area devastated by a fire. A city built in a dangerous region may exist because its inhabitants chose to remain there despite successive catastrophes.

The advantage of this model is that the world stops looking like a collection of biomes built to distribute content. Geography itself begins to tell stories. The player can find a region and ask why it is the way it is, investigate what happened, and discover that its current appearance is a consequence of historical events. Exploration stops being merely a search for points of interest and also becomes an attempt to understand how that world became what it is.

Calendar and natural disasters therefore work in different but complementary directions. The calendar shows that the world continues to happen after the player leaves. Natural disasters show that the world already existed before they arrived. Together, they help build an important feeling for any open world: that the protagonist is not the center of reality. They are entering a society with its own traditions, a geography shaped by previous events, and a world that will continue changing after they decide to move elsewhere.

## Personal relationships can be progression systems

There is another dimension that is frequently secondary in games: the protagonist's personal life. Family members, friends, and close acquaintances often appear only as characters who give quests, participate in important narrative moments, or serve as motivation for the protagonist to continue their journey. Even when a game has relationship systems, they often function as a parallel progression, measured by an affinity bar or a sequence of dialogues that unlocks new rewards. The relationship exists, but rarely interferes deeply with the structure of the character's life.

An alternative would be to treat personal relationships as permanent systems, with consequences that span the entire journey. Friends, family members, partners, and other close people could offer advice, resources, protection, and opportunities, but could also create responsibilities, conflicts, and limitations. One person could help the protagonist negotiate a loan, another could provide information about a region, another could protect them during a crisis. At the same time, these relationships would require time, attention, and reciprocity. Constantly ignoring someone important could weaken the bond. Keeping a promise could strengthen trust. A decision made to benefit one person could harm another.

This would create a type of progression different from the one normally represented by levels, equipment, and abilities. The player could begin the adventure practically alone and, over the years, build a network of people who become part of their trajectory. A friend could become a political ally. A former colleague could open a business together. A family member could manage a property while the protagonist is traveling. A character who received help in the past could appear decades later to return the favor. Likewise, someone who was abandoned or betrayed could carry that decision throughout their life. The value of a relationship would not lie only in what it immediately unlocks, but in the possibilities it creates or eliminates in the future.

Memories could also function as part of this system. Instead of presenting the protagonist's past only through long dialogues or explanatory scenes, childhood and adolescence events could be revealed gradually as certain people, places, or situations are revisited. An old house could trigger a memory. A reunion with someone from childhood could reveal an unknown part of a past event. A decision made when the character was young could explain a relationship that continues influencing their life decades later. These memories would not need to exist only to explain who the protagonist is. They could help explain why certain people trust them, why others reject them, and why certain decisions carry different weight for that character.

This would also allow conflicts that cannot be resolved simply with money, combat, or the correct choice in a mission. The player could receive an extremely important professional opportunity precisely when a family member needs their presence. They could have the chance to participate in a lucrative expedition but need to decide whether to stay and accompany someone close to them during an illness or crisis. They could accept a political position that would increase their influence, knowing that this would require temporarily leaving their family. None of these choices would need to be presented as good or bad. The conflict would lie precisely in choosing which part of one's life deserves priority.

Consequences could accumulate over decades. An apparently small decision could affect a relationship much later. A broken promise could prevent a future alliance. A friendship preserved during a difficult moment could generate an unexpected opportunity years later. A character could raise their children in a particular way and discover that their choices influenced the next generation. A family conflict could affect inheritances, properties, and political alliances. The relationship would stop being a sequence of isolated events and begin to accompany the player throughout their entire trajectory.

This would also help differentiate personal relationships from traditional reputation systems. Reputation normally represents what a society thinks about the protagonist. A relationship represents what a specific person thinks and feels about them. The player could be publicly admired while having a family that considers them absent. They could be a powerful political leader while losing the trust of old friends. They could have a bad reputation among nobles and still be deeply respected by their community. These dimensions could coexist without being reduced to a single number.

The most interesting consequence would be giving the player something to lose that could not simply be recovered with more money or experience. In many games, missing an opportunity means merely missing a reward. If an important relationship is involved, missing an opportunity could mean losing a person, trust, or part of the character's own story. This creates a different kind of tension because the player begins making decisions not only with efficiency in mind, but also considering what kind of life they want to build.

The journey would then stop being exclusively about conquering territories, defeating enemies, or finding artifacts. It would also be about building and preserving relationships. The player would not simply be developing a more powerful character, but building a life with social and emotional consequences. One campaign could end with an extremely wealthy and powerful protagonist who is practically alone. Another could produce someone with little political influence but surrounded by a family and community they helped build. Both would be legitimate forms of progress.

The game would thus begin treating the character's life as part of the gameplay. Progression would not be only what increases the protagonist's attributes or unlocks new abilities. It could also be what they built with other people over time. And in this context, perhaps one of the most important decisions in the game is not which enemy to defeat or which equipment to buy, but for whom the protagonist is willing to dedicate their time.

## Magic can be a complete system

It is probably in magic that the difference between a list of abilities and a system becomes most evident. In many games, magic is essentially treated as a collection of spells. The player unlocks an ability, assigns it to a button or menu, and uses it when they have enough mana. Progression occurs when new spells are added to the list, while mana functions as a resource that simply limits how many times the player can use those abilities. The system works, but there is little relationship between magic and the world. The player has powers because the game has determined that they can have them, and those powers normally work in the same way regardless of where they are used, who uses them, or what consequences their use produces.

An alternative would be to build magic from rules that produce consequences of their own. Mana compatibility could be one such system. Each mage could be born with a specific compatibility, similar to the logic of blood groups, making certain sources of mana naturally more suitable for them than others. Using compatible mana would increase efficiency and reduce risks, while using incompatible mana could decrease performance, cause magical rejection, inflict damage, or even produce corruption effects. A mage would not simply have a mana bar that regenerates over time. They would need to understand where the energy they use comes from, which sources are compatible with their body, and what risks they are willing to take to gain more power.

This would immediately create a mana economy. Natural sources, stored reserves, specialized donors, and certain types of mana would have different values depending on who needs them. A city built near a rare source could become an economic center. A family capable of producing or storing a particular type of mana could acquire political power. A market could emerge around the collection, transportation, and trade of this energy. A rare mage with universal compatibility could be extremely valuable precisely because they could use practically any type of mana without suffering rejection. Magic would stop being merely a resource that regenerates and would acquire origin, compatibility, scarcity, and consequences.

The same philosophy could be applied to consumables that normally function as instant solutions. Stamina potions, for example, could stop simply restoring energy and instead temporarily mask fatigue. The character would be able to continue functioning beyond their natural limits, but the body would demand a price afterward. When the effect ended, there could be a period of increased exhaustion, reduced regeneration, and decreased performance. The player could use the potion to survive a critical situation, complete an urgent journey, or win a particularly difficult battle, but would know that they are bringing forward a physiological debt that will need to be paid later. The consumable would stop being merely a recovery button and become a decision.

Conjuration itself could also abandon menus. In a voice-based casting system, the player would use a microphone to pronounce magical words or combinations that the game would interpret. Different elements of language could determine the target, intensity, element, duration, or behavior of the spell. The player would not simply select an ability called fireball from a list. They would need to know a magical language, learn its components, and discover how to combine them. The voice would stop being merely a means of communication with the real world and become a gameplay interface.

The most interesting aspect of this approach is that it could turn knowledge into progression. The game would not necessarily need to grant a new ability every certain number of hours. The player could discover a new word, understand a combination, or realize a relationship between different magical elements. Two known words could produce a result the player had not yet experienced. A combination could work in one context and fail in another. Progression would occur because the player understood the system better, not merely because they received a new ability.

This opens the door to another possibility: living grimoires. Instead of functioning merely as books that store a list of spells, grimoires could automatically record the knowledge, experiences, mistakes, and discoveries of their owners. Over decades or centuries, each copy would become unique. A grimoire could record a technique developed by an ancient master, an attempt that ended in failure, a different interpretation of a particular ritual, or an adaptation created in a specific situation. Some could even develop a kind of personality based on the experiences accumulated by their former owners.

In this context, inheriting a grimoire would mean receiving part of the experience accumulated by previous generations. A new mage could consult an artifact containing centuries of knowledge, but would also need to interpret what they received. An ancient teaching could be incomplete. A technique might work only under certain conditions. A mistake recorded by an ancient master could prevent the next person from making the same error. Destroying a grimoire, on the other hand, would not merely mean losing a rare item. It would mean eliminating knowledge that might not be reproducible.

Magic could also alter the world's geography itself. If magic has its own rules, then the territory where it is practiced can also have magical properties. Mystical geography would allow certain places to be naturally more suitable for certain forms of magic. Ancient forests could strengthen natural magic. Ruins could amplify ancestral rituals. Mountains could concentrate certain types of energy. Battlefields could favor magic related to death or conflict. A location could be powerful not because it contains a rare physical resource, but because its history has made that territory particularly suitable for a particular type of ritual.

This would even change how magical societies organize themselves. Families and schools could carefully choose where to build their strongholds, taking into account the magical properties of the territory. Certain locations could be contested for centuries not because of gold, water, or fertile land, but because of the ritual value of their location. A city could have been built on an ancient sacred area precisely to control access to that source of power. An apparently insignificant region on the map could be one of the most strategically important territories in the magical world.

Magical saturation could take this idea even further. Excessive use of magic in a particular location could leave residual energy in the environment, accumulating changes until the region became unstable. Anomalies, distortions, creatures, or unpredictable phenomena could emerge as a consequence of excessive energy concentration. Ancient cities could bear magical scars left by wars. A region could have been permanently altered by a ritual performed centuries earlier. Mages could avoid repeatedly casting certain spells in the same location because they know the cumulative effects this produces.

In this model, a mage who uses magic indiscriminately would not merely be spending mana. They would be altering the territory. A battle could leave an area contaminated. A war could permanently modify a region. A civilization could have disappeared not only because of its enemies, but because it abused a source of power that ultimately destroyed the environment around it. The player would not simply be using the world as a backdrop for their abilities. Their actions could modify the very system that sustains those abilities.

Objects could also possess memory. Family heirlooms could emerge when objects continuously used by mages absorb small amounts of mana over generations and develop properties that cannot be artificially reproduced. A staff, ring, sword, or amulet could carry part of the history of its owners and acquire unique characteristics as it is used. The object would not be powerful merely because it has high attributes. It would be powerful because it has a history.

This creates a direct alternative to the traditional loot model, in which equipment is constantly replaced by numerically superior versions. Instead of abandoning a sword because they found another with five additional points of damage, the player could keep the same weapon for decades because it has developed properties over time and because there is a relationship between that object and their family. Equipment found at the beginning of the campaign could remain relevant at the end not because its numbers were artificially scaled, but because its history made it unique.

Losing a family heirloom would also have a different meaning. Losing ordinary equipment means losing attributes and needing to find a replacement. Losing a family heirloom would mean losing part of that family's history. An object could have been used by the protagonist's grandfather, carried during a war, and later passed on to the next generation. Recovering it could matter not only because it is powerful, but because its existence connects different moments in that character's history.

The magical economy could follow the same logic. Instead of determining the value of ingredients exclusively by their physical rarity, certain components could be valuable because of the symbolic meaning they carry. An object used during a historical event could be indispensable for a particular ritual. An ingredient associated with a particular person, family, or tragedy could possess properties that an ordinary substitute cannot reproduce. This would create an economy based not only on scarcity, but on meaning.

In this world, a merchant of magical ingredients could be just as important as a great mage. Explorers could search for historical objects. Collectors could compete for relics. Families could hide components that possess ritual value. An expedition might be necessary not to find a rare ore, but to recover a specific object that participated in a particular event decades earlier. The player could discover that they possess an apparently useless item and only later realize that it is a fundamental component of a ritual requiring exactly that object.

Even conflicts between mages could be transformed into systems. Ritualized duels could allow two participants to establish absolute rules before the fight. A magical circle could ensure that certain conditions are respected, preventing the use of certain schools of magic, restricting weapons, prohibiting certain actions, or establishing specific victory conditions. Instead of simply placing two characters in an arena and allowing them to use all available abilities, the confrontation itself could involve defining the rules that will limit the participants.

This would turn the duel into a strategic contest before it even begins. A mage could accept a limitation because they have an advantage within that particular set of rules. Another could attempt to negotiate more favorable conditions. The rules could reflect cultural traditions, local laws, or agreements between families. In certain societies, a ritualized duel could be the official method for resolving disputes, replacing conflicts that might otherwise result in larger wars.

The schools of magic themselves could be built around this logic. Instead of functioning simply as skill trees or universities offering different spells, they could represent traditions preserved by families across generations. Each school would have its own methods, philosophy, and techniques, functioning more like a martial arts tradition than an academic institution. The player would officially belong to a school and develop their magical identity within that lineage, potentially eventually becoming a master responsible for preserving its legacy.

Exchange between schools could continue to exist without destroying this identity. An experienced mage could temporarily study another tradition to acquire complementary knowledge, establish alliances, or develop new interpretations of magic. Even so, the deepest secrets would remain restricted to the original lineage. In this way, the world would have genuinely different schools, with their own methods and philosophies, while also allowing enough knowledge circulation for magic to evolve.

The result would be a structure in which magic stops being merely a list of abilities available to the character and becomes a set of connected systems. Compatibility determines who can use a particular energy. The economy determines how that energy is obtained. Geography determines where it works best. Saturation turns the use of magic into a force capable of altering the environment. Grimoires preserve knowledge. Relics preserve memory. Schools preserve tradition. Duels establish rules. Ingredients possess historical and symbolic value.

In this type of game, learning magic would not mean simply unlocking new spells. It would mean understanding a system. The player could discover that a particular ritual works better in a specific region, that a certain source of mana is incompatible with their character, that an ancient grimoire contains a forgotten technique, or that a family heirloom possesses a property no one else can reproduce. Power would stop being a collection of numbers and abilities and become something the player needs to study, experiment with, and understand.

This is precisely the kind of system I find most interesting when I think about innovation. There is no need to invent a spell that has never appeared in any other game. It is possible to take the idea of magic, which has existed practically since the beginning of video games, and ask what would happen if it actually obeyed the rules of a world. When a mechanic begins to possess origin, cost, context, memory, and consequences, it stops being merely a tool available to the player. It becomes part of the very structure of that universe.

## The economy can be more immersive

The economy in games is also usually extremely simplified. The player accumulates a universal currency and practically every merchant accepts the same unit of value, regardless of where they are, which kingdom they belong to, or the economic conditions at that moment. Gold is gold everywhere. A sword can cost the same number of coins in a wealthy city, an isolated village, or a territory devastated by war. This model is convenient for the player, but it also eliminates an important characteristic of real economies: the value of money depends on place, time, and the trust people place in it.

An alternative would be to make currency part of each region's identity. Each kingdom could have its own currency, produced from metals, precious stones, or other materials, and its value could vary according to the conditions of that territory. The player would not necessarily need to follow charts or consult a screen full of indicators. They could simply notice that a particular currency is more widely accepted in one region, that merchants are refusing a foreign currency, or that a war has altered confidence in a particular kingdom's money.

This would make traveling also mean moving through different economic systems. A currency that has great value in one kingdom could be of little use in another. A trading city could accept practically any currency, while an isolated community might prefer metals or physical resources. In a politically unstable region, merchants might avoid accepting the local currency and demand a form of payment considered more reliable. The player would begin to understand the economy not because the game provided an explanation of it, but because they had to deal with its consequences.

Minting could deepen this relationship. The value of a coin would depend not only on the material used to produce it, but also on the quantity in circulation, the kingdom's reserves, political stability, trade capacity, and trust in the authority responsible for issuing it. A war could reduce the value of a currency. A new trade route could increase demand for another. A political crisis could cause merchants to prefer precious metals. The player could perceive these changes through prices and merchant behavior without necessarily needing to see all the calculations behind the system.

This would also create situations in which money stops being merely a reward accumulated throughout the campaign. The player might need to decide what is worth carrying during a journey. A particular currency might be more useful in the next territory, while another could take up space unnecessarily. A small amount of ruby or another extremely valuable material could function as a store of value in a region where foreign currencies are not trusted. The player would not simply be carrying an abstract amount of wealth. They would be carrying different forms of wealth that have different uses depending on where they are.

The interesting thing is that this does not necessarily need to turn the game into an economic simulator. The goal would not be to force the player to track inflation, exchange rates, and financial indicators all the time. The complexity could remain hidden. The player would only need to perceive the consequences. A merchant could say that a particular currency has lost value. A city could begin accepting only payments in a certain metal. A war could alter prices. A new route could cause certain products to appear more frequently. The economy would be understood through experience, just as the player learns to navigate the world by observing its geography.

This could also make trading more interesting. Instead of buying items simply because they have better attributes, the player could discover that certain products are common in one region and extremely valuable in another. A city producing a particular resource could sell it cheaply, while a distant region where the same resource is rare would pay much more. The player could transport goods not because the game created a specific mission for it, but because they noticed an opportunity within the world itself.

In this context, a dungeon, a road, or a city could also acquire economic importance. A newly explored region could reveal valuable resources. A road considered dangerous could prevent goods from reaching a particular market. Building a bridge could reduce transportation costs and increase trade between two cities. An outpost created by the player could turn a previously insignificant area into an important transit point. The economy would begin responding to the transformation of the world.

The main change, therefore, would not be making the economic system more complex. It would be making money feel like it belongs to that world. When each region has its own currencies, when wars affect their value, when merchants have different preferences, and when trade depends on geography, the economy stops being merely an interface between the player and the shops. It becomes a characteristic of the place where the player is.

The player would not need to know exactly how much each currency is worth relative to all the others. They would only need to perceive that they are in a world where money does not have a universal and immutable value. The best currency to carry in one region might not be the best in another. The best way to preserve wealth during a war might differ from the one used in stable times. And, above all, the player could discover these things by playing, rather than receiving all the answers through an information screen.

In this model, the economy contributes to immersion precisely because it stops existing only to facilitate progression. Money begins to tell a story about the world. When a currency loses value, there is a reason. When a particular product becomes expensive, there is a reason. When a merchant refuses a certain payment, there is a reason. The economy stops being merely a system that rewards the player and becomes another way of communicating that the world has its own rules.

## Competitions can have different categories

Even ranking systems can be questioned. A single general scoreboard tends to place players with completely different playstyles, goals, and availability into the same competition. A player who dedicates a few hours per week has a different relationship with the game compared with someone who plays every day in an extremely competitive environment. Putting both on the same table and measuring them according to exactly the same criteria can turn the ranking into a competition that favors a single playstyle, rather than recognizing different forms of excellence.

An alternative would be to create multiple independent scoreboards, each with its own rules and criteria. A Casual mode could limit the daily time available for competition. A Hardcore mode could allow only one life, making every decision carry permanent consequences. A Cosmetic mode could eliminate competitive advantages and value exclusively appearance, customization, and player expression. A General mode could impose no restrictions and represent the most open experience possible. The player would choose which category they want to compete in before placing their character on that ranking.

Participation could be optional and the player could leave a competition whenever they wanted, but their character would be permanently removed from that scoreboard. This would create an important difference between simply activating a ranking option and committing to a particular set of rules. Competition would come to represent a conscious choice about how the player wants to play, rather than merely a consequence of being connected to the game.

This structure would also allow different forms of excellence to be recognized. One player could be extremely efficient in combat, while another could build a much more powerful economy. One could dedicate themselves to exploration, another to politics, and another to building a lineage that survives for generations. There would not necessarily be a single metric capable of representing all these achievements. The game could recognize that there are different ways to be exceptional within that world.

This could connect directly to the other mechanics presented throughout this text. One scoreboard could measure accumulated wealth. Another could consider political influence and controlled territory. Another could measure the longevity of a lineage. One could evaluate the amount of magical knowledge preserved. Another could track the reputation built over decades. Each ranking would transform a different dimension of the world into a form of competition.

The most interesting part is that these metrics would not necessarily need to be compatible with one another. A player could lead the economic ranking and be outside the top 100 in longevity. Another could have little wealth but have built the longest-lasting lineage on the server. A third might never have conquered large territories but could be recognized as the greatest master of a particular school of magic. The game would not need to turn these achievements into a single number to determine who is best.

This also changes the relationship between competition and identity. In many games, the ranking ends up defining a general hierarchy in which being higher simply means being better. With multiple competitions, the ranking begins to answer a more specific question: better at what? The player could choose a category that corresponds to the way they want to experience the world and compete within it without needing to accept that all other ways of playing are less relevant.

The system could even allow the characters themselves to carry these achievements as part of their history. A player recognized for their wealth could be known as a great merchant. Another could be remembered for exploring territories no one had reached. A particular family could be famous for the longevity of its lineage. Competition would stop existing only outside the world, in a table accessed by the player, and could produce recognition within the universe itself.

In the end, the game would not need to decide which of these things is most important. It would not need to state that wealth matters more than exploration, that combat is more important than politics, or that a long lineage represents a greater achievement than a major discovery. It could simply offer different forms of excellence and allow players to choose those they consider relevant.

The question would stop being merely who is in first place. It would become what it means to be in first place.

## The problem is not having too much content

Perhaps this is the most important conclusion of all. The problem with modern games is not necessarily that they have too much content. It is that they frequently have too much content and too few possibilities. A gigantic map is not necessarily a deep world. One hundred missions do not necessarily mean one hundred different experiences. Hundreds of weapons do not necessarily mean a complex combat system. A tree with fifty abilities does not necessarily mean the player has fifty relevant ways to play. It is perfectly possible to spend dozens of hours in a game and still realize that almost everything we did could be reduced to a small set of repeated interactions.

There is a fundamental difference between content and possibility. Content is what the designer produces. Possibility is what the player can produce through interaction between systems. A specific mission is content. A system that allows the player to solve problems in different ways produces possibilities. A dungeon built to be completed once is content. A dungeon that can be transformed into a trading outpost, contested by factions, or used to alter an economic route produces possibilities. A mission written to happen in a particular way depends on the designer's work to exist. A rival with their own objectives can produce situations that were not even individually anticipated.

It is precisely through interaction between systems that the number of possibilities begins to grow disproportionately relative to the amount of content produced. An investment economy can create situations that do not need to be written one by one. A dynamic rival can take a position the player intended to occupy. An economy based on different currencies can turn a war into a commercial opportunity. Magical saturation can modify a region in different ways depending on the amount of magic used there. A social calendar can make the same place completely different depending on the time of year. The more systems are capable of influencing one another, the less each possibility needs to be manually built by the developer.

This may be one of the greatest opportunities for game design today. The industry has the technical ability to create enormous worlds, but still builds much of its experiences as a sequence of predetermined content. The challenge should not merely be filling a larger map. It should be building systems capable of making that map produce different situations depending on what the player does. The most interesting scale of a game is not necessarily the number of square kilometers, missions, or campaign hours. It is the number of possibilities that emerge from the rules of that world.

## Modders realized this a long time ago

There is particularly interesting evidence that there is room for this kind of experimentation: the modding community itself. In many games, players create modifications to fix systems they find inconvenient, deepen mechanics they find superficial, remove elements they find unnecessary, or simply transform the experience into something closer to what they would like to play. Many of these modifications do not arise from an attempt to create a completely different game. They arise from a very simple question: what if this mechanic worked differently?

The phenomenon is interesting because the modder usually starts from exactly the position that should be extremely valuable to a developer. They are a player who has found a limitation in the existing experience and decided to invest time trying to solve it. Instead of merely complaining that a particular system could be better, they open the available tools, change the rules, and test the result. When a modification of this kind becomes popular, the community is providing a kind of informal design experiment. This does not mean that all players who use a mod represent the entire market, but it does mean that there is a segment sufficiently interested in a particular change to dedicate time and effort to it.

This behavior can be found in practically every type of system discussed in this text. Mods can alter interfaces, modify progression, transform economic systems, expand consequences, reduce markers, improve NPC behavior, reformulate combat, add new forms of interaction, and completely change the player's relationship with the world. In many cases, the goal is not to add content, but to remove or modify structures that were limiting the experience. The player did not necessarily want another hundred missions. They wanted the missions that already existed to work differently.

The industry could pay closer attention to this ecosystem. Developers and publishers naturally have constraints that a modder does not. A studio needs to consider budget, schedule, stability, support, accessibility, market, and an enormous number of risks that simply do not exist when one person modifies a game as a hobby. Even so, this does not diminish the value of the experiments conducted by the community. On the contrary. They can serve as signals of demand for certain experiences that would be difficult to identify through traditional research.

There is also an irony here. The industry frequently uses extremely sophisticated metrics to understand player behavior. It measures session time, completion rate, abandonment, purchases, retention, and countless other variables. At the same time, it may ignore one of the most explicit forms of feedback available: players dedicating hundreds of hours to voluntarily changing a game's rules because they believe it would be better in another way.

Modders are not necessarily better designers than professional teams, just as the popularity of a mod does not automatically mean its mechanic should be incorporated into the official game. But they represent a source of experimentation worth observing. They test ideas that might be considered too risky for a commercial production. Some experiments fail. Others work only for a niche. And some reveal that there is real demand for an experience the original game did not provide.

In this sense, the modding community can function as an innovation laboratory. Instead of asking only what content players want to receive, the industry could observe which rules players themselves are trying to change. This difference is important. Asking for more content means asking what we should add to the game. Observing mods means asking what exists in the game that players are trying to transform.

## The industry needs to keep experimenting

There is an understandable reason why the industry has arrived where it is. The larger a game's budget, the greater the risk associated with experimental decisions. Using familiar systems that have already proven to work in other productions is a rational choice. The problem arises when thousands of individually rational decisions collectively produce an increasingly conservative industry. No one needs to decide that every game should have XP, loot, crafting, fast travel, skill trees, or maps full of markers. It is enough for each team to independently conclude that these are good bets. The result is standardization without there necessarily being an official standard.

For this reason, perhaps the industry does not need to abandon the conventions it has created, but to question them again. Fast travel can be infrastructure. XP can represent learning. Reputation can be perceived through the world's behavior. Inventory can be a matter of preparation. Money can be investment. Equipment can accumulate history. Magic can transform the territory. A rival can have their own objectives. None of these ideas requires a completely unprecedented mechanic. What they require is a willingness to treat familiar systems as starting points, rather than definitive answers. Innovation often begins when someone asks why a mechanic needs to work exactly the way it has always worked.

In this sense, players and the modding community can be a particularly valuable source of experimentation. When someone modifies a game to alter its interface, progression, economy, combat, or interaction systems, they are demonstrating concrete dissatisfaction with the existing experience and trying to build an alternative. Not every mod represents a good direction for the industry, but many reveal that players do not necessarily want more content. Sometimes they want the content that already exists to work differently. Ignoring these experiments means ignoring one of the few environments in which new design ideas can be tested directly by the players themselves, without the need to justify their commercial viability in advance.

The industry does not need a mechanical revolution every generation. It needs to preserve a willingness to experiment, even when the answer is not yet known. The goal should not simply be to produce bigger, longer, or more system-filled games, but to create worlds in which player actions have room to produce possibilities that were not entirely determined in advance.
