# Companies Are Not Soccer Clubs, but Perhaps It Is Useful to Think of Them That Way

_In a country that breathes soccer, perhaps looking at companies as clubs can help us see culture, strategy, talent, training, and management from another angle._

**Summary:** I propose looking at companies as soccer clubs: today's result does not depend only on who is on the field, but on culture, structure, development, and relationships built over time. Therefore, hiring good professionals is not enough; it is necessary to build complementary teams, provide clarity about how to play, develop autonomy, and know when to preserve or break patterns. Managers must also create reasons for people to wear the shirt and build an environment in which talent, questioning, and development contribute to something greater than immediate performance. In the end, managing well is less about replacing players and more about building a club capable of continuing to evolve.

---

Over the past few weeks, I have followed some of the discussions about the Brazilian National Team and, especially, about Carlo Ancelotti's work. Amid analyses of lineups, performance, results, and the possibility of keeping or replacing the coach, I started thinking about how curious the way we usually look at soccer teams is. When something goes wrong, it is tempting to look for the person responsible. Replace the coach, hire other players, change the formation, and start over. Perhaps because it is easier to think that way. A culprit is easier to identify than a system. If the result did not come, someone needs to explain why, and replacing one person offers an immediate sense of action and change.

But a soccer team is much more complex than the coach standing in front of it. There is a history, a culture, players with different characteristics, a development structure, infrastructure, processes, decisions made long before that match, and a series of relationships that do not disappear when the coach leaves the position. A new coach can change the way the team plays, introduce new concepts, and draw different characteristics from the same players, but he does not receive an empty group. He receives a team that already has habits, relationships, expectations, strengths, and limitations built over time.

A company works in a similar way. Replacing a manager may be necessary, just as replacing an employee may be necessary, but neither decision, in isolation, rebuilds a team. The new manager also does not start with a blank sheet. He finds an established culture, processes that already exist, systems that support operations, people who have already developed relationships and ways of working, decisions made by previous management teams, and an organizational architecture that conditions what can be done. Perhaps that is precisely why thinking of companies as soccer clubs is an interesting analogy: in both cases, the result we see today is merely the manifestation of a structure that began to be built long before the match.

## Hiring Players Is Not the Same as Building a Squad

A century-old club is not just the team that takes the field that season. There is a stadium, a training center, youth academies, support staff, hiring processes, development mechanisms, and a history that began long before the current players arrived. There is also an identity that allows different generations of players to pass through the club without it ceasing to be recognized as the same institution. It is possible to replace practically the entire squad and still recognize that club.

This happens because the team we see on the field is only the most visible part of a much larger structure. Before a player puts on the shirt, there is an entire system that allows him to get there, be developed, trained, and integrated into the group. Hiring is only one of the stages. The player needs to find a position in which he can contribute, understand how the team plays, develop relationships with his teammates, and acquire enough repertoire to make decisions on the field. A squad is not merely a collection of contracts. It is a group of people that needs to function within a given culture.

In companies, the logic is similar. It is easy to reduce team building to hiring people, especially because hiring is one of the few parts of this process that appears in a very concrete way. A position is opened, candidates are evaluated, someone is chosen, and that person becomes part of the organization. But hiring is only the beginning. From there, there is onboarding, development, definition of responsibilities, training, relationships with other areas, access to tools and information, and, above all, the gradual construction of a common way of working.

Culture is precisely this common way of working. It is not merely a set of values written on a wall or presented during onboarding. It is the set of behaviors that an organization develops and reinforces over time. It is reflected in how a team reacts when something goes wrong, how a difficult decision is made, how much room there is for disagreement, how people treat someone who has just arrived, which behaviors are recognized, and which are tolerated. It is also found in what no one needs to explain anymore because everyone already knows how things work.

In soccer, a player who joins a club needs to learn much more than the names of his teammates and the position in which he will play. He needs to understand what that club expects from its players. He needs to perceive when he should accelerate a play, when he should recover his position, when he can improvise, and when he needs to respect the structure. Some of these things can be taught formally, but many are absorbed by watching other players, spending time with the coaching staff, and participating in the club's daily life. After some time, that stops feeling like an external rule and becomes part of the way the player understands the game.

Organizational culture works in a similar way. The more mature it is, the less it needs to turn every behavior into an explicit rule. People develop shared references that allow them to make decisions without having to consult a manual for every situation. This is particularly important in large and complex organizations, in which it is impossible to create sufficiently detailed processes to anticipate every situation that may occur.

This is where culture stops being an abstract subject and begins to have a direct impact on performance. Two teams can have equally competent professionals, similar tools, and identical objectives and still produce very different results because they make decisions in different ways. One team may have the freedom to experiment, while another avoids any risk. One may treat a mistake as an opportunity to learn, while another immediately looks for someone to blame. One may share information because it understands that the result belongs to the team, while another may withhold information because its members have learned that knowledge is power.

None of these characteristics necessarily appears on an organizational chart. Even so, they profoundly influence how the organization works.

Therefore, when someone joins a company, they are not merely occupying a position. They are entering a system of behaviors, expectations, and relationships that already existed before their arrival. The question is not only whether that person has the skills required to perform their role, but also whether they can understand that system and, eventually, contribute to transforming it. Likewise, when a company hires someone, it is not merely acquiring productive capacity. It is also introducing a new influence into the existing culture.

That is why culture should not be confused with homogeneity. A healthy team does not need everyone to think the same way. On the contrary, different perspectives can be one of the greatest sources of a team's advantage. What needs to exist is a common foundation strong enough for different people to work together. A player can have a different style, a different personality, or a different way of seeing a play without failing to understand what the team is playing for.

Perhaps this is one of the most important functions of culture: allowing diversity without turning diversity into lack of coordination. The goal is not to make everyone play the same way, but to make everyone understand what they are trying to build together.

## The Best Players Do Not Make the Best Team

A soccer team does not need eleven superstars. In fact, perhaps there is nothing more misguided than imagining that the best team would be the one with the best possible player in every position. Real Madrid's Galácticos are an interesting example. Bringing together players such as Figo, Zidane, Ronaldo, and Beckham seemed, in theory, like a strategy that would be difficult to get wrong. Even so, the concentration of individual talent did not produce proportional superiority as a team.

Brazil in 1986 offers another perspective. It was a national team full of extraordinary players, with names such as Zico, Sócrates, Falcão, Júnior, and Careca, but it was eliminated in the quarterfinals. Talent was not lacking. What was missing was not necessarily a better player. Perhaps the example is precisely this: at a certain level, adding individual talent is no longer enough to increase the quality of the team.

Soccer does not work like a spreadsheet of attributes. Players occupy spaces, have different characteristics, share responsibilities, and depend on one another. A striker needs someone who can find him in a position to finish. A full-back needs to know when to move forward because someone is covering his space. A midfielder can be excellent with the ball, but his contribution also depends on who is beside him. A player's value lies not only in what he can do alone, but also in what his presence allows others to do.

This applies to companies. A good team is not necessarily the one that brings together the best professionals available, but the one that brings together complementary professionals. Some people are excellent specialists, others can connect different areas, some are better at execution, others at structuring problems, some are extremely creative, others are excellent at turning ideas into processes that work. The goal should not be to maximize the individual quality of each position in isolation, but to find a combination capable of producing the best collective result.

We do not need a Pelé or a Messi for every position. Besides probably not being enough of them, it would be a rather expensive way to discover that individual talent does not replace complementarity.

There is also a more difficult issue: every talent comes with trade-offs. There is no exceptional player without particular characteristics, and those same characteristics that create value in certain situations can create difficulties in others. Romário is a good example. He was an extraordinary player and had personality and behavioral characteristics that required a particular management approach. This does not diminish his ability on the field, but it also does not make the cost of working with someone like that irrelevant.

The same happens in companies. A technically exceptional professional may be difficult to work with. Another may produce slightly less individually but raise the performance of those around them. One may be extremely creative and unpredictable. Another may be disciplined and consistent. One may question almost everything. Another may execute exactly what was agreed upon. None of these characteristics is necessarily good or bad in itself. Their value depends on the context, the position occupied, and the needs of the team.

That is why hiring excellent people should not be confused with building an excellent team. Management also involves understanding the trade-offs of each person, discovering how their characteristics combine with those of others, and deciding which combinations make sense for the strategy the organization has chosen. Sometimes, the most valuable professional is not the one who produces the most alone, but the one whose presence makes the entire team perform better.

A team is not the sum of the individual capabilities of its members. There is something that emerges from the way those capabilities combine. And it is precisely this difference between individual talent and collective capability that makes building a good squad so difficult.

## Strategy Is Choosing How to Play

In soccer, strategy is deciding how we want to play. It is choosing what characteristics we want the team to have, how we intend to occupy the field, where we want to create an advantage, and which risks we are willing to take. Tactics are about turning that intention into concrete decisions in the face of a match that never happens exactly as designed. Strategy establishes a direction; tactics interpret that direction in light of circumstances.

A team may decide to press high, for example. That decision says a lot about how the team intends to play, but it does not determine exactly what each player will do during the ninety minutes. The opponent may play out through the flank, find a space through the middle, change the speed of the play, or simply do something that was not anticipated during preparation. At some point, someone on the field will need to interpret the situation and decide. The player needs to know whether to keep pressing, drop back, close a space, or track an opponent. No coach can be inside every player's head throughout the entire match.

This is precisely why strategy cannot be confused with a detailed script. The more complex the environment, the greater the number of situations that cannot be anticipated. A very detailed strategy may seem safe on paper, but quickly becomes useless when it encounters a reality different from the one used to construct it. The role of strategy is to create principles that continue to make sense even when circumstances change.

Therefore, a good team does not train only movements. It trains principles, fundamentals, and situations. The player needs to understand not only where he should be, but why he should be there. He needs to understand what the team is trying to do so that he can adapt his behavior when something deviates from the plan. A player who merely memorizes a play knows how to execute a sequence. A player who understands the game model can interpret the match.

This difference matters because soccer, like work, is full of situations in which there is no predefined answer. The player receives a ball in an unexpected position, realizes that his teammate is marked, and needs to decide within seconds whether to move forward, pass backward, or attempt an individual play. There is no time to consult the coach. The quality of the decision depends on the repertoire that was built before that moment.

Something similar should happen in companies. Training should not serve only to teach employees to follow instructions, but to develop enough repertoire for them to make good decisions when instructions are not sufficient. Processes are important, policies are important, and standards are important, but no process can predict every situation a complex organization will encounter.

There is a difference between teaching someone to perform a task and teaching someone to understand the work they are doing. In the first case, the organization creates dependency. Whenever a new situation arises, someone will need to say what to do. In the second, the organization develops autonomy. The employee can interpret the context, evaluate the alternatives, and make a decision consistent with what the company is trying to achieve.

This is also why good managers should not try to make every decision. Their role is to create enough clarity about the strategy so that decisions can be distributed throughout the organization. If every small decision needs to move up the hierarchy, perhaps the problem is not a lack of control, but a lack of context.

In soccer, a coach cannot enter the field to correct every play. He needs to prepare the players so that they can solve problems without him. In companies, a manager should also not be the decision point for everything that happens around them. If the team needs to ask the manager what to do in every new situation, perhaps it is following instructions, but it is not yet really playing.

## Haste Is Not Performance

A team that runs more does not necessarily play better. A team that accelerates every play does not necessarily control the match. Sometimes the best decision is to accelerate. Sometimes it is to slow down, reorganize the team, and wait for the right moment. There is an important difference between playing quickly and playing well, just as there is a difference between being busy and producing results.

In soccer, fundamentals remain important even when the game demands speed. A player needs to control the ball, occupy space correctly, understand when to move forward and when to recover his position, and know where his teammates and opponents are. None of these things becomes unnecessary because the game is faster. On the contrary. The greater the speed of the match, the more important the fundamentals become, because there is less time to correct a bad decision.

In companies, sometimes the opposite seems to happen. Pressure for speed can cause fundamentals to be treated as bureaucracy. Documentation starts to be seen as slowness, planning as excessive caution, review as a lack of agility, testing as an obstacle, and processes as something that should be eliminated whenever possible. In the name of speed, organizations may begin to give up precisely the practices that allow speed to be sustainable.

It is easy to confuse this with performance because the results appear quickly. A project delivered ahead of schedule looks like a victory. A decision made within a few hours looks efficient. A team that responds immediately to every demand looks agile. But speed is only an advantage when it is associated with the correct direction. Delivering something quickly that will need to be redone is not necessarily more efficient. Making a decision quickly without gathering enough information may simply bring the error forward. Reducing steps can increase execution speed while simultaneously increasing the cost of maintenance, rework, or correction.

In soccer, a team can even win a match while playing in a disorganized way. A late goal can hide problems that will appear in the next match. The same applies to companies. An exceptional result in a given period does not necessarily mean that the system is working well. It is possible to achieve great performance despite poor processes, excessive dependence on certain people, or a routine that requires unsustainable effort from the team.

There is also a difference between speed and intensity. A team can play with great intensity without turning every decision into a race. It can press the opponent but know when to stop. It can attack quickly but also control possession. It can accelerate a play because it has identified an opportunity and then slow down because it realizes there is no space. Intelligence lies precisely in knowing when to do each thing.

A mature organization should work in the same way. Not every demand needs to be treated as urgent, just as not every decision needs to be made immediately. There are situations in which speed is a clear competitive advantage and others in which a few additional minutes, hours, or days of analysis prevent weeks of rework. Knowing how to distinguish these situations is part of management capability itself.

Perhaps the problem is not in seeking speed, but in turning speed into an objective. When delivering faster becomes more important than delivering well, fundamentals begin to look like obstacles. When responding immediately becomes more valued than responding correctly, thinking before acting begins to look like slowness. When the organization measures only what happens in the short term, everything that sustains long-term performance risks being treated as a cost.

In soccer, fundamentals are not what we do when we are not in a hurry. They are precisely what allows a team to continue performing when the match becomes difficult. In companies, perhaps we should think in the same way.

## The Disruptive Player

Perhaps this is where one of the most interesting characteristics of any team appears: the disruptive player. Modern soccer has become extremely sophisticated, with increasingly structured tactical systems, data analysis, physical preparation, and detailed study of opponents. The more sophisticated the system, the greater the temptation to turn each player into someone predictable, capable of doing exactly what was planned. After all, the more predictable each individual's behavior, the easier it seems to coordinate the whole.

In companies, there is a similar tension. Processes, metrics, methodologies, and structures are necessary to coordinate complex organizations, but there is a risk when predictability becomes confused with excellence. An organization can become so efficient at executing what it already knows that it loses the ability to perceive when it should do something different.

The professional who questions a decision may be seen as someone who is troublesome. The one who insists on asking questions may seem like someone who creates problems. The one who proposes a different solution may be interpreted as someone who does not adhere well to the process. In certain environments, the word “disruptive” even comes to be used almost as a synonym for someone who simply refuses to accept the rules of the game.

But there is another possibility. Perhaps that person is seeing something that the others have not yet seen.

The important difference lies in the reason why someone breaks with the pattern. There is the player who abandons his position because he does not want to follow the system, and there is the one who abandons his position because he realizes that, in that specific situation, following the system would produce a worse result. There is the employee who questions because he does not want to do what was agreed upon, and there is the one who questions because he believes there is a better way to achieve the objective everyone shares.

From the outside, the two may look the same. Both are going against the standard. The difference lies in the understanding behind the decision.

The disruptive player is not necessarily the one who does things differently. It is the one who understands the system well enough to know when doing things differently may generate more value than simply following the standard. He knows the strategy, understands his role within the team, and precisely because of that can recognize situations in which the literal application of what was planned no longer makes sense.

This also means that not every break with convention is innovation. Sometimes the player who abandons his position has simply stopped fulfilling his role. Sometimes the employee who questions a process is merely creating resistance. Disruption without purpose is simply lack of coordination. The difference between the two lies in the ability to connect the exception to the team's larger objective.

There is an important difference between the player who does not follow the system because he does not want to play for the team and the one who understands the system so well that he can recognize when he needs to do something different. The first is indiscipline. The second can be intelligence.

Perhaps that is why one of the best references for this discussion comes precisely from Batman, from Christopher Nolan. There is the idea of the hero who is not necessarily the one the city would like to have, but who may be precisely the one it needs.

In organizations, the most valuable professional may also, at certain moments, be the one who seems least convenient. Not because they are always right, but because they are capable of seeing what others are not seeing. A mature organization should not eliminate questioning, but learn to distinguish the professional who is simply resisting the game from the one who is trying to show that the game has changed.

## Not Every Player Is at the Same Point

There are also different types of players, and perhaps this is one of the greatest challenges of management. A team is made up of people who are not necessarily at the same professional stage or in the same phase of life. Two people with similar skills can perform completely differently depending on the context in which they are placed. What works for one may not work for another, and what one person can deliver today may be different from what they will be able to deliver a few years from now.

There is the player in a slump, who still has the ability but is not delivering their best performance at that moment. A slump does not necessarily erase the talent that exists there. Sometimes it is necessary to understand what changed, give the player time to recover, or simply find a different way to use that player. In soccer, putting a player on the bench for a few matches may be a better decision than concluding that he is no longer capable of playing. In companies, not every decline in performance should immediately be interpreted as a loss of capability.

There is also the player who is out of position. He may be competent, committed, and even achieve excellent results, but be asked to demonstrate characteristics that do not correspond to his main strengths. A good midfielder will not necessarily be a good full-back simply because he understands soccer. Likewise, an excellent technical specialist may not be the best person to lead a team. Sometimes the problem is not with the player, but with the position in which we have decided to place him.

There is the rookie, who still needs training, experience, and playing time. Perhaps he makes mistakes that an experienced professional would not make, but that does not necessarily mean he has less potential. There is a difference between not knowing how to do something and not being capable of learning how to do it. An organization that expects every new employee to immediately deliver the same result as someone who has spent years building experience is confusing experience with competence.

There is also the player who is in a different phase of their own life. People do not come to work like machines that can deliver exactly the same capacity for decades. Their priorities, responsibilities, interests, ambitions, and availability change. A professional may be at a point of accelerating their career, while another may be seeking stability. Someone may be willing to take on greater risks and responsibilities, while another person may be more interested in deepening their specialization. None of these choices, in itself, determines the quality of the professional.

This does not mean that every decline in performance should be justified by context. Management is not about finding an explanation for everything. It is precisely about understanding the context in order to make a better diagnosis. In some cases, the person needs training. In others, they need a change of position. In others, they need time. And in some cases, the problem really is lack of commitment.

That last case also needs to be handled differently. There is a difference between someone who does not yet know how to do something, someone who cannot do it in that context, and someone who simply does not want to do it. The first may need training. The second may need a change. The third may have chosen not to play for the team. Confusing these situations is bad both for the professional and for the organization.

Putting all these cases into the same category of “bad professional” is a rather rudimentary form of management. The problem may lie with the person, the position, the training, the system, or the very context in which that person is being asked to play.

The manager's job, therefore, is not merely to assess who is performing well and who is performing poorly. It is to understand why each person is performing that way and decide what can be done based on that diagnosis. Sometimes the best decision is to replace the player. Sometimes it is to change their position. Sometimes it is to send them to training. And sometimes it is simply to understand that the player is going through a different moment and help them find their place within the team again.

## Wearing the Shirt Is Also the Manager's Responsibility

This is where one of the most commonly used expressions in corporate soccer comes in: wearing the shirt. The phrase has become so common that it has almost lost its meaning. It is common to hear that a particular professional needs to wear the company's shirt more, as if belonging were a characteristic that the employee should simply bring with them. As if it were enough to hire the right person, place them inside an organization, and expect them to develop identification with that place on their own.

Perhaps the question before that should be another one: what has the organization done to make that shirt mean something?

A player who grew up at a club, knows its history, was developed within it, lived alongside former idols, and understands what that crest is known for probably has a different relationship with the shirt. Not necessarily because he possesses some special moral characteristic, but because there is meaning built around it. The shirt represents something because there is a history associated with it. There is a collective memory, achievements that came before, people who left their mark, and an expectation about what it means to be part of that group.

In a company, culture works in a similar way. Belonging is not built by asking people to be more engaged. It is not enough to say that a given organization values collaboration, excellence, or innovation. People observe what happens in practice. They observe how decisions are made, how managers behave under pressure, how mistakes are handled, who is recognized, which behaviors are tolerated, and whether there is consistency between what the organization says it is and what it actually does.

It is in this daily experience that the shirt gains meaning. An employee does not begin to identify with an organization simply because they received an ID badge or attended a presentation about the company's values. Identification is built through the experience of being part of that group. It is realizing that one's work matters, that there is room to contribute, that the people around them share certain principles, and that there is some consistency between what the organization promises and what it delivers.

Therefore, perhaps “wearing the shirt” is less an exclusively player-side responsibility and more a responsibility shared with those who manage the club. The player needs to be willing to play for the team, naturally. Commitment cannot be outsourced to the organization. But the manager also needs to build an environment in which there is a reason for someone to want to play for that team.

This changes the conversation about engagement considerably. When an employee shows little connection to the organization, the first reaction is usually to ask whether they are committed. Perhaps the question should begin on the other side: are we offering them the conditions to commit? Do they understand why that work matters? Do they know the team's history? Do they understand how their position contributes to the collective result? Do they receive enough autonomy, recognition, and development to see value in continuing to play?

Wearing the shirt does not mean agreeing with everything. A player can be deeply committed to the club and still disagree with the coach. He can question a decision, point out a problem, or defend a different way of playing. Likewise, an employee who genuinely cares about the organization may be precisely the one who questions a decision because they believe it will hurt the team. Commitment should not be confused with obedience.

Perhaps the true sign that someone wears the shirt is not how much that person repeats the organization's values, but how responsible they feel for the result the team builds together. And this relationship does not arise from a demand. It is built over time through culture, relationships, and, above all, the way managers lead the team.

## Building and Being Part of a Legacy

This leads us to another characteristic that soccer preserves better than many organizations: the idea of legacy. A club that has existed for one hundred years is not concerned only with the current squad. It carries achievements, failures, idols, stories, and symbols built by people who are no longer part of that team. A player takes the field knowing that he is representing something that began before him and, ideally, will continue after he leaves.

There is something special about this idea. Whoever wears a shirt that has existed for decades is not merely playing today's match. They are becoming part of a story that began before they arrived and will continue after they leave. The achievements of previous generations are remembered, celebrated, and incorporated into the club's identity. Players come and go, but their stories remain. A goal, a title, an exceptional season, or even a less visible contribution can become part of that institution's collective memory.

Companies can also build this kind of legacy. Projects end, managers change, teams are reorganized, and strategies are replaced, but an organization that intends to last can preserve what was built by different generations of employees. This can appear in better processes, knowledge that was shared, technologies that were developed, relationships that were built, and, above all, in the people who had the opportunity to grow within that organization.

This is where developing new talent gains a meaning that goes beyond simply reducing hiring costs. A club with a good youth academy is not merely trying to find cheaper players. It is creating opportunities for new people to become part of its history. At the same time, more experienced players transmit knowledge, share experiences, and help preserve what they have learned throughout their careers. The new generation does not need to repeat the previous one exactly. It can receive what was built, add something to it, and take the club somewhere else.

But development also means recognizing that a person's career can change position over time. An experienced player may no longer have the same speed or physical condition they had at their peak, but that does not mean their knowledge has lost value. An athlete with years of experience has accumulated game reading, tactical understanding, experience in pressure situations, and knowledge about other players' behavior. Perhaps they can become a coach, assistant coach, executive, or take on another role within the club. Their contribution changes in nature, but does not necessarily end.

In companies, something similar happens. A professional may no longer want or be able to perform a certain role in the same way they did a few years ago, but that does not mean their experience is no longer useful. Perhaps they can take on a leadership position, act as a specialist, become a mentor, move to another area, or participate in projects in which their accumulated knowledge has greater value. An organization that offers these possibilities can preserve knowledge while, at the same time, creating new growth opportunities for those who have already helped build its history.

There is, naturally, an economic logic to this development. Developing professionals internally can reduce dependence on external hiring and allow the organization to build specific capabilities over time. But there is also a cultural logic. An organization that develops people creates a story that can be shared among different generations. Those who arrived earlier can tell how things were built, what difficulties were faced, and which achievements deserve to be remembered. Those who arrive later can bring new ideas, new capabilities, and a different perspective.

Legacy, therefore, does not necessarily mean preparing someone to take your place. It can mean leaving a contribution that other people will be able to use to build something even greater. It can be a solution that solved a difficult problem, knowledge that was shared, a person who was given an opportunity, or simply a culture that became a little better because you were part of it.

Perhaps this is one of the most interesting characteristics of a soccer club. A player can be remembered decades after leaving the pitch, not only for the numbers he produced, but for what his time there represented for that institution. Great teams do not celebrate only the players who are on the field today. They celebrate those who helped build the history that made that shirt important.

In companies, perhaps we should think in a similar way. Working in an organization should not mean merely delivering what is under our responsibility today. There is also an opportunity to build something that will remain after our time there, share what we have learned, continue growing within the organization, and make room for new talent to contribute to the next part of the story.

## In the End, It Is About Building the Club

Perhaps that is why a company resembles less an isolated soccer team and more a club.

The team is what we see on the field today. The club is the structure that allows different teams to exist over decades. It is its culture, infrastructure, development, history, processes, symbols, and ability to incorporate new people without losing what makes that institution continue to be recognized as the same one.

Throughout this text, we have talked about players in a slump, players out of position, rookies who need training, professionals who need new opportunities, and people who question the game because they can see something that others are not seeing. We also talked about those who simply are not committed to the team. These are different situations, and treating them all in the same way may be one of the simplest ways to turn a management problem into a people problem.

In the end, perhaps there is no bad professional purely by themselves. There is the professional who is not committed and perhaps should not be on that team. There is the professional who is out of position and may need to be reassigned. There is the professional who is not yet ready and needs training. There is the professional in a slump, who may need time, guidance, or simply better conditions to perform again. And there is the professional who is bothersome because they question things, but who may be seeing what no one else is seeing.

The work of management is not simply choosing who takes the field. It is building the conditions for different people to play together. It is defining a strategy, developing a culture, building adequate infrastructure, developing people, understanding the trade-offs of each player, and creating a system structured enough to provide direction, but flexible enough to allow judgment.

Perhaps a good organization is not the one that has the best players. Nor the one that quickly replaces people when the result does not appear. Perhaps it is the one that can build an environment in which different people can perform their roles well, learn from one another, make decisions when the game changes, develop new talent, and preserve what is worth carrying forward.

Because a company, like a soccer club, should be greater than the people who are on the field today. Today's manager should find an organization better than the one they received and, when the time comes to leave the field, hand over something that continues to function, evolve, and be capable of developing the next players.

In soccer and in companies, the match never happens exactly as it was drawn on the tactical board. Perhaps the quality of a team lies precisely in what it is able to do once the game begins.
