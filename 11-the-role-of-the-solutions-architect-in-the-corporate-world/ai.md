Solutions architects should function more like defensive midfielders than protagonists: their value lies in understanding the whole system, anticipating risks, connecting disciplines, and creating conditions for specialists to make good decisions autonomously.

Architectural work is often less visible than other corporate functions because its outputs frequently consist of decisions, constraints, risk mitigation, and prevented failures rather than tangible deliverables. A successful architectural intervention may manifest as a problem that never occurs. The architect therefore operates largely between present decisions and their future consequences; better anticipation often produces less dramatic visible outcomes.

The role should not become that of technology’s “number 10”: an authority expected to know everything, provide every answer, and participate in every relevant decision. Technical breadth and sufficient depth are necessary, but the architect’s value comes more from recognizing important questions, consequences, dependencies, and structural decisions than from personally possessing every answer. Like a defensive midfielder, the architect creates value through positioning and anticipation rather than constant protagonism.

The defensive-midfielder analogy emphasizes enabling rather than controlling. Players such as Claude Makélélé, Sergio Busquets, Casemiro, and Fernandinho illustrate a role that protects structure, occupies important spaces, recovers possession, and gives other players freedom to perform their specialized functions. Similarly, an architect should create enough safety and context for developers, data specialists, infrastructure professionals, security specialists, and other experts to exercise their own judgment. The architect does not need to know more than every specialist; they need enough understanding to connect their decisions and consequences.

A key architectural capability is knowing when not to intervene. Complex solutions contain many simultaneous decisions, but only some have architectural significance. Integration choices, data models, infrastructure decisions, and temporary exceptions can create long-term coupling, cost, or evolutionary constraints; many other technical choices do not require architectural involvement. Not every technical decision is an architectural decision, not every disagreement deserves escalation, and not every implementation needs to match the architect’s preferred approach. The architect must distinguish structural architectural risk from personal technical preference.

Excessive intervention increases friction and reduces autonomy. If teams stop making decisions because they expect the architect to approve or replace them, the architect becomes indispensable through dependency rather than organizational capability. The objective should instead be to establish principles, explicit constraints, known trade-offs, relevant risks, and decision boundaries so teams can make coherent decisions without constant consultation. Authority makes decisions pass through the architect; influence allows decisions to remain coherent even when the architect is absent.

Architecture should therefore be understood as responsibility rather than ownership. The architect should establish principles, document important decisions, clarify constraints, and provide context without making the solution permanently dependent on their presence. A healthy architecture does not require every decision to match what the architect personally would have chosen; it requires important decisions to follow coherent reasoning. Giving up some control enables scale.

However, non-intervention must not become absence. The architect must actively participate when decisions have substantial structural consequences: major business-technology trade-offs, conflicts between legitimate but incompatible interests, creation of durable dependencies, or choices that could constrain future evolution. Autonomy does not eliminate direction, delegation does not eliminate responsibility, and trust does not mean transferring decisions that require architectural perspective. The architect must alternate between stepping aside and taking responsibility according to the significance of the decision.

The role therefore requires two opposing capabilities: knowing when to get out of the way and knowing when to step onto the field. Centralization prevents scalability; excessive detachment abdicates responsibility. Maturity consists of participating when architectural perspective is necessary and disappearing when it is not.

The architect is also not the coach. The manager and architect have complementary responsibilities. The manager operates from a broader organizational perspective, managing people, schedules, budgets, capacity, priorities, conflicts, and resources. The architect operates within the solution, understanding technical decisions, dependencies, risks, constraints, and consequences. The architect should understand business priorities and organizational constraints but should not attempt to manage people, schedules, or execution as a substitute for the manager. Conversely, managers should not unnecessarily determine technical details without the relevant architectural context.

The relationship should involve mutual trust. The manager provides strategy, objectives, and constraints; the architect interprets those conditions within the technical reality and exercises judgment on the field. The architect may challenge assumptions, propose alternatives, and expose risks, but their decisions remain connected to the collective business strategy. Neither role replaces the other.

The apparent invisibility of architecture is therefore partly an inherent trade-off. The more effectively an architect enables teams to operate autonomously and prevents problems before they materialize, the less visible their individual contribution may become. Yet invisibility should not be confused with absence. The architect must remain present when responsibility requires a position, a difficult trade-off, or intervention.

The ultimate measure of the role is not how many decisions require the architect’s approval or how often their name appears. Relevant indicators are whether teams make better decisions, whether different areas communicate more effectively, whether risks surface before becoming problems, whether solutions evolve with less friction, whether important decisions are understood by those who must sustain them, and whether the architect can assume responsibility when architectural judgment is genuinely required.

The central thesis is that a good solutions architect improves the quality and autonomy of the entire organization rather than maximizing personal control or visibility. Like a defensive midfielder, the architect reads the whole game, protects structural spaces, connects players and disciplines, anticipates danger, creates freedom for others, intervenes when necessary, and otherwise allows the play to continue. Their success is reflected less in individual recognition than in a team that makes better decisions, avoids preventable problems, evolves with less friction, and can function effectively without depending on one person.
