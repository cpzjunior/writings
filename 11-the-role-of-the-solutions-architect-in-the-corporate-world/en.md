# The role of the solutions architect in the corporate world

_A good solutions architect is like a defensive midfielder: not necessarily the one who gets the most attention, but the one who keeps the team functioning._

**Summary:** I argue that the solutions architect should act more like a defensive midfielder than the protagonist of the team: their role is to understand the entire game, anticipate risks, connect different areas, and create the conditions for specialists to make good decisions autonomously. This requires knowing when to intervene in truly structural decisions and when to get out of the way, distinguishing architectural risk from personal preference. In the end, their value does not lie in controlling everything or being indispensable, but in making the team make better decisions, evolve with less friction, and function better without constantly depending on them.

---

There is a feeling that I believe is quite common among solutions architects: that of working hard and, even so, having difficulty seeing that work reflected in the way the company perceives their value. We spend hours discussing alternatives, connecting areas, questioning assumptions, anticipating risks, understanding legacy systems, reconciling technical and business constraints, and trying to prevent seemingly small decisions from creating major problems in the future. After all that, when the solution works and the project moves forward, it is almost always someone else who appears in the picture.

For some time, it is easy to interpret this as a lack of recognition. After all, other roles have more tangible deliverables. The developer delivers a feature. The manager delivers a project. Product puts something on the market. The salesperson closes a deal. The architect, often, delivers a decision, a direction, a constraint identified before it became a problem, or simply prevents a problem from happening. It is difficult to put this kind of contribution into a results spreadsheet. Even harder is explaining its value when the outcome of the work is precisely something that did not happen.

There is, however, an irony in this perception. Perhaps part of the invisibility is precisely a consequence of good work. If an architectural decision prevents an outage, reduces a dependency, prevents a poorly designed integration, or keeps a technology choice from becoming a structural limitation a few years later, there is not necessarily an event to celebrate. The problem simply did not happen. And nobody usually stops to celebrate a problem that did not happen.

Perhaps the mistake is expecting architecture to produce visibility in the same way other functions do. The architect often works on what has not yet happened. Their field of action lies precisely between the decisions made today and the consequences those decisions may produce tomorrow. The better they are at anticipating those consequences, the less dramatic the outcome of their work tends to be.

## The architect is not the number 10

There is also a curious expectation surrounding the role of the solutions architect. In some organizations, they are expected to be a kind of number 10 of technology, the professional who needs to know every subject deeply, always have the right answer, and participate in every relevant decision. It is an understandable expectation, especially because architecture requires technical breadth and the ability to make difficult decisions. The problem begins when this expectation turns the architect into an authority who needs to be at the center of every play. At that point, what should be a guidance function begins to turn into a control function.

When we think of a classic number 10, names like Zico, Riquelme, or Kaká help build that image. They are players associated with creation, the ability to decide a match, and those moments when the team looks for someone to produce something different. The defensive midfielder occupies a different space in the football imagination. Think of players like Claude Makélélé, Sergio Busquets, Casemiro, or Fernandinho. None of them needed to be the protagonist of every play to be fundamental to the team's functioning. Much of their value lay precisely in what happened before the decisive play.

The architect also does not need to be the person who knows the most about every component of the solution. They need enough depth to understand the problems and enough breadth to understand how they relate to one another. They need to know which questions to ask, which consequences to investigate, and which decisions truly deserve attention. More than having all the answers, they need enough experience to recognize when an apparently simple answer hides an important decision.

## The defensive midfielder plays for the team

Makélélé is perhaps the most emblematic example of this position. His importance became so associated with the role that his name came to be used to define a specific position on the field. What he did was not simply win the ball back. He protected the defense, occupied spaces, recovered possession, and provided a reference point so that the more attacking players could take risks. His presence gave others freedom because there was someone maintaining balance behind them.

Casemiro also represents this idea well. For many years, his presence allowed players like Modric and Kroos to occupy more advanced positions and have greater freedom in building the game. He did not need to compete with them for the spotlight. His role was to create the conditions for them to perform their own functions better.

This may be one of the best ways to think about the architect. Their work should not limit the autonomy of specialists, but create the safety for them to make decisions and move forward. The architect does not need to demonstrate that they know more than the developer, the data specialist, the infrastructure professional, or the security specialist. On the contrary. The more competent the team around them is, the more the architect should be able to trust it.

The defensive midfielder does not play to receive credit for the play. They play to make the play possible. Likewise, the architect should seek to create the conditions for other people to execute their own functions well.

## The defensive midfielder knows when not to touch the ball

There is another characteristic of this position that I consider especially important for architecture: knowing when not to participate in the play. A midfielder who tries to chase every ball abandons the space they are supposed to protect. If they try to participate in every play, they stop fulfilling their own function. Their value lies precisely in their ability to choose where their presence is necessary.

The same thing happens in architecture. In a complex solution, hundreds of decisions are being made simultaneously, and most of them do not need architectural intervention. Some, however, may define the system's behavior for years. An integration choice can create a permanent coupling. A data model can limit the evolution of several products. An infrastructure decision can significantly alter operational costs. An exception created to meet a project's needs can become a pattern that nobody else can remove. The architect needs to know how to distinguish these situations.

Not every technical decision is an architectural decision. Not every disagreement deserves a discussion. Not every choice needs to be documented in a corporate forum. And not every decision that the architect would make in a certain way needs to be corrected. This distinction is fundamental because architecture should not be a permanent dispute over which implementation is more elegant, which technology is more modern, or which solution the architect themselves would have chosen.

This ability not to interfere is harder than it seems. It requires trust in the team and, above all, maturity to distinguish architectural risk from personal preference. When the architect intervenes in everything, they may increase their perceived importance, but they also increase friction and reduce team autonomy. Gradually, a situation emerges in which people stop making decisions because they know that someone will eventually make them for them.

That is where one of the greatest paradoxes of the role emerges. An architect can become very important to an organization in precisely the wrong way. If every decision has to go through them, if every technical discussion requires their approval, and if teams cannot move forward without their presence, they have become indispensable. But indispensability is not always synonymous with value. In many cases, it is simply a sign of dependency.

The goal should be different. The architect needs to create enough context for teams to make good decisions without having to consult them at every step. This means establishing principles, making constraints explicit, making trade-offs known, identifying relevant risks, and making clear where the boundaries are within which each team can decide freely. After that, they need to trust them.

This is an important difference between authority and influence. Authority makes a decision pass through the architect. Influence makes a decision remain coherent even when the architect is not present. If a team can make a good decision because it understood the principles and boundaries established earlier, the architect exercised influence without needing to be in the room. If a solution can evolve because its structural decisions were well thought out, there is architecture there even if nobody is talking about the architect.

That is exactly what we expect from a defensive midfielder. They do not need to have the ball to influence the play. Often, their contribution lies in occupying a space, providing a passing option, or preventing a dangerous situation from happening. And, in certain plays, their best decision is simply not to touch the ball. They trust that the other players know what to do because they understand that their role is not to control the entire game, but to keep the team balanced.

Perhaps this is the difference between an architect who centralizes and an architect who truly scales their role. The former needs to be everywhere. The latter creates the conditions for the team to function without them. The former accumulates decisions. The latter distributes context. The former becomes indispensable. The latter makes the organization more capable.

And this naturally leads us to the next point: to know when to touch the ball and when to let it move on, the defensive midfielder needs to understand the entire game. The same is true of the architect.

## The architect's job is to make the team play better

There is an important difference between being responsible for architecture and being the owner of architecture. The first is part of the role. The second can be a trap. When the architect begins to see architecture as something that belongs to them, a need to control decisions, review everything, and ensure that every choice follows exactly their vision begins to emerge. The result may even look like a more consistent architecture, but it is often just an organization that is more dependent on one person.

The architecture of a solution should not permanently depend on the architect's presence. If the professional does their job correctly, they establish principles, make constraints explicit, record important decisions, and create enough context for teams to make coherent decisions on their own. The goal is not to increase the number of decisions that pass through the architect. It is to increase the quality of the decisions made around them.

This requires trust. The architect needs to trust the specialists who are closer to the implementation. They need to accept that a team may make a decision different from the one they would have made and that this does not necessarily mean an architectural failure. They need to distinguish what is structural from what is personal preference. And they need to understand that a healthy architecture is not one in which every decision is the same as the ones they would have made, but one in which important decisions follow a coherent logic.

This may be one of the hardest parts of the profession. There is legitimate satisfaction in finding a technically elegant solution and being able to see how all the pieces could fit together. The problem appears when that satisfaction turns into a need to make everyone see the problem in exactly the same way. The architect needs to give up some control to gain something more important: scale.

A defensive midfielder also does not run after every teammate to tell them where they should be. They create the conditions for the team to maintain its organization. When the team functions, each player can fulfill their role without needing to look at the midfielder every second. The midfielder does not need to control every player's movement because there is a shared understanding of how the team should behave.

The same should happen in a mature organization. The architect helps establish the playing field, but does not need to control every movement within it. Their job is to create enough clarity for teams to know where they can move forward, which spaces they need to respect, and which consequences they need to consider before making a decision.

And perhaps this is one of the greatest indicators that an architect is doing their job correctly: when the team starts making good decisions even without them. At that point, the architect stops being merely someone who designs solutions and begins to perform a much more relevant function. They increase the organization's ability to solve problems on its own.

This also changes how we should think about recognition. If the architect needs to appear in every decision to prove that they are adding value, something is wrong. Their goal should not be to make the team depend on them to function. It should be to make the team function better because they exist.

## The architect also needs to step onto the field

There is, however, a risk at the other extreme. If the architect does not need to be the protagonist, that does not mean they can always remain on the sidelines. There is a difference between not needing to participate in every play and avoiding the plays that genuinely require their participation. A defensive midfielder does not chase every ball, but they also do not stand in the center circle while the opponent advances. They choose where they need to be because they understand that there are moments when their intervention is indispensable.

In architecture, these moments appear precisely in the highest-impact decisions. When there are significant trade-offs between business and technology, when different areas defend legitimate and incompatible interests, when a decision may create a structural dependency, or when the chosen path may compromise the future evolution of the solution, it is not enough to say that the team has autonomy. The architect needs to take responsibility, put their position on the table, and participate in the decision.

Autonomy does not mean an absence of direction. Delegating does not mean abandoning. And trusting the team does not mean transferring to it decisions that require a perspective that only someone with architectural responsibility can build. The architect needs to know when to get out of the way and also when to step into the path to prevent the team from making a decision that will have consequences that are difficult to reverse.

This balance is particularly difficult because there is a comfortable temptation at both extremes. The centralized architect wants to control everything. The absent architect protects themselves by saying that the decision belongs to the team. The first does not scale. The second abdicates their own responsibility. Between the two lies a more difficult position: participate when your perspective is necessary and disappear when it is not.

This is where the defensive midfielder metaphor becomes even more precise. A good midfielder is not constantly running to solve the problems of other players. But when they notice that the defense is exposed, they do not wait for someone to ask for help. They position themselves. If they need to cover, they cover. If they need to stop a dangerous play, they intervene. Then they return to their position.

The architect should have the same ability to read the game. Some decisions can be left to the teams. Others require their direct participation. Knowing how to distinguish these situations may be one of the most difficult manifestations of maturity in the role.

This also reinforces an important distinction: not being the protagonist does not mean being a supporting character. The architect remains responsible for an important part of the game. They simply do not need to turn that responsibility into permanent control.

## The defensive midfielder is not the coach

There is an important difference between influencing the game and managing the team. The defensive midfielder is not the coach. They do not select the players, define the strategy alone, distribute tasks, or take responsibility for controlling every movement of the team. Their function is different. They are on the field, participating in the game and using their reading of it to help the team function better. But that does not mean that defensive midfielder and coach are competing roles. On the contrary. They are complementary roles. The coach manages the team from outside the field, while the defensive midfielder is one of the trusted players responsible for interpreting that strategy on the field. The coach defines the organization and priorities. The defensive midfielder needs to understand that organization, perceive what is happening in the match, and make decisions within it.

In the corporate environment, the relationship should be similar. The manager looks at the team from a broader perspective. They monitor schedule, budget, capacity, priorities, conflicts, and performance. They need to understand when to change strategy, when to redistribute resources, and when to intervene to keep the project moving forward. The architect is inside the solution. They need to understand the technical decisions, risks, dependencies, and consequences that emerge as the work progresses.

The architect, therefore, should not try to take on the manager's role. But they also should not act as someone completely detached from management decisions. Good architecture needs to consider schedule, budget, operational capacity, business priorities, and organizational constraints. The architect needs to understand the context in which the decision will be executed, just as the defensive midfielder needs to understand the game plan before making decisions on the field.

The difference lies in responsibility. The manager manages the team. The architect helps the team play. The manager looks at the whole from outside and decides how to organize the available resources. The architect interprets that strategy on the field and helps turn it into a technically viable, sustainable solution consistent with what the organization intends to build.

This relationship also requires trust. The coach cannot interrupt the match every few seconds to tell the defensive midfielder where they should be. They need to trust the player's reading of the game. Likewise, the manager should not need to decide every technical detail of the solution. There is a strategy, there are constraints, and there are defined objectives. Within that context, the architect needs autonomy to exercise their judgment.

At the same time, the defensive midfielder does not play a different match from the one planned by the coach. If they realize that the game has changed, they need to adapt their play, but they continue working in favor of the collective strategy. The architect needs to do the same. They can question a decision, propose an alternative, or warn of a risk, but their actions need to remain connected to the business objectives and priorities defined for that context.

It is precisely this complementarity that makes the relationship important. A good coach needs players capable of thinking on the field. A good manager needs architects capable of making technical decisions without turning every choice into a hierarchical escalation. When this relationship works, there is trust in both directions: the manager trusts the architect's technical judgment, and the architect understands the constraints and objectives that guide management decisions.

The problem appears when one tries to occupy the other's space. When the architect starts managing people, controlling schedules, and centralizing execution decisions, they stop playing their own position. When the manager starts determining technical details without having the necessary context, they also invade a space that should be occupied by the architect.

The defensive midfielder does not need to be the coach to be fundamental to the outcome of the match. They are precisely one of the players who allows the coach to execute their strategy on the field. Likewise, the architect does not need to manage the team to be fundamental to building the solution. Their role is to read the game, protect the structure, connect different parts, and make the technical decisions that keep the team functioning.

This is the complementarity that matters to the metaphor. The coach manages the team from outside the field. The defensive midfielder helps manage the game from within it. One defines the context and strategy. The other interprets that context and makes decisions in response to reality. One does not replace the other. The team needs both.

## The trade-off of not being the protagonist

Perhaps this is where the idea of invisibility needs to be completely reframed. There is an important trade-off in the architect's role: the more they manage to make the team function without depending on them, the less visible their individual participation tends to be. If nobody noticed that the architect prevented a problem, that does not necessarily mean their work had no value. It may mean that the problem was identified early enough to never become a crisis.

This is a particularly thankless characteristic of architecture. A well-designed architecture does not generate a notification saying that a particular outage failed to happen. A well-made decision does not produce an alert informing everyone that a bad dependency was avoided. A coherent technology choice does not necessarily appear in any business metric. An important part of success lies precisely in the absence of a problem that could have existed.

But there is an important limit to this idea. Not being the protagonist does not mean disappearing. The architect cannot use discretion as an excuse to avoid difficult decisions, just as they cannot use team autonomy as a justification for remaining silent when their perspective is necessary. There are moments when it is necessary to take a position, defend a decision, confront a trade-off, or simply say that a particular path does not seem appropriate. The defensive midfielder does not need to touch every ball, but they also cannot watch the game from a distance when their cover is necessary.

Therefore, there is a difference between influence and absence. The architect should seek to influence the game without needing to control it, but they need to be present when their responsibility requires it. They need to know when to let a decision run its course and when to interrupt the play. They need to recognize when a choice is merely a technical preference and when it can compromise the architecture for years. They need the humility to let the team decide and the courage to take responsibility when the decision is truly theirs.

Perhaps professional maturity lies precisely in this ability to alternate between presence and absence without confusing either with value or lack thereof. The centralized architect needs to learn to get out of the way. The absent architect needs to learn to step onto the field. One turns the organization into a dependency on their own presence. The other abandons a responsibility that exists precisely because someone needs to see the game from a broader perspective.

Perhaps the true indicator is something else. Can the team make better decisions? Can the areas communicate better with one another? Do risks appear before they become problems? Can solutions evolve without unnecessary disruption? Are important decisions understood by those who will have to sustain them in the future? And when a decision truly requires an architectural perspective, can the architect take responsibility for it? If the answer is yes, perhaps the architect is doing exactly what they should be doing.

The defensive midfielder does not play to be noticed. They play so that the team functions. They cover when someone moves forward, offer a passing option when there is no way out, accelerate when they find space, and slow down when the game requires control. They will not always be remembered for the play that decided the match, but often the play was only possible because someone was occupying the right space a few seconds earlier. And when the team needs them, they are there.

Perhaps the same can be said about solutions architecture. A good architect does not need to be the protagonist of every decision. They need to understand the entire game, anticipate where the play might go wrong, connect different people and disciplines, create the conditions for the rest of the team to play better, and, when necessary, step onto the field to make the decisions that cannot be delegated.

In the end, perhaps the architect's value does not lie in the number of times their name appears, but in the quality of the game happening around them. If teams can make better decisions, if problems are anticipated, if solutions evolve with less friction, and if the organization can move forward without depending on a single person, there is well-executed architectural work there.

A good defensive midfielder does not need the fans to leave the stadium talking about them. They need to know that, when the match ended, the team was balanced, the spaces were covered, and the players were able to do what they needed to do. A good architect perhaps should think the same way. Their job is not to make the company constantly notice that they are there. It is to make the company function better because they were there. That is the trade-off of not being the protagonist. You give up some visibility to gain something harder to measure: the ability to make the entire team play better.
