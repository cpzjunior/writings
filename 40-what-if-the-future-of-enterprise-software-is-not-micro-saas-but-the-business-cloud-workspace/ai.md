I have been following the Micro-SaaS model, in which small teams identify specific problems and build focused software, but AI is making specialized capabilities increasingly cheap and easy to create, potentially reducing the defensibility of isolated functionality. This does not necessarily eliminate Micro-SaaS; instead, it raises the question of where abundant specialized software will live and where value will accumulate. Enterprise software has historically moved toward specialization through ERP, CRM, HR, project, contract, expense, and other SaaS products, while Micro-SaaS extends that logic to increasingly narrow needs. Cloud and AI have reduced implementation costs, but complexity has not disappeared: it has shifted toward deciding how components should be combined and managing their consequences. As companies accumulate more applications, integrations, credentials, data, contracts, and external dependencies, the potential opportunity is not necessarily to create fewer specialized applications, but to provide a layer capable of absorbing them without transferring their full financial, technical, operational, and cognitive complexity to users. This motivates the proposed Business Cloud Workspace (BCW): an environment where software, agents, data, knowledge, and integrations can be composed around business work rather than around independent products, with value residing partly in the common infrastructure that makes those capabilities coherent.

SaaS simplified the acquisition of enterprise capabilities, but fragmentation can make operation increasingly complex. Every application introduces its own data model, authentication, permissions, interface, billing, integration mechanisms, vendor relationship, and dependency, while dozens of individually inexpensive subscriptions can produce substantial aggregate costs for implementation, administration, training, maintenance, and recurring fees. External services also create a broader supply-chain dependency surface because outages, vulnerabilities, pricing changes, security issues, or product decisions at vendors can affect processes the company does not directly control. A less visible cost is cognitive: moving between applications requires people to reconstruct context, understand where information resides, and adapt to different representations of the same business problem. As AI makes increasingly specialized software economically viable, this abundance may intensify, shifting costs from building individual capabilities toward integrating and operating the resulting collection coherently. The proposed response is therefore not necessarily less specialization, but an abstraction layer through which new capabilities can be incorporated as modules, plugins, agents, or integrations while sharing context, data, identity, permissions, workflows, governance, and experience. The central question becomes how a company can use more software without proportionally increasing its financial, dependency, and cognitive burden.

The BCW begins by reversing the conventional organization of enterprise software: instead of organizing the environment around the applications a company purchases, it would organize it around the capabilities the company needs to use. Like a cloud platform, it could provide capabilities on demand and allow each organization to progressively compose its own environment. AWS illustrates the underlying pattern: a company can begin with a small set of infrastructure services and add resources as its needs evolve, without having to determine the entire architecture in advance. A BCW could apply this model to sales, finance, people, projects, payments, reconciliation, automation, and other business capabilities, allowing them to share data, identity, permissions, workflows, and context. A task requiring customer information, a contract, financial approval, and a document should therefore appear as one business operation rather than as a sequence of applications that the user must understand and coordinate. The platform would not need to build every capability itself; it could provide enterprise primitives such as identity, data, permissions, billing, workflows, and governance while third parties supply specialized modules and agents. Unlike a traditional enterprise suite that attempts to provide a closed collection of products, the BCW would provide an extensible substrate on which each company assembles its own operating environment, allowing it to start small and add capabilities without repeatedly reconstructing its architecture.

A cloud's ability to support both simple applications and sophisticated operations suggests a similar model for business creation. Building even an MVP today often requires infrastructure unrelated to the hypothesis being tested, including authentication, users, permissions, documents, payments, communications, workflows, security, operations, and other foundational capabilities. The proposed BCW could abstract much of this infrastructure so that experimentation focuses on the business hypothesis rather than on recreating the supporting systems required to operate a company. This changes the meaning of an MVP: instead of building a miniature version of an entire company's infrastructure, the entrepreneur would build only what is necessary to test the relevant hypothesis. If validated, the same solution could progressively incorporate more users, processes, agents, integrations, and modules without requiring a complete reconstruction. The distinction between validating a business and structuring its operational environment could therefore become less pronounced, with the platform supporting a continuous trajectory from experiment to operation. The key property would be that the same foundation remains usable as the organization's complexity increases, allowing infrastructure to grow with the business rather than becoming a separate problem after the MVP stage.

The principal differentiator of the BCW would be the experience it provides rather than the collection of applications it contains. An all-in-one suite such as Zoho can reduce the number of vendors by placing CRM, finance, projects, HR, and other functions under one provider, but users may still have to understand product boundaries, determine which application contains a capability, and manage transitions between modules. The BCW instead treats the unit of experience as the work that needs to be accomplished. A user should be able to hire a supplier, consult a contract, discover a policy, execute a repetitive process, or complete an operation involving financial information, customer data, and approvals without needing to understand which underlying systems provide those capabilities. CRM, finance, HR, documents, agents, workflows, and third-party modules become composable capabilities rather than destinations. Integration becomes an inherent property of the environment instead of a user-facing activity. Consequently, competition would shift away from simply adding more features or applications and toward enabling users to accomplish business tasks without understanding the technical architecture underneath them. The analogy with Starbucks is used to illustrate that the value of an experience can reside in the organization and consistency of its surrounding elements, not merely in the fundamental product itself; similarly, the BCW would be defined by the coherent experience produced when applications cease to be the center of interaction.

AI could provide the interpretation layer that makes this abstraction practical by translating between how a company describes its business and how software needs to represent it. Traditionally, this translation requires configuration, consulting, training, and development because users must learn the system's entities, relationships, workflows, and permissions. A BCW could ingest spreadsheets, documents, policies, procedures, and existing systems, identify relevant entities and relationships, and help construct an operational model. Users could describe desired outcomes in natural language while the platform determines the data, capabilities, and workflows required to perform them. The objective would not merely be natural-language interaction or automated data import, but reducing the conceptual distance between the company's own mental model of its business and the structures required by software. This could be especially significant for small organizations that lack dedicated enterprise-systems expertise, with the platform assuming more of the translation burden. The interpretation could also be continuous, allowing changing business requirements to become new structures, workflows, automations, and configurations without requiring users to know in advance how those changes should be modeled. AI would therefore be part of the BCW's fundamental abstraction mechanism, allowing sophisticated underlying capabilities to remain available without requiring every user to understand their implementation.

Once data, documents, and processes coexist in the same environment, organizational knowledge could become an operational component rather than merely documentation. A BCW could provide a company wiki connected to its processes, with policies, procedures, manuals, decisions, and lessons learned related directly to the workflows that implement them. This knowledge could be treated as versioned and governed, while AI uses it as context for answering questions, executing processes, and guiding agents. Policies could become execution rules, procedures could become workflows, and recurring guidelines could become agents. These agents would not merely converse with users; they could access platform resources, consult organizational knowledge, execute workflows, and operate within defined permissions. Companies could configure their objectives, permissions, tools, decision criteria, and even problem-solving methodologies, such as PDCA or OODA, as part of agent behavior. Workflows could likewise be adapted to organizational approval rules, conditions, stages, and responsibilities. The important distinction is that the company would not simply store knowledge in the BCW; it could transform knowledge into operational behavior. If the necessary primitives already existed within the platform, organizations would not need to independently build agents, MCP infrastructure, or specialized integrations simply to connect AI to their operational systems. The BCW would consequently become both the environment where processes execute and the environment where the organization defines how those processes and agents should operate.

Micro-SaaS could therefore remain relevant by becoming extensible rather than remaining isolated. A BCW Marketplace could allow developers to create highly specialized modules, legacy-system integrations, industry-specific workflows, or new AI capabilities that the central platform could not anticipate. The Skyrim-mod analogy illustrates the intended ecosystem model: the platform vendor supplies an extensible foundation while an external community creates capabilities that the vendor could neither predict nor economically build itself. In a BCW, these extensions would add business capabilities rather than merely content, while the platform would supply common primitives such as identity, permissions, data, billing, workflows, and governance. This could reduce the infrastructure burden on Micro-SaaS developers and eliminate the need for each product to create another independent data and operational island. Customers could acquire new capabilities within an environment they already use, while developers could access an existing ecosystem rather than acquiring every customer independently. The model would also reduce experimentation costs because highly specialized modules could be developed without recreating enterprise infrastructure. However, extensibility necessarily increases governance requirements around identity, permissions, isolation, security, auditing, versioning, observability, billing, and policy enforcement. The BCW would therefore centralize common infrastructure and experience rather than all software itself: external payroll, legal, or specialized solutions could remain external while participating in the environment. The vendor would provide the substrate, while the ecosystem discovers and develops capabilities on top of it; the Marketplace would consequently become part of the mechanism by which the platform evolves beyond the vendor's own product roadmap.

This architecture could create a form of lock-in based on accumulated value rather than restriction. Instead of retaining customers because leaving is technically or contractually difficult, the platform could become valuable because the organization progressively builds its processes, workflows, agents, integrations, and knowledge within it. AWS provides an analogy in which users may eventually frame new infrastructure problems in terms of how to solve them within an environment they already understand. A BCW could create a similar dynamic at the business level: after years of configuration and use, the organization would not merely be using a generic product but operating a version of the environment shaped by its own way of working. The Skyrim analogy reinforces this distinction: extensive customization can transform a generic product into an individually configured experience. In the business context, accumulated organizational knowledge, processes, agents, and integrations could make the BCW a unique operational environment. The resulting lock-in would therefore be based on the value of what the organization has built within the platform, with continued use reflecting the fact that leaving would mean abandoning an accumulated operating environment rather than merely being unable to export data or migrate technically.

As the BCW becomes the center of an organization's operations, connecting organizations to one another becomes a possible next step, producing a Business Network. VTEX provides an analogy in which a platform connects multiple actors involved in commerce rather than functioning solely as an isolated application. A BCW could extend this principle to broader business relationships by combining a public services catalog with operational capabilities. Suppliers could register companies, products, services, capabilities, geographic coverage, and commercial terms, while buyers could discover providers, request quotes, negotiate, contract, issue orders, and pay without repeatedly reconstructing context across separate systems. Because the platform would already understand the buyer's users, policies, approval limits, and processes, an internal purchasing need could potentially become an external transaction without manual duplication of information; suppliers could likewise manage the relationship within their own BCW environments. The distinction from the Marketplace is that the Marketplace connects a company to software capabilities, whereas the Business Network connects companies to one another. This could introduce network effects: more participating companies would increase the supply of discoverable providers, more providers would increase usefulness for buyers, and the expanding user base would create a larger market for third-party modules, agents, and services. At this point, enterprise software would begin to overlap with business infrastructure, because the platform would not only support internal operations but also facilitate the external relationships through which companies operate.

The proposal contains a significant risk: a platform capable of doing almost everything could itself become difficult to operate. Salesforce illustrates how extensibility and deep configurability can create an administrative layer requiring specialized knowledge. A BCW could reproduce this problem if its flexibility resulted in excessive features, permissions, configurations, and abstractions, effectively replacing many independent systems with one highly complex system. The proposed solution is progressive abstraction: complexity should remain available to users who need it but should not become mandatory for everyone. A small company should be able to operate the platform without understanding its internal architecture, while advanced users, administrators, and enterprise architects should be able to access progressively deeper configuration, policies, workflows, APIs, integrations, and controls. Different users could therefore interact with radically different levels of complexity without requiring separate products. AWS again provides the reference model: the same underlying infrastructure can support simple abstractions and highly sophisticated configurations. The BCW would similarly need to offer depth without imposing depth, making its advantage not the elimination of complexity—which is unlikely for such a broad platform—but ensuring that each user encounters only the complexity necessary for their work.

The BCW can be understood less as a completely new invention than as a possible synthesis of trends already developing across enterprise technology. Salesforce has made enterprise software increasingly extensible through applications, data, automation, and platforms; Microsoft has brought productivity, data, development, and agents closer together; Shopify and VTEX demonstrate ecosystem-oriented platforms connecting business operations with third parties; and AWS demonstrates how a common infrastructure layer can provide diverse capabilities without requiring every user to understand its internal complexity. What remains unresolved is the combination of these ideas into a coherent work-oriented experience. AI is lowering the cost of software creation, platforms are becoming more extensible, agents are beginning to participate in operations, marketplaces connect organizations to new capabilities, and companies continue accumulating software. The resulting question is therefore not simply whether enterprises will use more software, but whether that software will remain organized as separate applications and suites or become different forms of capability within a shared environment. In the proposed model, the company would not have to select all of its future software in advance: it could begin with a small environment, incorporate new capabilities, connect existing systems, add agents, and install specialized extensions as its needs evolve. The unit of value would consequently shift from the individual application that solves one problem toward the environment that allows a company to solve many different problems without repeatedly rebuilding its way of working.
