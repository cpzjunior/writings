# Risk Is an Organizational Responsibility

_Why risk is not the responsibility of a single function, but a dimension of every organizational decision_

**Summary:** Risk is not the responsibility of a specific function, but a dimension of every organizational decision, because every choice creates future dependencies, concentrations, and constraints. Specialized functions should structure, challenge, and monitor these exposures, but they cannot assume the responsibility of those who make the decisions. Risk management, therefore, does not mean eliminating uncertainty, but making it visible, assigning ownership, and consciously choosing which risks to take. Ultimately, a mature organization is not one that predicts or avoids every risk, but one that can make good decisions in the face of them.

---

I have a curious memory from the first times I studied risk management. Amid so many more concrete project management concepts, there was a chapter dedicated to risks that always seemed a little strange to me. At the same time that the subject was treated with enormous seriousness, the concept seemed almost limitless. It was necessary to identify risks, assess probability and impact, define responses, establish owners, and monitor the evolution of exposures. Everything seemed quite rational until we started thinking about what, exactly, could be considered a risk.

A meteor could hit a company's headquarters. It is extremely unlikely, but not impossible. We could, in theory, record the event, estimate its probability, assess its impact, discuss some response strategy, and even assign someone to monitor it. Perhaps, in that case, it would be more appropriate to call an astronomer or a meteorologist. The idea seems absurd, but that is precisely why it is interesting. If practically any future event can be treated as a risk, where does risk management end and the attempt to control the unpredictable begin?

Perhaps the issue is not trying to catalog everything that can happen, but understanding what we are doing when we call something a risk. We are talking about uncertainty and about the possibility that something that has not yet happened may produce some effect on the objectives we intend to achieve. The meteor helps expose the limits of this concept, but it also reveals its importance. The fact that something is unlikely does not necessarily mean it is irrelevant. Likewise, something quite likely may have such a small impact that it does not justify any significant effort to address it. At some point, therefore, we need to decide what deserves our attention, how much we are willing to invest to reduce a given exposure, and which risks we are simply willing to accept.

This is where the concept starts to become less abstract. Risk is not only found in the major events that appear in a matrix or risk register. It is present in everyday decisions, in the dependencies we create, in the choices we make, and, above all, in what we expect to happen in the future.

## A Concept Larger Than It Seems

Perhaps the problem begins precisely when we try to define what can be considered a risk. Choosing a supplier involves risk. Not changing a supplier also involves risk. Investing in a new technology involves risk. Staying with an old technology involves another. Expanding into another country creates new exposures, but remaining concentrated in a single market does as well. Even an apparently conservative decision can carry risks that will only be noticed when the context changes.

The more we observe organizational decisions, the harder it becomes to separate what is “risk management” from what is simply management. Risk does not appear only when there is a possibility that something will go wrong. It appears when there are different possible paths and we do not know exactly what consequences each one will produce.

This also makes the idea that risk is necessarily negative less obvious. A new technology may fail, but it may also create a competitive advantage. Internationalization may increase operational complexity, but it may also reduce geographic concentration. Concentrating suppliers may generate efficiency and scale, while diversifying them may increase costs and complexity. In each case, the decision involves a different combination of exposures and possibilities.

There is, therefore, no completely risk-free choice. There is a choice among different risk structures. And this begins to bring the concept closer to something much larger than what we normally associate with the word. If every relevant decision involves some degree of uncertainty about its outcomes, perhaps risk is not merely a category within management. Perhaps it is a characteristic of the decision itself.

The next question, then, is no longer simply how an organization should manage its risks. It becomes: who, within the organization, is responsible for the decisions that create them?

## Who Is Responsible for Risk?

This is where an interesting organizational question arises. If risk is present in so many decisions, who is actually responsible for it? The most intuitive answer would be to point to the Risk function. After all, this is the function that normally concentrates methodologies, policies, limits, metrics, assessment processes, and monitoring mechanisms. In more mature organizations, it is also common for Risk Management to establish frameworks, challenge decisions, question assumptions, and assess whether certain exposures are consistent with the risk appetite defined by the organization.

This specialization is important. An organization needs a structured and independent view of its exposures, consistent criteria for assessing them, and mechanisms to identify when certain risks are increasing or exceeding established limits. The problem begins when the existence of this function is confused with the existence of a place to which responsibility for risk can be transferred.

There is a fundamental difference between being responsible for a risk management function and being responsible for a specific exposure. The Risk function can challenge a decision, point out a concentration, question an assumption, or indicate that a given risk is above the established appetite. That does not mean it made the decision that created that exposure. Ownership remains associated with the activity, process, or decision over which someone has authority and the ability to act.

This distinction becomes clearer when we look at other specialized functions. Legal addresses legal and contractual exposures. Compliance addresses regulatory and conduct exposures. Security addresses security-related exposures. Technology addresses technical and operational exposures within its domain. Each of these areas has specific knowledge, responsibilities, and controls. None of them, however, becomes the owner of all consequences that may arise within its area of expertise.

Imagine, for example, a strategic procurement decision. Procurement can lead the selection and negotiation. Legal can structure the contract. Compliance can assess regulatory requirements. Security can evaluate the supplier's security controls. Technology can assess its technical capabilities and architectural dependencies. Risk can challenge the risk assessment and verify its adherence to the framework. Even so, someone within the organization remains responsible for deciding whether that commercial relationship makes sense and whether the resulting exposure is acceptable given the objectives the organization intends to achieve.

It is a subtle but important difference. Specialized functions exist to improve the quality of decisions and ensure that different dimensions of exposure are considered. They do not exist to replace the decision-making process of those who own the business.

Ultimately, an organization can create as many control structures as it wants, but it cannot outsource the consequences of the decisions it makes. Responsibility can be distributed, assessments can be independent, and controls can be sophisticated, but risk remains tied to the reality the organization chose to create. Specialization improves risk management. It does not eliminate the responsibility of those who decide.

## When Outsourcing Also Means Taking on Risk

Outsourcing is often associated with a relatively straightforward efficiency rationale. An activity can be transferred to someone with greater scale, specialization, technical capability, or a more suitable cost structure. The organization stops performing that activity directly and hires a third party to do it. From an economic perspective, the decision may be perfectly rational. But transferring execution does not mean fully transferring the risk.

When an activity is outsourced, the organization replaces an internal capability with an external dependency. What was previously under its direct control now depends, to some extent, on another organization's ability to deliver what was contracted. This relationship can affect continuity, security, quality, operational capability, compliance, time to market, concentration, strategic flexibility, and even the future ability to replace the solution.

A third party can affect the organization in many ways, from schedule and adherence issues to financial or regulatory difficulties and security incidents. It can become an attack vector or, due to a strategic change, discontinue precisely the solution on which the organization has come to depend. The issue is no longer simply whether the supplier can fulfill the contract, but what happens to the organization if it fails to do so.

There is also a less obvious dimension: the outsourcing relationship can change the risk profile over time. A supplier that was initially small may become critical as more processes come to depend on it. A solution that was initially peripheral may become essential infrastructure. A procurement decision that seemed advantageous because of its cost may create lock-in that reduces future negotiating power. A decision to concentrate suppliers may generate economies of scale while also increasing the impact of a potential failure.

This means that a decision can be excellent from an efficiency perspective and still significantly increase the organization's exposure. The lowest cost is not necessarily the lowest risk. A simpler contract is not necessarily a more resilient relationship. And a technically competent supplier is not necessarily a supplier that creates an acceptable exposure.

This is where Procurement and Sourcing also become risk disciplines. Not because Procurement should assume the role of Risk, Legal, Compliance, or Security, but because every procurement decision changes the organization's dependency structure. Choosing a supplier involves price and commercial terms, but it also involves concentration, substitutability, criticality, continuity, security, and response capability.

Outsourcing an activity does not mean outsourcing responsibility for its outcomes. The organization can transfer execution, but it remains exposed to the consequences of a failure. When choosing a supplier, therefore, it is not merely buying a product or service. It is choosing a particular dependency structure, a particular response capability, and, to some extent, a particular configuration for its future.

## Redundancy Is Not Necessarily Resilience

This dependency becomes even more interesting when we think about redundancy. It is common to assume that having two suppliers for a given activity is automatically better than having only one. In many cases, it is. But the number of suppliers, by itself, does not determine resilience. What matters is how much the organization actually reduces its exposure to the same cause of failure.

Two suppliers may depend on the same cloud infrastructure, the same identity provider, the same network, the same software component, the same geographic region, or the same supply chain. In this scenario, the organization may have two contracts, two points of contact, and two formally distinct suppliers, while remaining exposed to the same failure mode.

The same reasoning applies to technology architectures. Distributing systems across different clouds may reduce certain risks, but it does not necessarily create independence if the services used have common dependencies. A failure in a shared component can affect environments that, from an architectural perspective, appeared completely independent. Nominal diversification exists, but effective diversification is lower than it appears.

This question also arises outside technology. Having multiple suppliers does not necessarily eliminate concentration if all of them are subject to the same jurisdiction, market, geographic region, or supply chain. Likewise, having several people on a team does not necessarily represent redundancy if only one possesses the knowledge required to perform a critical activity.

The relevant question, therefore, is not simply how many alternatives we have, but which of them actually fail independently. When two resources have the same failure mode, adding the second may increase operational capacity without proportionally reducing exposure.

This changes how we think about resilience. Resilience is not merely having more resources, suppliers, or alternatives. It is having alternatives that are sufficiently independent so that the failure of one does not simultaneously cause the failure of the others.

Ultimately, redundancy is a quantitative property, while resilience is a structural property. We can count suppliers, servers, clouds, or people. What really matters is understanding the relationships among them and identifying where common points of failure still exist.

## The Risk of People as Single Points of Failure

The same logic can be applied to people. An organization may have multiple suppliers, a modern architecture, and formalized processes and still have a single point of failure in one person. This is not simply about “depending on people,” something inevitable in any organization, but about concentrating in a single person knowledge, expertise, authority, or a relationship whose absence could significantly compromise operations.

It may be the professional who knows a legacy system deeply, the architect who masters a critical integration, the specialist who knows an operational rule that is not documented, or the manager who concentrates important decisions and relationships. While this person is available, the concentration may remain invisible. The problem appears when they leave the organization, become unavailable, or simply can no longer perform that function.

That person's departure can produce a disruption disproportionate to their formal position. An organization may have dozens of professionals in a given area and still have only one person capable of performing a critical activity. In this case, redundancy exists in the organizational chart, but not necessarily in operational capability.

The problem can be even deeper when the concentration is not only in knowledge but also in authority. In excessively personalized structures, certain decisions may depend on a specific person because only that person has the autonomy, legitimacy, or context necessary to make them. Formally, there is an organizational structure. In practice, there is a single decision point.

This type of concentration can also occur in relationships. An executive may be solely responsible for a strategic relationship with a client, regulator, partner, or supplier. The organization has the contract and the formal structure, but the relationship is effectively concentrated in one person. Their departure may represent a loss that is not recorded in any asset inventory.

Documentation, succession, delegation of authority, cross-training, and knowledge distribution are, in this context, mechanisms for reducing single points of failure. They do not eliminate the importance of people, but they reduce the possibility that the absence of one person will produce a disproportionate disruption.

The relevant question is no longer how many people exist in the organization, but how many people are capable of keeping a given critical capability operating. A resilient organization does not need to eliminate indispensable people. It needs to recognize when they exist and understand what happens if they become unavailable.

### When Risk Extends Beyond the Company

So far, it is possible to think about risk through relatively close organizational elements: people, suppliers, technologies, processes, and internal decisions. But no company operates in isolation. It is embedded in markets, industries, supply chains, financial systems, regulatory environments, telecommunications and energy infrastructure, and, ultimately, in countries and regions that have their own risks.

This means that an organization can reduce its internal concentrations and still be highly concentrated in an external dimension. A company may have dozens of suppliers and depend on a single country for a particular raw material. It may have hundreds of customers and remain exposed to a single economic sector. It may distribute its systems among different technology providers and still depend on the same connectivity infrastructure. It may have operations in several cities and remain subject to the same regulatory environment or macroeconomic conditions.

There is, therefore, a risk that does not originate within the company but can directly affect its ability to achieve its objectives. Industry risk, country risk, political risk, geopolitical risk, regulatory risk, foreign exchange risk, infrastructure risk, and climate risk are examples of exposures that can cross organizational boundaries. In some cases, the company does not even have the ability to control the event producing the risk. It can only assess its exposure and decide how to position itself in relation to it.

This is where internationalization appears as a possible diversification strategy. Distributing operations, revenues, suppliers, development centers, or production capacity across different countries can reduce exposure to local events. An economic crisis in one country may have less impact if the organization has significant revenues in other markets. A domestic regulatory change may be less decisive when part of the operations are subject to other jurisdictions. A climate event or infrastructure disruption in one region may be absorbed more easily when alternative capacity exists elsewhere.

But internationalization can also increase risk. Operating in more countries means dealing with more regulatory regimes, currencies, tax systems, political environments, compliance requirements, and operating conditions. The organization becomes exposed to risks that did not previously exist or had little relevance. An international operation may reduce country concentration risk while increasing geopolitical risk. It may diversify revenues while simultaneously increasing foreign exchange exposure. It may create operational redundancy while increasing the complexity required to manage that redundancy.

There is also a subtler issue: different countries are not necessarily independent exposures. A global crisis can affect several markets simultaneously. A given technology may be present in every country where the company operates. A global supply chain may have a common point of origin. An international regulatory change may produce effects across multiple jurisdictions. In other words, placing operations in different geographies does not necessarily create independence among them.

The same applies to market diversification. A company may sell to several countries and remain concentrated in the same industry. It may have customers in different regions and still be exposed to the same economic cycle. It may distribute production across different factories and depend on a single global supplier for a critical component.

Diversification, therefore, needs to be analyzed in terms of correlation among exposures. Two elements may be geographically separated and still respond to the same events. Likewise, two companies may be legally independent and still share the same infrastructure or supply chain.

This perspective is especially important because the pursuit of resilience can produce a kind of paradox. In trying to reduce one concentration, the organization may create another. In internationalizing to reduce country risk, it may increase foreign exchange and geopolitical risk. In adding suppliers, it may increase operational complexity. In distributing systems across clouds, it may create new shared dependencies. In diversifying markets, it may increase exposure to different economic cycles. There is no absolute diversification. There is a particular dependency structure, with different degrees of concentration and correlation.

Therefore, the question should not simply be “are we diversified?” It should be “diversified against what?” And, most importantly, “which events are still capable of affecting simultaneously what we believe are our alternatives?” When we look at it this way, the boundary between internal and external risk begins to disappear. The organization is merely one component of much larger systems on which it depends to operate. Managing risk also means understanding these interdependencies and consciously deciding which of them are acceptable.

## Risk Can Also Be in the Future

There is another dimension of risk that is particularly difficult to perceive because its effects do not appear immediately: the risk created by today's decisions for tomorrow's organization. Many decisions look excellent when evaluated only by the result they produce in the present. They reduce costs, accelerate delivery, simplify processes, or increase efficiency. The problem is that some of them also reduce the options available to those who will need to make decisions in the future.

A decision can reduce current costs while increasing knowledge concentration. It can accelerate a launch while creating technological lock-in. It can centralize decisions and increase execution speed while making the organization dependent on a specific leader. It can eliminate documentation because everyone knows the process today, without considering what will happen when those people leave the company. It can choose an extremely efficient solution for the current scenario while, at the same time, making it much harder to adapt when the scenario changes.

This type of risk is particularly difficult to identify because it does not necessarily appear as a failure. The organization continues to operate. Results continue to be delivered. Indicators may even improve. The risk is being accumulated in the form of a gradual reduction in the ability to choose. An organization may be functioning perfectly while simultaneously becoming less capable of functioning in the future.

This leads us to an important distinction between operational continuity and organizational continuity. The first asks whether the company can continue operating when something fails. The second asks whether we are building an organization capable of continuing to exist, learn, and evolve as people, technologies, markets, and external conditions change.

The difference may seem subtle, but it changes the management horizon. An organization may have contingency plans to keep an operation running during an outage and still have no successors prepared for critical positions. It may have system backups and, at the same time, lack sufficient knowledge to rebuild them. It may have contracts with exit mechanisms and still lack the technical capability to replace a supplier. It may have formalized processes but no real ability to adapt them when the context changes.

This is where the idea of legacy emerges. Legacy does not need to mean only what an organization leaves to society or to its successors. It can simply mean the capability that one generation of professionals leaves for the next. Transferred knowledge, prepared people, evolvable systems, understandable processes, sustainable relationships, and decisions that preserve future options are forms of legacy.

The opposite is also true. Knowledge that is not transferred, successors who are not prepared, processes that are not documented, architectures that cannot evolve, and decisions that concentrate authority may not represent a problem today. But they can drastically reduce the options available to the organization tomorrow.

There is, therefore, an intertemporal dimension to risk. Today's decisions do not affect only today's outcomes. They also determine which decisions will be possible tomorrow. Perhaps this is why a useful question for important decisions is less “what is the risk of this decision?” and more “what will this decision do to the organization's ability to make decisions in the future?” And, looking even further ahead, there is a question that may be more difficult: what risks are we transferring to the organization that will come after us?

## If Everyone Is Responsible for Risk, There Is a Risk That No One Is Responsible for It

There is an important objection to the idea that risk is an organizational responsibility: if everyone is responsible, do we run the risk that no one is actually responsible? It is a legitimate concern. An organization can turn “we are all responsible for risk” into an institutionally comfortable but operationally empty statement. When an exposure grows, everyone can recognize its existence and yet no one may have clear authority to make a decision about it.

Organizational responsibility does not mean diffuse responsibility. It means that risk cannot be artificially separated from the activity that produces it. Every relevant exposure needs to continue having a clear owner, with authority, decision-making capability, and an obligation to act. The existence of a Risk function does not replace that ownership, just as the existence of a Security function does not turn security into an issue exclusively for Security.

This distinction is important because specialized functions have different roles. Risk can establish methodologies, consolidate exposures, challenge assessments, and escalate situations that exceed the defined appetite. Legal can identify legal consequences. Compliance can assess regulatory exposures. Security can establish controls and assess threats. But whoever decides to hire a supplier, launch a product, enter a market, or adopt a given technology remains responsible for the exposure created by that decision.

A good governance structure, therefore, does not distribute responsibility until it disappears. It distributes responsibilities explicitly. Those who decide need to know what risk they are taking. Those with expertise need to have room to challenge the decision. Those in control functions need sufficient independence to escalate what they consider inappropriate. And the organization needs clarity about who, ultimately, has the final say.

Perhaps maturity lies precisely in being able to combine these two things: risk as everyone's responsibility and clearly defined accountability for each relevant risk.

## If Everything Is Risk, Nothing Is Risk

There is a second objection, perhaps even more important. If practically every decision involves some uncertainty, then everything can be called risk. But if everything is risk, nothing is risk. A concept that encompasses every possibility loses its ability to guide decisions.

The meteor at the beginning of the article is useful again here. It is possible to imagine a company investing time in assessing the risk of an extremely unlikely event while neglecting much more probable and relevant risks to its operations. The problem would not be recognizing that the meteor is possible. It would be treating its existence, by itself, as justification for the same level of attention devoted to a critical dependency, a supplier concentration, or a known vulnerability.

Risk management therefore requires materiality. Not every uncertainty needs to be recorded, measured, or mitigated. The organization needs criteria to distinguish what deserves attention from what can simply be accepted. Probability, impact, speed of realization, response capability, correlation with other exposures, and strategic relevance are some of the dimensions that can make this distinction.

This also means that there is no ideal number of risks for an organization to monitor. A list containing hundreds of risks can create a false sense of control if the main failure modes are hidden among relatively insignificant exposures. Likewise, an organization may have few formally recorded risks and still be deeply exposed to concentrations that were never recognized as such.

The goal is not to turn every uncertainty into a control item. It is to identify the uncertainties that can materially alter the organization's ability to achieve its objectives and make conscious decisions about them.

Risk management, in this sense, is also a discipline of choice. It is necessary to choose what deserves attention, what needs to be mitigated, what can be transferred, and what can be accepted. And then the initial thesis gains a second condition: risk is an organizational responsibility, but not every risk deserves the same treatment.

## Risk Is Also a Matter of Culture

An organization can have sophisticated frameworks, risk matrices, committees, policies, and specialized functions and still have poor risk management. This happens when people do not feel authorized to talk about what could go wrong.

Risk management begins long before a risk enters a tool or is presented to a committee. It begins when someone feels comfortable saying that there is a problem, a dependency that is concerning, an assumption that may be wrong, or a scenario that has not yet been considered.

This seems simple, but it is not always part of an organization's culture. In some environments, there is a kind of superstition around risk. Talking about what could go wrong can be interpreted as pessimism, lack of confidence, or even as a way of “attracting” the problem. It is as if acknowledging the possibility of a failure somehow contributes to making it happen.

The effect is perverse. When pointing out a risk is perceived as something negative, people learn to hide uncertainties until they cease to be uncertainties and become concrete problems. The organization begins to discover its risks through their materialization.

A mature culture works differently. A manager should not ask only whether the team is delivering what was planned, but also what could prevent that delivery from happening. And, above all, they should create an environment in which an answer such as “there is a risk” is received as useful information rather than as a threat to the person who identified it.

This does not mean encouraging a culture of pessimism or turning every meeting into a list of things that could go wrong. It means creating space for people to raise hypotheses before they become incidents. The goal is not to find culprits in advance, but to increase the amount of information available for decision-making.

In this context, identifying a risk should not be confused with asserting that it will necessarily happen. An identified risk is a possibility that has gained visibility. From there, the organization can assess its probability, impact, speed of realization, interdependencies, and relationship to risk appetite. Only then can it decide whether to mitigate, transfer, avoid, or simply accept it.

There is an enormous difference between an organization in which people say “this will go wrong” and one in which they can say “there is this scenario, these are the conditions under which it could happen, and this would be the impact.” The first produces anxiety. The second produces information for decision-making.

Therefore, perhaps one of the clearest signs of a mature risk culture is the ability to talk about risks before there is a problem to justify the conversation. Risk does not need to be a prophecy. It does not need to be a prediction of disaster. It is information about a future possibility that can help the organization make better decisions in the present. And, in this sense, creating a risk culture does not mean teaching people to see problems everywhere. It means teaching them to see possibilities, make them visible, and allow the organization to consciously choose what to do about them.

## Risk as a Dimension of Decision-Making

Perhaps it is possible to look at all these examples through the same lens. The problem of risk management is not only identifying events that may happen. It is understanding the relationships the organization establishes to achieve its objectives, where its concentrations are, which failure modes can affect it, which options remain available when the scenario changes, and which opportunities may arise precisely from the exposures it is willing to assume.

Every decision changes some of these relationships. A procurement decision creates dependencies. A technology architecture creates paths for evolution and future constraints. An organizational structure distributes or concentrates knowledge and authority. Geographic expansion changes the company's exposures. A decision not to invest also produces consequences. Risk does not appear after the decision, as a problem that needs to be sent to a specialized function. It was already present when the decision was made.

This does not mean that every dependency is bad or that every concentration should be eliminated. Many are necessary, efficient, and economically rational. An organization that attempted to eliminate all its exposures would probably become incapable of operating. The goal is not to build a risk-free organization, but to understand which exposures exist, which are deliberate, which are known, and which we are willing to accept in exchange for the results we seek.

Likewise, maturity in risk management does not mean minimizing every possible exposure. It means making conscious decisions about them. In some cases, the right decision will be to reduce the risk. In others, it will be to transfer, avoid, or accept it. And in certain situations, taking on greater exposure will be precisely what allows the organization to capture an opportunity that would otherwise not exist.

This is where risk management stops looking like an abstract exercise in cataloging improbable events and becomes part of what every organization actually does every day: make decisions.

The Risk function has a fundamental role in this process. It can structure, guide, challenge, and monitor. Legal, Compliance, Security, Technology, Procurement, Human Resources, Operations, and all other functions also have specialized perspectives that need to be incorporated into decisions. But none of these functions can remove from the organization the responsibility for the choices it makes and the exposures that result from them.

This responsibility also needs to appear in the culture. A mature organization does not wait for risk to materialize before beginning to address it. It creates space for people to raise concerns, challenge assumptions, and point out potential failure scenarios before there is an incident to justify the conversation. Identifying a risk does not mean asserting that it will happen. It means making a possibility visible so that someone can decide what to do about it.

Perhaps this is the most important part of a risk culture: not creating an organization that is afraid to make decisions, but an organization capable of making decisions while knowing what it is taking on. The goal is not to make everyone see problems everywhere. It is to ensure that relevant uncertainties can be seen, discussed, and addressed before they become facts.

Ultimately, risk is not only in what may happen. It is also in what we choose to do in response to what may happen. It is in the dependencies we create, the concentrations we accept, the alternatives we preserve, the opportunities we pursue, and the options we eliminate for the future.

That is why perhaps the most important question is not simply “what is the risk?” but “what risk are we choosing to take in order to achieve what we want?” This question moves risk from a specific place in the organizational structure into the decision-making process itself. Risk does not belong only to one function. It does not appear only when someone opens a risk register. It does not begin when an exposure is presented to a committee. It is present at the moment the organization chooses one path among several possible ones.

A mature organization is not one that has eliminated its risks. It is one that can make them visible, assign responsibility for them, and consciously decide which ones it is willing to take. And perhaps that is the essence of risk management: not predicting the future, but building an organization capable of making good decisions in the face of it.
