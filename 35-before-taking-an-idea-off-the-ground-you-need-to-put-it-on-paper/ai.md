A friend’s embryonic startup idea revealed a recurring problem: problems, solutions, features, technology choices, and business assumptions were mixed together without a structure for questioning them. Although canvases and frameworks can organize information, they cannot substitute for the reasoning that should precede them. This applies not only to startups but also to products, companies, transformations, social projects, and process changes. “Just start” can overcome inertia, but when uncertainty remains about the problem, solution, stakeholders, or conditions for success, execution without a defined learning objective can merely turn confusion into execution. Before acting, it is therefore useful to put the idea on paper—not to create a definitive plan, but to make assumptions explicit enough to question.

Thinking and doing are complementary rather than opposing approaches: excessive planning can avoid contact with reality, while execution without a hypothesis, expected observation, and decision criterion can produce activity without learning. The central issue is establishing a disciplined relationship between thought, action, and learning. Lean Startup provides the logic of turning hypotheses into experiments, while PDCA offers a broader cycle in which planning clarifies beliefs and unknowns, execution produces contact with reality, verification compares results with expectations, and action updates the understanding. Planning should prepare the next decision rather than anticipate the entire execution, and execution should produce knowledge as well as output. Uncertainty does not need to be eliminated before acting because some uncertainties can only be reduced through reality. Putting an idea on paper therefore means separating knowledge from assumptions, observations from interpretations, and intended solutions from perceived needs, creating the first opportunity to confront the idea critically.

The proposed methodology addresses the space between an initial hypothesis and an experiment or MVP by organizing an initiative into dimensions containing distinct questions, uncertainties, decisions, evidence, and outputs. It does not claim novelty over approaches such as Lean Startup, Design Thinking, Customer Development, Business Model Canvas, or Venture Design; instead, it seeks to connect existing approaches around the dimensions of an initiative and clarify when and why particular techniques should be used. A dimension is useful when it represents a meaningful set of decisions with its own questions, uncertainties, and evidence, while the proposed taxonomy remains pragmatic rather than definitive. Possible dimensions include problem and opportunity, product, business and market, UX, technology, domain and data, risk and security, management, sales and customer success, operations, finance, and execution, but not every initiative requires all of them or the same depth. They interact continuously: technical discoveries can alter products, market discoveries can alter business models, operational constraints can alter UX, and financial constraints can alter scope or pace. The key concept is the Artifact: not necessarily a document or template, but a sufficiently explicit set of decisions, hypotheses, evidence, models, and representations that allows others to understand and challenge the reasoning. Tools such as canvases, diagrams, or financial models are means to produce this understanding, not ends in themselves. Negative findings are also valid outputs because disproving an assumption or finding a path infeasible is useful knowledge. The methodology connects question, reasoning, tool, evidence, output, and decision, with each decision feeding the next PDCA cycle. Its depth should remain proportional to uncertainty, decision impact, and the cost of being wrong, avoiding the creation of bureaucracy in the name of reducing waste.

An initiative often begins with the perception that a situation, unmet need, or change could justify doing something differently. Problem and Opportunity Discovery seeks to distinguish what is actually observed from what is interpreted, understand who experiences the problem, its context, frequency, causes, and consequences, and determine why the problem represents an opportunity worth pursuing now. Interviews, observation, data analysis, exploratory research, root-cause analysis, Five Whys, stakeholder maps, and problem journeys may provide relevant evidence depending on the question. The resulting Problem & Opportunity Artifact should capture the current understanding of the problem, affected parties, context, evidence, contradictions, opportunity, relevance, assumptions, and remaining uncertainties. Its purpose is not to establish a definitive answer but to determine whether there is a sufficiently credible problem-and-opportunity hypothesis to justify investigating a solution.

Product Design begins once a problem and opportunity hypothesis exists, but it should start with a product thesis rather than a feature list. The thesis proposes that a particular solution can create value for particular people, address identified problems, and produce a relevant outcome. A problem may support multiple solutions, and a product may address several related problems, so discovering the problem does not automatically determine the product. The key questions concern the proposed solution, target users, problems addressed, value created, expected outcome, necessary capabilities, assumptions, and what must be learned before building. Prototypes, experiments, interviews, alternatives analysis, story mapping, and canvases can structure and test the reasoning, but none defines the product independently. The Product Design Artifact should make users and beneficiaries, problems, value proposition, expected outcomes, capabilities, initial scope, and unverified assumptions explicit. Its purpose is to formulate a product thesis clearly enough to test whether the solution can transform the identified opportunity into value, rather than to define the final product in advance.

Business & Market Design addresses the distinction between creating value and creating a viable business and examines how value will be captured and how the product will reach users or customers. Questions concern customers, users, payers, pricing, acquisition, channels, discovery, adoption, and barriers between product existence and effective use. Different segments, pricing models, channels, sales approaches, and value-capture mechanisms can be tested independently, with failure of one configuration not necessarily invalidating the product or opportunity. Timing is also part of the business thesis because regulatory, technological, behavioral, or competitive windows may be temporary. Business Model Canvas, Value Proposition Canvas, pricing analysis, unit economics, commercial interviews, channel tests, and acquisition experiments can generate relevant evidence when tied to decisions. The Business & Market Design Artifact should capture customers and users, routes to market, value creation and capture, acquisition, pricing and cost assumptions, timing constraints, and remaining hypotheses. Its purpose is to determine whether a plausible combination of product, market, economic model, and timing can sustain continued investment.

UX Design examines whether the proposed solution can actually be experienced and used in a way that allows the intended value to be obtained. It concerns the tasks users must perform, information they need, decisions they make, friction points, and aspects that need to be simple, predictable, or obvious. Journeys, flows, wireframes, prototypes, usability tests, and user research may be appropriate depending on the uncertainty and stage. UX investment should follow the risk and cost of being wrong rather than procedural expectations; an early experiment may need only a sufficient experience to test the product hypothesis, while greater maturity or complexity may justify specialized UX work. Poor UX can invalidate an experience hypothesis without necessarily invalidating the product thesis. The UX Design Artifact should represent relevant flows, tasks, interactions, information, decisions, and unverified experience assumptions in an appropriate form, and UX can be deepened over successive cycles rather than treated as a stage that must be completed once and for all.

System Design translates the product thesis into a hypothesis about the technological system required to provide its capabilities. It asks which capabilities, components, integrations, constraints, and quality attributes are required and emphasizes that architecture should respond to actual product and organizational needs rather than preferred technologies or defaults. Different products have different architectural requirements, and technical decisions can feed back into product and business assumptions when capabilities prove too costly, complex, slow, insecure, dependent on external systems, or otherwise infeasible. Architecture diagrams, C4, ADRs, technical prototypes, proofs of concept, and specific security or resilience analyses can investigate these uncertainties at different depths. The System Design Artifact should capture components and relationships, interfaces and integrations, quality attributes, constraints, key architectural decisions, trade-offs, and unresolved technical risks. Its goal is not a definitive architecture but enough technical understanding to determine what can be built, under what conditions, and what still needs to be investigated.

Domain & Data Design starts from the domain being represented rather than from database structures. It asks which concepts and entities exist, their relationships and states, the rules governing them, state transitions, and information required for coherent representation. The data model is treated as a consequence of domain understanding rather than a substitute for it, because an incorrect domain model can propagate into APIs, business rules, processes, interfaces, reports, and integrations and become expensive to change. Domain modeling, Event Storming, domain glossaries, entity-relationship modeling, and collaborative investigation can help establish and challenge this understanding. The Domain & Data Design Artifact should record the principal domain concepts, entities, relationships, states, rules, required data, ambiguities, assumptions, and modeling decisions. The objective is to establish a sufficiently coherent representation of reality to guide technical decisions without prematurely treating the physical database model as the domain itself.

Risk & Security Design makes explicit the risks that could compromise the initiative across product, market, technology, data, operations, organization, and execution, while distinguishing between risks that must be mitigated and those that can be accepted, transferred, avoided, or monitored. It asks what can go wrong, with what probability and impact, which assets or capabilities are exposed, which risks are acceptable, and which controls are justified relative to their cost. Risk is treated as an organizational concern because ordinary product, architectural, operational, supplier, and delivery decisions create risk exposure. Security is one component of this broader perspective, involving confidentiality, integrity, availability, privacy, and abuse concerns. Threat modeling, risk analysis, abuse cases, security architecture, control analysis, and security testing can provide evidence, while controls themselves can introduce cost and friction, making risk-free execution an unrealistic objective. The Risk & Security Design Artifact should document major risks, impacts, security requirements, controls, accepted risks, and unresolved uncertainties, with the aim of establishing which risks are acceptable and which conditions must be met to proceed.

Management Design addresses whether the initiative has the organizational capacity required to execute and operate it. It considers necessary skills, internal versus external capabilities, responsibilities, decision rights, management capacity, communication, incentives, governance, and coordination. Organizational structure is treated as more than an arrangement of people because information flow, decision-making, incentives, and concentration of knowledge affect execution. There is no universal structure: an early initiative may depend on generalists, while a mature operation may require specialization and more formal coordination, and the appropriate design depends on context, stage, uncertainty, and capabilities. Organizational design, capability mapping, role definitions, RACI, skills analysis, decision mechanisms, and capacity planning can support this reasoning. The Management Design Artifact should capture required capabilities, roles and responsibilities, critical skills, internal and external activities, capability gaps, and mechanisms for communication, governance, coordination, and decision-making. The key question is whether the necessary organizational capacity exists or can be created.

Sales & Customer Success Design focuses on the relationship through which the value proposition becomes a customer relationship and, after purchase, becomes realized value. It asks who buys and participates in the decision, how the proposal is presented, what objections arise, how contracting occurs, and how customers are onboarded, activated, supported, retained, and potentially expanded. The appropriate model varies significantly between transactional consumer products and consultative B2B offerings. Sales discovery, customer interviews, acquisition-process analysis, pilots, demonstrations, onboarding experiments, customer journeys, and activation, retention, and expansion metrics can generate evidence. The Sales & Customer Success Design Artifact should make buyers and participants, acquisition and contracting processes, channels and touchpoints, conversion steps, mechanisms for customer value realization, and unresolved acquisition, conversion, activation, retention, and expansion hypotheses explicit. The objective is not to design a definitive commercial operation but to understand whether the value proposition can become sustained customer value.

Operations & Support Design considers what is required to deliver, operate, and sustain a product after it exists technically. It addresses service delivery, manual activities, support, monitoring, exceptions, controls, automation, external dependencies, and operational capacity. Operational analysis can reveal that seemingly simple product features require recurring manual intervention, do not scale, create expensive exceptions, or demand capabilities absent from the initial design, potentially requiring changes to product, UX, or business model. Process mapping, service blueprints, procedures, capacity analysis, support-process design, and operational experiments can investigate these issues, and early operations may intentionally remain manual until recurring volume justifies automation or redesign. The Operations & Support Design Artifact should capture essential processes, roles, handoffs, controls, exceptions, dependencies, manual and automated activities, bottlenecks, risks, and capacity constraints. Its purpose is to determine whether the service can be delivered and sustained consistently under the required volume, quality, cost, and complexity conditions.

Finance & Investments Design treats economic constraints as part of the initiative’s thesis rather than merely a downstream consequence. It examines costs required to reach the next stage, fixed and variable costs, financial capacity, continuity risks, time until revenue or additional resources are needed, and variables that determine viability. The goal is not a perfect forecast but identification of the economic conditions and uncertainties that can alter strategy, scope, pace, or experimentation. Investment is considered incrementally: the relevant question is how much should be committed, for what purpose, when, and under what conditions investment should continue, decrease, or stop. If existing resources are insufficient, financing alternatives must also be considered in terms of capital requirements, timing, duration, conditions, growth pace, and commitments. Financial models, unit economics, cash-flow projections, scenarios, sensitivity analyses, and capital-structure analyses can support this reasoning. The Finance & Investments Design Artifact should document cost and revenue assumptions, economic conditions, capital requirements, financial horizon, risk scenarios, financing alternatives where relevant, and conditions for continued allocation. External investment is not assumed to be necessary; initiatives may rely on founders, revenue, internal budgets, or existing resources.

The Roadmap transforms accumulated understanding into a current execution strategy without requiring every uncertainty to be resolved first. A backlog represents the universe of possible features, experiments, fixes, integrations, and improvements, whereas the roadmap represents what has been selected and ordered given current constraints and priorities. The MVP is therefore not necessarily the smallest feature set but whatever combination of product, experiment, prototype, manual process, or other activity is required to test a relevant hypothesis and produce enough evidence for the next decision. A roadmap should represent dependencies, constraints, uncertainties, discoveries, decisions, builds, and deliveries rather than merely features assigned to dates. Its precision and horizon should match what is actually known, and it must be revised as execution produces new evidence. The Roadmap itself becomes an instrument for testing the thesis because it translates understanding into action and allows subsequent learning to change the planned path. The decision it supports is what sequence of actions, given current knowledge, best advances the thesis while reducing the most relevant uncertainties.

The methodology is presented as an initial proposal rather than a closed framework, mandatory sequence, or execution recipe. The available techniques, canvases, and frameworks already address many parts of initiative design, but the proposed layer seeks to connect them through a process of reasoning, decision-making, and learning. The dimensions can be combined, expanded, omitted, or reordered according to context, stage, constraints, and existing knowledge, and additional dimensions could be derived from Lean Startup, PMBOK, TOGAF, or other disciplines. Existing methods are not intended to be replaced: Lean Startup contributes hypothesis-driven experimentation and learning, PMBOK provides project-management foundations, and TOGAF structures aspects of enterprise architecture, while this proposal operates at the level of identifying which dimensions require consideration and selecting suitable methods and tools for each. A canvas, interview, prototype, financial model, architecture diagram, or other technique has value only when it helps answer a relevant question, reduce a specific uncertainty, and produce evidence sufficient for a decision. The common principle is to make the thesis explicit, identify uncertainties, plan the next step, execute, observe, and use the resulting learning to decide what follows. Putting an idea on paper is therefore not an attempt to make it execution-ready before reality is involved; it is an attempt to make the idea clear enough to know what we are trying to discover, after which execution can begin.
