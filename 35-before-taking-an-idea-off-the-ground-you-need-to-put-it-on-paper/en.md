# Before Taking an Idea Off the Ground, You Need to Put It on Paper

_Canvas and frameworks help us think through parts of a problem. The challenge is connecting them into a methodology capable of turning an idea into an initiative that can be understood and executed._

**Summary:** I propose a different methodology for taking ideas off the ground, starting from a critique of the use of disconnected frameworks that fragment what should be a continuous process. The proposal is to organize different approaches into a logical sequence, in which each stage answers a question and prepares the next one. In this way, problems, hypotheses, solution, and validation cease to be isolated exercises and become part of the same construction process. It is still an embryonic methodology, but one that seeks to bring coherence to the path between an idea and its execution.

---

Recently, a friend reached out to talk about a startup idea he wanted to develop. He had a perception of an opportunity, some ideas about the solution, and the desire to make it happen. The problem was that the proposal was still so embryonic that it was difficult to understand exactly what he wanted to build.

As he explained it, it became clear that several things were mixed together. The problem he imagined solving, the solution he had in mind, some features, a view of technology, and even some ideas about how the business could work. Everything seemed to make sense when he told the story, but there was no structure that would allow us to separate these things, question them, and understand which were assumptions and which were conclusions.

The most common reaction to this type of situation is to look for a tool. One canvas to organize the business, another to think about the product, some framework to validate the idea, and then a plan to begin execution. I myself have already designed canvases, filled out frameworks, and produced documents that seemed quite useful at the time. Several ended up forgotten in some folder or drawer. Over time, I began to realize that the problem was not necessarily with the tools, but with expecting the artifact to produce the reasoning that should have existed before it.

An idea does not become clearer because we put its elements into boxes. It becomes clearer when we are forced to think about the relationships between those elements. And this applies not only to startups. A new company, a new product, a transformation initiative, a social project, or even a change to an existing process can begin in exactly the same way: someone identifies an opportunity, perceives a problem, and imagines that it would be possible to do something about it. In all these cases, there is a recurring temptation to confuse willingness to act with clarity about what should be done.

“Just start” can be good advice when the goal is to overcome inertia. And, in many cases, starting is precisely the best way to learn. But when there is still uncertainty about the problem, the solution, the people involved, or the conditions required for the initiative to work, executing without knowing what we are trying to discover can simply turn a confused idea into confused execution. Before building something, we need to know what we are trying to discover, which hypotheses we are assuming, and what kind of evidence could make us change direction.

Logically, before taking an idea off the ground, we need to put it on paper.

## The False Dichotomy Between Thinking and Doing

When someone has an idea, two pieces of advice frequently appear. The first is to plan everything before starting. The second is to simply start and learn by doing. In practice, thinking and doing are not alternatives. They complement each other and form a learning cycle.

Planning indefinitely is a fairly efficient way to avoid contact with reality. It is possible to spend months refining an idea, building projections, designing architectures, and discussing possibilities without ever discovering whether anyone actually wants it. But starting without minimally formulating what we are trying to discover is also problematic. Building something does not necessarily mean learning something. If we do not know which hypothesis we are testing, what we expect to observe, and how the result should influence our decision, execution can become nothing more than movement.

The problem, therefore, is neither thinking too much nor executing too early. It is the absence of a disciplined relationship between thought, action, and learning.

The logic of experimenting and learning is not new either. Lean Startup popularized the idea of turning hypotheses into experiments, measuring the results, and learning from them. The point that interests me comes slightly earlier: between a hypothesis and an experiment, there is a considerable number of decisions. What problem are we actually trying to solve? For whom? What solution are we proposing? What needs to be tested? What needs to exist technically? What can be done manually? How will we find the first users? What economic conditions need to be true? Reaching an MVP is not simply about reducing a list of features. It is about deciding what needs to be built, tested, or discovered to produce relevant learning.

It is in this context that PDCA seems particularly useful to me. Not as an alternative to Lean Startup, but as a more general principle for organizing the relationship between thought, action, and learning. In planning, we make explicit what we believe, identify what we still do not know, and define what action can produce the necessary information. In execution, we put that hypothesis in contact with reality. In verification, we confront the observed result with what we expected. In action, we update our understanding and decide the next step.

Planning, in this context, does not exist to anticipate the entire execution. It exists to prepare the next decision. Execution, in turn, does not serve only to produce something, but also to produce knowledge about what we still do not know.

This establishes an important limit for planning. We do not need to eliminate all uncertainty before acting. Some can be reduced through reasoning; others can only be reduced through reality. Continuing to plan after there is already enough information to execute can be just as unproductive as executing before understanding what we are trying to discover.

That is why putting an idea on paper does not mean producing a definitive plan. It means transforming an intuition into something explicit enough to be questioned. An idea in our head carries countless invisible assumptions. When we need to write it down, we are forced to separate what we know from what we assume, what we observe from what we interpret, and what we want to build from what we believe someone needs.

Paper is not the destination of the idea. It is the first place where we can confront it.

## A Proposed Methodology

If Lean Startup helps us structure learning from hypotheses and PDCA organizes the cycle between planning, execution, verification, and action, there is still a practical question: what do we need to think about between a hypothesis and an experiment, or between an idea and an MVP?

My proposal starts precisely from this space. An initiative can be observed through different dimensions, and each of them represents a specific set of questions, decisions, and uncertainties that need to be understood for the initiative to move forward. These dimensions do not necessarily form a linear sequence. They are different perspectives on the same initiative and may become important at different moments in the cycle.

The proposal also does not assume that these principles are novel. There are different approaches that already combine problem discovery, value proposition definition, business modeling, hypothesis formulation, experimentation, and learning. Lean Startup, Design Thinking, Customer Development, Business Model Canvas, and Venture Design approaches, among others, already provide important parts of this logic and, in some cases, explicitly seek to integrate them.

What I propose here is a way to organize these approaches around the dimensions of an initiative and the decisions that need to be made within each of them. The intention is to create a structure that allows us to identify what we need to understand, which uncertainty we are trying to reduce, what type of evidence we need to produce, and which Artifact should consolidate that understanding. The contribution, therefore, is not to create new techniques, but to propose a way of connecting them and deciding when and why to use them.

A dimension deserves to be considered when it represents a set of decisions that are relevant to the initiative and has its own questions, uncertainties, and evidence. This criterion is more important than the existence of a definitive taxonomy. The dimensions presented here are a pragmatic way of organizing reasoning, not a claim that every initiative needs to be analyzed from exactly these perspectives.

An initiative can begin with understanding a problem and the opportunity associated with it. From there, a product thesis may emerge, which needs to be confronted with the business model, the way of reaching the market, customer acquisition and relationships, user experience, and technical feasibility. Next, questions may arise about domain, data, security, operations, organization, finance, investment, and execution. Not every initiative will require all these dimensions, and the depth of each will depend on the context, stage, and existing uncertainties.

These dimensions are also not independent. A discovery in one of them may alter hypotheses established in another. A technical constraint may modify the product. A market discovery may alter the business model. An operational limitation may require changes to the user experience. A financial constraint may modify scope or execution pace. The methodology needs to allow these relationships to be revisited as new evidence emerges.

The objective is not to turn these dimensions into a rigid process. It is to create a structure that helps recognize which questions need to be answered and which decisions need to be made. Each dimension can use different tools and produce different representations. What matters is that the result makes explicit the understanding necessary to guide the next decision.

It is in this sense that I use the concept of Artifact. A Product Canvas can be a tool for organizing a product hypothesis, but what matters is the understanding consolidated in the Product Design Artifact. A diagram can help discuss an architecture, but the System Design Artifact should make the relevant decisions, constraints, and trade-offs understandable. A financial model can organize economic assumptions, while the Finance & Investments Artifact should make it possible to understand the economic conditions that need to be true for the initiative to move forward.

The Artifact, therefore, does not need to be an extensive document or represent a definitive conclusion. It can be a set of decisions, hypotheses, evidence, models, or representations that are sufficiently clear for other people to understand and question the reasoning. Its value lies less in its form and more in its ability to make knowledge explicit and usable.

The result of an investigation also does not need to confirm the hypothesis. We may discover that an assumption does not hold, that a particular path is unfeasible, or that it is not worth continuing to invest in that direction. That is also knowledge and, therefore, also a valid result. The objective is not to produce documents that demonstrate progress, but to produce understanding that improves the quality of decisions.

The relationship that connects the methodology is therefore between question, reasoning, tool, evidence, output, and decision. The question guides the reasoning. The reasoning determines which tools may be useful. The tools help produce evidence and organize understanding. The output makes that understanding explicit. The decision uses this knowledge to define the next step. The result of that action, in turn, feeds the next PDCA cycle.

This changes the role of frameworks. We do not need to find the perfect canvas or fill out a predetermined set of templates. We need to know which question we are trying to answer, which uncertainty we want to reduce, what evidence would be sufficient to make a decision, and which tool can help us get there.

There is, however, an important limit. A methodology created to reduce waste can become a source of waste if it requires analyses, documents, and decisions that are more complex than the initiative needs. The depth of the analysis should be proportional to the existing uncertainty, the impact of the decision, and the cost of being wrong. We do not need to eliminate all uncertainty before acting. We need to obtain enough information to justify the next step.

The methodology, in this sense, does not prescribe a sequence of activities. It provides a structure for deciding what needs to be understood, what level of depth is appropriate, and how that understanding can be transformed into action and learning.

## Problem & Opportunity Discovery

An initiative usually begins with the perception that something could be different. There is a situation that produces a problem, a need that is not being adequately addressed, or a change that may create room for a new approach. From this perception, we need to understand the situation and assess whether it represents an opportunity worth exploring.

The first question concerns the problem. Who experiences it? In what context does it occur? How frequently? What are its causes and consequences? What are we actually observing, and what are we merely interpreting? It is common to start an initiative already convinced that we understand the problem because it seems obvious. But a complaint may only be a symptom, a stated need may conceal another need, and a problematic situation may be real without representing an opportunity significant enough to justify an initiative.

From this understanding, we can investigate the opportunity. Why look at this problem now? What has changed? Is there some technological, behavioral, economic, regulatory, or market circumstance that makes a different approach possible? For whom could this change create value? The existence of a problem does not, by itself, imply the existence of an opportunity. We need to understand why it is worth investing effort in trying to solve it.

Interviews, observation, data analysis, exploratory research, root cause analysis, and techniques such as the Five Whys are some of the tools that can help with this process. Stakeholder maps, problem journeys, and other ways of organizing evidence can also be useful. The choice depends on the nature of the question. The methodology does not determine the tool; it determines what we need to understand.

The output can be consolidated into a Problem & Opportunity Artifact. It is not necessarily a specific template, but a clear record of what we have been able to understand up to that point: what the problem is, who is affected, the context in which it occurs, its main causes and consequences, which evidence supports or contradicts this understanding, what opportunity we have identified, and why it is relevant. The main assumptions and uncertainties that still need to be verified should also be explicit.

The objective is not to produce a definitive answer. It is to reach a sufficient level of understanding to make the next decision: is there a problem and opportunity hypothesis that justifies investigating a solution?

## Product Design

It is at this point that what we normally call a product begins to take shape. But Product Design should not begin with a list of features. It begins with the thesis that a particular solution can create value for particular people, solve one or more identified problems, and produce a relevant outcome.

This relationship does not need to be one-to-one. The same problem may admit different solutions, and exploring alternatives may be precisely the best way to discover which proposal has the greatest potential. Likewise, a product may solve different problems, provided there is a coherent relationship between them and that this combination produces value. Therefore, defining the problem does not automatically determine the product. It establishes a space of possibilities that we need to explore.

What solution are we proposing? For whom? What problems does it intend to solve? What value does it intend to create? What outcome do we expect to produce? What capabilities are necessary to deliver that value? What needs to be true for the proposal to work? And, above all, what do we need to discover before investing in the build?

This principle also appears in “[Why I Would Start a Startup with More Than One Core Activity Today](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/por-que-eu-abriria-hoje-uma-startup)”, where I discuss the need to think about the startup from the perspective of a broader thesis before reducing it to the execution of a single idea. Here, the question is similar: the product is a hypothesis about how to transform an opportunity into something that effectively creates value, not simply a list of what we have decided to build.

Prototyping, experiments, interviews, alternative analysis, story mapping, and different canvases can help structure this reasoning. A prototype can allow us to test a proposal before building it. An experiment can verify a specific hypothesis. A canvas can help organize the relationships between the problem, users, value proposition, and capabilities. None of these tools defines the product by itself. They are instruments for thinking about and testing the thesis.

The output is a Product Design Artifact. It should make explicit the relevant users and other beneficiaries, the problems we intend to solve, the value proposition, the expected outcome, the product's main capabilities, its initial scope, and the assumptions that need to be true for the solution to work. It should also make clear which of these assumptions have not yet been verified.

This difference is important because a list of features or a completed canvas can create a misleading sense of progress. The Artifact, on the other hand, needs to make the product hypothesis explicit enough to be questioned, tested, and modified. Its purpose is not to describe the final product, but to record what we believe can create value, what needs to exist for that to happen, and which decisions still depend on evidence.

The objective, therefore, is not to define in advance everything that will be built. It is to formulate a product thesis clear enough for us to test whether the proposed solution actually has the potential to transform the identified opportunity into value.

## Business & Market Design

A solution can create value without necessarily constituting a business. Likewise, there can be an apparently interesting business model that cannot reach the market under conditions that make it viable. Therefore, we need to understand both how value will be captured and how the product will reach the people or organizations that can use it.

Who are the customers? Who are the users? Who pays? For what? How much? How does acquisition happen? Which channels are necessary? How does the intended audience discover the solution? What influences adoption? What barriers exist between the existence of the product and its effective use?

These questions do not need to produce a single answer. We can test different customer segments, pricing models, acquisition channels, sales approaches, and value capture mechanisms. A particular segment may not respond to the proposal, just as a pricing model, acquisition channel, or commercial approach may prove ineffective. This does not necessarily invalidate the product or the opportunity. It may simply indicate that that specific configuration of the business thesis does not hold.

Time is also part of this equation. An opportunity may depend on a market window, a regulatory change, an emerging technology, or a competitive condition that will not remain indefinitely. A product may be technically feasible and economically interesting, but reach the market too late. Time to market, therefore, is not merely an execution concern. It can be an assumption of the business thesis itself.

Business Model Canvas, Value Proposition Canvas, pricing analysis, unit economics, commercial interviews, channel tests, acquisition experiments, and other forms of investigation can help answer these questions. The choice depends on the uncertainties we need to reduce. A pricing experiment can reveal willingness to pay. A channel test can indicate whether we can reach the intended audience. A unit economics analysis can show that a particular commercial configuration is unviable. What matters is that each tool produces information capable of changing a decision.

The output can be consolidated into a Business & Market Design Artifact. It should make explicit the relevant customers and users, how the product reaches them, how it creates and captures value, the main assumptions regarding acquisition, pricing, and cost structure, and which opportunity window or time constraint needs to be considered. It should also make clear the hypotheses that still need to be tested.

The objective is not to prove that the business will work. It is to understand whether there is a plausible combination of product, market, economic model, and timing that justifies continuing to invest in the initiative.

The decision question for this dimension is: is there a plausible market and economic model configuration capable of sustaining the product within the existing opportunity window?

## UX Design

A product proposal can make sense and still fail in its interaction with the people who need to use it. UX Design addresses this dimension: how the solution will be experienced, how the user will interact with it, and how that interaction will allow the proposed value to actually be obtained.

What experience do we need to provide? What are the main tasks the user needs to perform? What information do they need to understand? What decisions do they need to make? Where are the points of friction? What needs to be simple, predictable, or obvious for the user to achieve the expected outcome?

Journeys, flows, wireframes, prototypes, usability tests, user research, and other UX techniques can help answer these questions. But the choice and depth of these activities need to be proportional to the decision we are trying to make. A product being validated may only need an experience that is good enough to test the proposal. It makes no sense to spend weeks refining an interface whose product hypothesis itself is still being questioned.

As I argued in “[Sometimes the Best UX Decision Is Not to Do UX at This Moment](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/as-vezes-a-melhor-decisao-de-ux-e)”, it is not necessary for every initiative to have a UX specialist from the very beginning. In early stages, whoever is leading the product may be able to define an experience suitable for the experiment. As the product matures, risk increases, or decisions become more complex, it may make sense to deepen the investigation and involve specialized professionals. What matters is that investment in UX follows uncertainty and the cost of being wrong, rather than a procedural obligation.

A poor experience can compromise the testing of a hypothesis without necessarily invalidating the product thesis. We may discover that the value proposition makes sense, but that a particular interaction makes it difficult to deliver. In this case, the learning concerns the experience, not necessarily the product as a whole.

The output can be consolidated into a UX Design Artifact. It should make explicit the main flows, tasks, interactions, information, and decisions required for the user to obtain the proposed value, as well as the experience hypotheses that still need to be verified. Depending on the stage and complexity, this understanding can be represented by a journey, a flow, a prototype, or another appropriate form of representation. What matters is making the experience we are proposing explicit so that it can be discussed and tested.

UX, therefore, is not a stage that needs to be completed before moving forward. It is a dimension that can be deepened as new cycles reveal that the experience has become one of the main sources of uncertainty.

The decision question for this dimension is: does the proposed experience allow the user to understand, use, and obtain the value the product intends to deliver?

## System Design

When the product thesis begins to take shape, the question of how to build it arises. The product defines the value we want to deliver and the capabilities we need to provide. System Design seeks to understand the system that needs to exist to support these capabilities.

What capabilities does the system need to provide? What components are necessary? How do they relate to one another? What integrations are required? What constraints need to be respected? Which quality attributes are relevant to the product? These questions help transform the product proposal into a hypothesis about the technological solution that can support it.

The architecture should not be built independently of the product. It needs to respond to what the product requires and to the conditions under which it needs to operate. An application that needs to process large transaction volumes has different needs from an internal tool used by a few people. A product whose differentiator depends on low latency will involve different decisions from one in which that attribute is irrelevant. Architecture needs to be subordinate to the initiative's actual needs, not to the technologies we know or would like to use.

This is one of the issues I discussed in “[The Role of the Solutions Architect in the Corporate World](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/o-papel-do-arquiteto-de-solucoes)”: architecture is not simply about choosing technologies, but about making decisions that reconcile business needs, technical constraints, and organizational context. Likewise, in “[Cloud Computing: Choosing Beyond the Default](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/cloud-computing-escolhendo-alem-do)”, I explored how adopting a technology or service because it is the available default does not replace analyzing the alternatives and trade-offs involved.

At the same time, technical decisions can feed back into the product thesis itself. A capability may be too expensive, too complex, too slow, or unfeasible within the existing constraints. An integration may depend on a system we do not control. A security or availability requirement may significantly change the cost of the solution. In these cases, technical discovery is not merely an implementation detail. It may require a change to the product, the business model, or even the opportunity itself that we are exploring.

C4, architecture diagrams, ADRs, technical prototypes, proofs of concept, and other instruments can help investigate these questions. The tool should be chosen according to the uncertainty. A simple architecture may be sufficient for an initial hypothesis, while a decision involving an unknown technology may justify a PoC before any larger commitment. Aspects such as security and resilience may require specific analyses when they are relevant to architectural decisions.

The output is a System Design Artifact. It should make explicit the main components and their relationships, relevant interfaces and integrations, important quality attributes and constraints, key architectural decisions, their trade-offs, and technical risks that still need to be investigated. The level of detail should be proportional to the stage of the initiative and the decisions that need to be made.

The objective is not to produce the definitive architecture. It is to build enough technical understanding to decide what can be built, under what conditions, and which uncertainties need to be resolved before moving forward.

The decision question for this dimension is: is there a plausible technological solution to support the product's necessary capabilities within the existing constraints?

## Domain & Data Design

Systems represent something. Before discussing tables, APIs, or storage structures, we need to understand the domain we are trying to represent. The problem does not begin in the database. It begins with what we know about the world that the system needs to model.

What concepts exist? Which entities are relevant? What states can they assume? What relationships exist between these concepts? What rules need to be preserved? What characterizes a state change? What information needs to be known for this domain to be represented coherently?

This distinction is important because the data model is a consequence of understanding the domain, not a substitute for it. It is possible to build a technically well-designed structure to represent an incorrect domain model. When this happens, the problem is not limited to persistence. An incorrect understanding of the domain tends to spread through API contracts, business rules, processes, interfaces, reports, and integrations, making subsequent decisions more difficult and expensive to modify.

Domain modeling, Event Storming, entity-relationship modeling, domain glossaries, and other techniques can help build and challenge this understanding. The choice depends on the nature and complexity of the domain. In some cases, a simple representation will be sufficient. In others, it will be necessary to involve different people from the organization to confront interpretations, identify conceptual conflicts, and reach a shared understanding.

The output is a Domain & Data Design Artifact. It should make explicit the main domain concepts, their entities and relationships, relevant states, business rules, and the data needed to represent this context. It should also record ambiguities, modeling decisions, and assumptions that still need to be verified.

The objective is not to immediately produce a physical database model. It is to build a sufficiently consistent representation of the domain to guide technical decisions and reduce the risk of turning an incorrect understanding of reality into structural decisions that are difficult to revise later.

The decision question for this dimension is: do we sufficiently understand the domain and the information that needs to be represented to build a solution consistent with the reality we are trying to model?

## Risk & Security Design

Every initiative involves risks. Some are related to the product and market; others to technology, data, operations, organization, or the ability to execute itself. Not all need to be eliminated, and not all justify specific controls. The first step is to understand which risks may compromise the thesis and decide which ones need to be addressed.

Risk & Security Design seeks to make these risks explicit and understand how they should influence the initiative's decisions. What can go wrong? What is the probability of that happening? What would be the impact? Which assets, people, processes, or capabilities could be affected? Which risks are acceptable? Which need to be reduced, transferred, avoided, or monitored? What controls are necessary, and what will be the cost of implementing them?

This perspective is related to what I discussed in “[Risk Is an Organizational Responsibility](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/risco-e-uma-responsabilidade-organizacional)”. Risk is not an exclusive concern of a specialized area. The decisions that create risk are usually made within the initiative's own dimensions: choosing a business model, defining an architecture, storing certain data, depending on a supplier, automating a process, or prioritizing a particular delivery are all decisions that also create risk exposure.

Security is one of these dimensions. A solution may be technically feasible and economically interesting, but present confidentiality, integrity, availability, privacy, or abuse risks that make its adoption inappropriate. Threat modeling, risk analysis, abuse cases, security architecture, control analysis, security testing, and other techniques can help investigate these questions. The choice depends on the nature of the initiative and the risks involved.

At the same time, security cannot simply be treated as the maximization of controls. As I discussed in “[The Security Dilemma: Between Control and Speed](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/o-dilema-de-security-entre-o-controle)”, controls also have a cost, can introduce friction, and can reduce execution speed. The objective is not to make the initiative risk-free, something that is rarely possible, but to find an appropriate relationship between risk, control, cost, and speed. In some cases, consciously accepting a particular risk may be a better decision than eliminating it.

Discoveries in this dimension can also feed back into the others. A risk may require a change in architecture, alter the user experience, increase operational cost, modify the business model, or even make a particular opportunity unviable. Likewise, a product decision may create risks that need to be addressed before the initiative can move forward.

The output is a Risk & Security Design Artifact. It should make explicit the main risks identified, their potential impacts, relevant security requirements, necessary controls, accepted risks, and the main uncertainties that still need to be investigated. When appropriate, it should also record decisions regarding the treatment, transfer, or acceptance of risks.

The objective is not to produce a risk-free initiative. It is to build enough understanding to decide which risks we are willing to assume, which need to be addressed, and which conditions need to be met for the initiative to move forward.

## Management Design

At some point, we need to answer not only what to build, but who needs to build and operate it. A thesis can be technically and economically plausible and still not be executable with the available capabilities.

Management Design addresses the organizational capacity required to turn the thesis into execution. What skills are necessary? What needs to be inside the team? What can be provided externally? How will responsibilities be distributed? Which decisions need to be made, and by whom? What management capacity will be required as the initiative evolves?

But an organization is not merely a structure of people. It is also a structure of communication, decision-making, and incentives. In “[The Engineering of Organizational Communication](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/a-engenharia-da-comunicacao-organizacional)”, I discussed how the flow of information determines the distance between operational reality and those who make decisions. In “[People, Incentives, and Culture as Mechanisms for Strategy Execution](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/pessoas-incentivos-e-cultura-como)”, the perspective is complementary: even when people have sufficient information, execution depends on the incentives and mechanisms that guide their behavior.

This means we need to think not only about who will do each thing, but also about how the organization will know what is happening, how decisions will be made, how information will flow, and how people will be guided to act. A structure can define responsibilities and still produce slow decisions. A team may have the necessary skills and still lack the right incentives to execute the strategy. An organization may have well-defined processes and still depend excessively on knowledge concentrated in a few people.

There is no universal organizational structure, just as there is no universal way to conduct an initiative. The tailoring principle I discussed in “[PMBOK Is Not a Cookbook: The Role of Tailoring in Project Management](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/pmbok-nao-e-receita-de-bolo-o-papel)” applies directly here. The structure needs to be appropriate to the context, stage of the initiative, level of uncertainty, and available capabilities. An early-stage initiative may depend on a few generalists, while a more mature operation may require different specialization, leadership, and coordination mechanisms.

Organizational design, capability mapping, role and responsibility definition, RACI, skills analysis, decision-mechanism design, and capacity planning are some of the tools that can help with this reasoning. The objective is not to produce a sophisticated organizational chart, but to understand which capabilities need to exist and how information, authority, responsibility, and decision-making should relate to one another so that the initiative can execute and learn.

The output is a Management Design Artifact. It should make explicit the capabilities necessary to execute and operate the initiative, the roles and responsibilities involved, critical skills, what will be performed internally and what may be provided externally, the main capability gaps, and the relevant mechanisms for decision-making, communication, governance, and coordination.

The point is to recognize that the organization is also part of the solution. It is not enough to know what needs to be built and how it should work. We need to know whether the organizational capacity necessary to make it happen exists or can be created.

The decision question for this dimension is: do we have, or can we create, the organizational capacity necessary to execute and evolve the initiative in a coordinated manner?

## Sales & Customer Success Design

Having a product that solves a problem and a plausible business model still does not mean knowing how to acquire it, sell it, and ensure that the customer obtains the promised value. There is a specific dimension of the initiative dedicated to understanding how the relationship with the customer takes place before, during, and after the purchase.

Who buys? Who participates in the decision? How is the proposal presented? Which objections need to be overcome? What needs to happen for someone to be willing to pay? How does the purchase happen? And afterward, how does the customer begin using the solution, perceive value, and continue using it?

Sales & Customer Success Design seeks to make this relationship explicit. In some businesses, acquisition may be transactional and practically autonomous. In others, it may involve negotiation, demonstration, implementation, training, and ongoing relationship management. Likewise, Customer Success may be a core business capability or a relatively minor concern, depending on the product and relationship model.

This perspective is also related to what I discussed in “[When the Sale Is Completed, the Relationship Has Just Begun](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/quando-a-venda-se-concretiza-o-relacionamento)”: signing a contract does not end the relationship with the customer. It transfers responsibility to a new stage, in which the customer needs to actually obtain the value that motivated the purchase. If this does not happen, the sale may even appear as a short-term success, but the business thesis begins to lose its support.

Sales discovery, customer interviews, analysis of the acquisition process, pilots, demonstrations, onboarding experiments, customer journey definition, and activation, retention, and expansion metrics are some of the tools that can help investigate these questions. Again, there is no mandatory set. A consultative B2B sale requires different reasoning from a consumer product purchased directly by the user.

The output is a Sales & Customer Success Design Artifact. It should make explicit the buyers and other participants in the decision, the acquisition and contracting process, the main channels and touchpoints, the steps necessary to convert an opportunity into a customer, and the mechanisms necessary for that customer to obtain the promised value. It should also make explicit the main hypotheses regarding acquisition, conversion, activation, retention, and expansion that still need to be verified.

The objective is not to design a definitive commercial operation. It is to understand how the value proposition becomes a customer relationship and, after the purchase, effectively perceived value. This understanding will be one of the inputs for designing the operation necessary to sustain the service.

The decision question for this dimension is: can we transform the value proposition into acquisition, contracting, adoption, and recurring value generation for the customer?

## Operations & Support Design

Building the product does not necessarily mean knowing how to operate and sustain it. A solution that works technically may depend on manual processes, service, support, monitoring, exception handling, controls, and other activities to function consistently in the real world.

Operations & Support Design seeks to make explicit the capabilities necessary to deliver, operate, and sustain the service. How will the service be delivered? What activities need to exist before, during, and after the interaction with the user? What happens when something goes wrong? Which exceptions need to be handled? How will service and support be provided? Which activities can be automated? What external dependencies exist? What operational capacity will be required to sustain the expected volume?

Thinking about this dimension before building also allows us to discover that certain product characteristics may be difficult or expensive to operate or sustain. An apparently simple feature may require recurring manual intervention. A process may depend on an activity that does not scale. An exception may require controls that significantly alter the experience or cost of the service. A support need may require capabilities that were not anticipated in the initial design. In such cases, operational discovery may require changes to the product, experience, or business model itself.

Process mapping, service blueprint, procedure definition, capacity analysis, support process design, and operational experiments are some of the tools that can help with this reasoning. In an initial initiative, operations may be deliberately manual to allow the hypothesis to be tested with little investment. As volume grows or certain activities become recurring, it may make sense to automate, transfer, or redesign them.

The output is an Operations & Support Design Artifact. It should make explicit the main processes required to deliver, operate, and sustain the service, the roles involved, control and handoff points, relevant exceptions, external dependencies, and activities that depend on manual intervention or automation. It should also highlight the main risks, bottlenecks, and capacity constraints that still need to be investigated.

The objective is not to design definitive operations and support before the product exists. It is to understand what will be necessary so that what we are building can be delivered, operated, and sustained consistently and sustainably once it is put into operation.

The decision question for this dimension is: can we deliver, operate, and sustain the service under the volume, quality, cost, and operational complexity conditions required by the initiative?

## Finance & Investments Design

Every initiative operates under economic constraints. A good product thesis may not survive a cost structure incompatible with the market, just as a relevant opportunity may not be executable with the available resources. The financial dimension seeks to make these constraints explicit and understand which economic conditions need to be true for the initiative to move forward.

How much does it cost to reach the next stage? Which costs are fixed and which vary with volume? What financial capacity is required to sustain execution? Which scenarios could compromise continuity? How long can the initiative operate before it needs to generate revenue or obtain new resources? The objective is not to produce a perfect forecast, but to identify the variables that may determine the viability of the thesis and the uncertainties that need to be reduced.

This concern is at the center of “[Good Ideas Break Too](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/boas-ideias-tambem-quebram)”. An initiative can be creating value and still fail because its resources run out before the business reaches sustainable conditions. The financial constraint, therefore, is not merely a consequence of strategy. It can modify the strategy itself, the pace of execution, the product scope, and the choice of experiments.

The investment dimension adds another question: given what we know today, how much is it worth continuing to invest in this initiative? Not every next step requires more capital, and not every investment should be justified by a long-term return projection. Investment can be incremental and conditioned on the learning produced at each stage. The decision is to understand how much resource should be committed, for what purpose, at what point, and under what conditions we should continue, reduce, or stop the allocation.

When available resources are not sufficient to sustain execution, the question of how to finance the initiative also arises. How much capital is needed? For what purpose? At what point? For how long? Under what conditions? The decision is not simply to raise money, but to understand how different financing alternatives affect the ability to execute the thesis, the pace of growth, and the commitments assumed to make it viable.

A financial model, unit economics analysis, cash flow projections, scenarios, sensitivity analysis, and different ways of structuring capital requirements can help with this reasoning. The tool depends on the stage and nature of the initiative. A social initiative, a small business, and a high-growth startup will have very different financial needs and alternatives.

The output is a Finance & Investments Design Artifact. It should make explicit the main costs and revenue assumptions, relevant economic conditions, capital requirements, financial horizon, scenarios representing greater risk, and, where applicable, financing alternatives and their implications for execution. It should also make explicit the assumptions that condition continued investment and the criteria that may justify increasing, maintaining, reducing, or stopping the allocation of resources.

Not every initiative will need external investment. Some may be financed by the founders themselves, by generated revenue, by an internal budget, or by resources already available. The point is to recognize that every initiative has economic constraints and that, when they are relevant to execution, they need to be part of the thesis from the beginning.

The decision question for this dimension is: given the available evidence and existing economic constraints, how much is it worth investing to reach the next stage, and under what conditions should we continue investing?

## Roadmap

At some point, we need to decide what to do first. This is when the accumulated understanding needs to be transformed into an execution strategy. The Roadmap consolidates current decisions about how to move forward, but it does not need to be a final stage and does not assume that all dimensions of the initiative have already been resolved.

It is natural for the backlog to grow as we think about the initiative. Features, improvements, experiments, integrations, fixes, and new possibilities emerge. The backlog represents this universe of possibilities; the roadmap represents what we have decided to do, and in what order, given the existing constraints and priorities. The problem begins when we treat everything as equally important. When everything is a priority, nothing is.

The MVP should also not be defined simply as the smallest possible set of features. It should be understood as what needs to be done first to test a relevant hypothesis and produce enough evidence to guide the next decision. In some cases, this will be a functional product. In others, it may be an experiment, a prototype, a manual operation, or a combination of these elements. What defines the MVP is not its size, but the learning it needs to produce.

A Roadmap, therefore, should not simply be a list of features distributed across dates. It is a representation of how we intend to move forward given the existing dependencies, constraints, capabilities, and uncertainties. The question is not only what needs to be built, but what needs to be discovered, decided, or validated before each next step.

What needs to be discovered before building? What depends on a previous decision? Which experiment can reduce an important uncertainty? What needs to be built to enable the next learning? Which activities are critical to time to market? Which commitments need to be made now, and which can still be postponed?

This view also changes the relationship between the roadmap and planning. The objective is not to predict the entire execution with precision, something particularly difficult when relevant hypotheses still need to be tested. The roadmap should organize the next steps with a horizon and level of precision compatible with what we know. As execution and learning cycles produce new evidence, the roadmap needs to be revised.

A roadmap can be structured with different tools, from a simple representation of milestones and dependencies to more elaborate planning, prioritization, and portfolio management techniques. The choice depends on the complexity of the initiative and the level of detail required for the decision.

The output is the Roadmap itself. It should represent the sequence of initiatives, experiments, decisions, builds, and deliveries necessary to advance the thesis, making clear the main dependencies, priorities, and uncertainties that condition this sequence. It does not need to predict the entire future. It needs to make explicit the path that, given what we know today, makes the most sense to follow.

For this reason, the roadmap is a consequence of the thesis and, at the same time, an instrument for testing it. It transforms understanding into action, and action produces new learning that may require the roadmap to be redone. This is how planning and execution remain connected without one being confused with the other.

The decision question is: given what we know today, what is the next sequence of actions that maximizes our ability to advance the thesis and reduce the most relevant uncertainties?

## An Initial Proposal

We have an enormous number of techniques, frameworks, and canvases for thinking through different parts of an initiative. What seems to be missing to me is a layer that connects them around a process of reasoning, decision-making, and learning.

The structure presented in this article is an initial proposal for a methodology. It is not a closed framework, a mandatory sequence, or an execution recipe. The steps presented are dimensions that I consider relevant for structuring an initiative, but their application depends on the reality of each case. Some can be combined, others may require greater depth, and others may simply not make sense in a particular context. The order may also change according to the nature of the initiative, its stage, its constraints, and what we already know.

It would be possible to add many other dimensions based on what already exists in disciplines such as Lean Startup, PMBOK, TOGAF, and various other frameworks and methods. It would also be possible to decompose some of the dimensions presented here even further. For now, I preferred to focus on what I consider essential for transforming a poorly structured idea into an initiative that can be understood, questioned, and executed.

This does not mean replacing existing methods. Lean Startup, for example, offers important principles for working with hypotheses, experimentation, and learning. PMBOK provides a comprehensive foundation for project management. TOGAF organizes different aspects of enterprise architecture. The proposal here operates at another level: creating a structure that helps us understand which dimensions of an initiative need to be considered and, from there, choose which methods and tools are appropriate for each situation.

The same applies to the tools. A canvas, an interview, a prototype, a financial model, an architecture diagram, or any other technique only makes sense when it helps answer a relevant question. The methodology should not determine which tool to use, but help identify what we need to understand, which uncertainty we are trying to reduce, and what evidence would be sufficient to make a decision.

The principle connecting these dimensions is to make the thesis explicit, identify its uncertainties, plan the next step, execute, observe the result, and use that learning to decide what to do next. It is this cycle that prevents both planning from turning into procrastination and execution from turning into movement without direction.

In the end, putting an idea on paper does not mean trying to make it ready before executing it. It means making it clear enough for us to know what we are trying to discover.

And then start.
