An initiative should begin by transforming an intuitive idea into an explicit, questionable representation before execution. Disconnected canvases and frameworks can fragment reasoning; the core problem is not lack of tools but lack of a methodology connecting questions, reasoning, evidence, outputs, decisions, and learning. Writing an idea down is valuable because it exposes assumptions, distinguishes observations from interpretations and desired solutions from perceived needs, and creates a basis for questioning the thesis. This applies to startups, products, transformations, social projects, process changes, and other initiatives.

Thinking and doing are complementary rather than opposing approaches. Excessive planning can avoid contact with reality, while execution without a defined learning objective can become movement without learning. Lean Startup provides hypothesis-experiment-learning principles, while PDCA provides a broader cycle connecting planning, execution, verification, and action. Planning should prepare the next decision rather than predict all execution; execution should produce both outputs and knowledge. Uncertainty does not need to be eliminated before action: some uncertainty can be reduced through reasoning, while some requires contact with reality. Planning becomes wasteful once sufficient information exists to execute, just as execution becomes wasteful when the initiative does not know what it is trying to discover.

A proposed methodology organizes an initiative around dimensions representing distinct sets of decisions, questions, uncertainties, and evidence. These dimensions are perspectives rather than a mandatory linear sequence, and not every initiative requires all of them or equal depth. Existing approaches such as Lean Startup, Design Thinking, Customer Development, Business Model Canvas, Venture Design, PMBOK, and TOGAF already contain relevant techniques; the proposal is not to replace or claim novelty over them, but to provide a structure for deciding which questions need answers, which uncertainty should be reduced, what evidence is sufficient, which tools are useful, and which Artifact should consolidate the resulting understanding.

The fundamental relationship is: question → reasoning → tool → evidence → output/Artifact → decision → action → new learning. A tool is selected because it helps answer a relevant question, not because a framework requires it. An Artifact is not necessarily a large or definitive document; it is any sufficiently explicit set of decisions, hypotheses, evidence, models, or representations that allows people to understand and challenge the current reasoning. Negative findings are valid outcomes: disproven assumptions, infeasible approaches, and decisions to stop investing are forms of knowledge. Analysis depth should be proportional to uncertainty, decision impact, and the cost of being wrong. A methodology intended to reduce waste becomes wasteful if it demands unnecessary analysis, documentation, or decisions.

Problem & Opportunity Discovery establishes whether a meaningful problem and associated opportunity exist. It examines who experiences the problem, context, frequency, causes, consequences, observed evidence versus interpretation, and why the problem matters now. A problem does not automatically constitute an opportunity; technological, behavioral, economic, regulatory, or market changes may determine whether solving it is worth pursuing. Interviews, observation, data analysis, exploratory research, root-cause analysis, Five Whys, stakeholder maps, and problem journeys may be used according to the question. The Problem & Opportunity Artifact records the current understanding, supporting or contradictory evidence, opportunity rationale, assumptions, and unresolved uncertainties. Its decision question is whether there is sufficient problem/opportunity evidence to justify investigating a solution.

Product Design formulates a product thesis rather than a feature list. The thesis proposes that a particular solution can create value for particular people by addressing identified problems and producing an expected outcome. Problems do not uniquely determine solutions; multiple alternatives may need exploration, and a product can address multiple coherent problems. The analysis asks what solution is proposed, for whom, what problems it addresses, what value and outcome it should create, what capabilities are required, what must be true, and what needs to be discovered before investment in building. Prototypes, experiments, interviews, alternatives analysis, story mapping, and canvases can support this reasoning. The Product Design Artifact records users and beneficiaries, problems, value proposition, expected outcome, capabilities, initial scope, and unverified assumptions. Its purpose is to make the product hypothesis testable and modifiable, not to define the final product. The decision question is whether the proposed solution plausibly transforms the identified opportunity into value.

Business & Market Design examines whether the product can reach a market and capture value under viable conditions. It considers customers versus users, payer, pricing, acquisition, channels, discovery, adoption influences, barriers, alternative customer segments, commercial approaches, and value-capture mechanisms. A product hypothesis may remain valid even if a specific segment, pricing model, channel, or commercial configuration fails. Timing is also part of the business thesis because market windows may depend on technological, regulatory, behavioral, economic, or competitive conditions. Business Model Canvas, Value Proposition Canvas, pricing analysis, unit economics, commercial interviews, channel tests, and acquisition experiments can provide evidence. The Business & Market Design Artifact records customers/users, value creation and capture, acquisition, pricing, costs, timing constraints, and unresolved business hypotheses. The decision question is whether a plausible product-market-economic configuration can sustain the product within the relevant opportunity window.

UX Design examines whether users can actually obtain the proposed value through the interaction with the product. It considers required experience, tasks, information, decisions, friction points, and aspects that must be simple, predictable, or obvious. Journeys, flows, wireframes, prototypes, usability tests, and user research can be applied according to the uncertainty and decision at hand. UX investment should follow uncertainty and cost of being wrong rather than procedural requirements; an early experiment may need only sufficient experience to test the product thesis, while increasing maturity or complexity may justify specialized UX work. A poor experience can invalidate an interaction hypothesis without necessarily invalidating the product thesis. The UX Design Artifact records relevant flows, tasks, interactions, information, decisions, and experience hypotheses. The decision question is whether the proposed experience enables users to understand, use, and obtain the intended value.

System Design translates product capabilities into a technological solution hypothesis. It examines required capabilities, components, relationships, integrations, constraints, quality attributes, and operating conditions. Architecture should be subordinate to actual product needs and contextual constraints rather than preferred technologies or defaults. Technical decisions can also modify the product or business thesis when capabilities are too expensive, complex, slow, insecure, dependent on uncontrollable systems, or otherwise infeasible. C4, architecture diagrams, ADRs, technical prototypes, PoCs, security/resilience analyses, and similar tools should be selected according to uncertainty. The System Design Artifact records components, interfaces, integrations, quality attributes, constraints, architectural decisions, trade-offs, and technical risks at an appropriate level of detail. The decision question is whether a plausible technological solution can support the product capabilities under existing constraints.

Domain & Data Design establishes what the system needs to represent before determining database or storage structures. It identifies concepts, entities, states, relationships, rules, state transitions, and required information. A data model is a consequence of domain understanding rather than a substitute for it; an incorrect domain model can propagate into APIs, business rules, processes, interfaces, reports, and integrations, making change more expensive. Domain modeling, Event Storming, entity-relationship modeling, and domain glossaries can create and challenge shared understanding. The Domain & Data Design Artifact records concepts, entities, relationships, states, business rules, required data, ambiguities, modeling decisions, and unresolved assumptions. The decision question is whether the domain and information requirements are sufficiently understood to build a solution consistent with the reality being modeled.

Risk & Security Design makes risks explicit across product, market, technology, data, operations, organization, and execution. It asks what can go wrong, probability and impact, affected assets or capabilities, acceptable risk, treatment options, required controls, and control costs. Risk is an organizational responsibility because ordinary initiative decisions create risk exposure; security is one dimension of this broader risk context. Security considerations include confidentiality, integrity, availability, privacy, and abuse. Threat modeling, risk analysis, abuse cases, security architecture, control analysis, and security testing can be used according to relevant risks. Controls themselves create cost, friction, and execution constraints, so the goal is not zero risk but an appropriate relationship among risk, control, cost, and speed; consciously accepting a risk can sometimes be preferable to eliminating it. The Risk & Security Design Artifact records major risks, impacts, security requirements, controls, accepted risks, treatment decisions, and unresolved uncertainties. The decision question is which risks can be accepted, which require treatment, and what conditions must hold for continuation.

Management Design determines whether the initiative has the organizational capacity to execute and evolve. It considers required skills, internal versus external capabilities, responsibilities, decision authority, management capacity, communication, incentives, governance, and coordination. Organizational structure alone is insufficient: information flow affects decision quality, incentives affect behavior, and concentrated knowledge can create dependency even when formal processes exist. There is no universal structure; organizational design should be tailored to stage, context, uncertainty, and available capabilities. Organizational design, capability mapping, role definitions, RACI, skills analysis, decision-mechanism design, and capacity planning can help. The Management Design Artifact records required capabilities, roles, responsibilities, critical skills, internal/external allocation, capability gaps, and mechanisms for communication, decision-making, governance, and coordination. The decision question is whether the necessary organizational capacity exists or can be created.

Sales & Customer Success Design addresses the customer relationship before, during, and after purchase. It examines buyers and decision participants, presentation of the proposition, objections, willingness to pay, purchasing mechanics, onboarding, adoption, perceived value, retention, and expansion. Acquisition and customer success models vary by context; a transactional consumer product differs from a consultative B2B sale. Completing a sale does not guarantee realized customer value, and failure to deliver that value can undermine the business thesis. Sales discovery, customer interviews, acquisition analysis, pilots, demonstrations, onboarding experiments, customer journeys, and activation/retention/expansion metrics can provide evidence. The Sales & Customer Success Design Artifact records buyers, decision participants, acquisition and contracting process, channels, touchpoints, conversion steps, mechanisms for value realization, and unresolved hypotheses. The decision question is whether the value proposition can be converted into acquisition, contracting, adoption, and recurring customer value.

Operations & Support Design determines whether the product can be delivered, operated, and sustained consistently. It examines delivery activities, operational processes, exceptions, failures, support, monitoring, controls, external dependencies, automation, and capacity requirements. Product characteristics may create operational costs or complexity that require changes to product, UX, or business model. Early operations can deliberately be manual to test hypotheses cheaply; recurring or scaling activities may later require automation, transfer, or redesign. Process mapping, service blueprints, procedures, capacity analysis, support design, and operational experiments can help. The Operations & Support Design Artifact records core processes, roles, handoffs, controls, exceptions, dependencies, manual/automated activities, bottlenecks, risks, and capacity constraints. The decision question is whether the service can be delivered and sustained at required volume, quality, cost, and operational complexity.

Finance & Investments Design identifies the economic conditions required to sustain the initiative. It considers fixed and variable costs, cost to reach the next stage, financial capacity, continuity risks, revenue timing, financial horizon, and scenarios. The objective is not perfect forecasting but identification of variables that determine viability and uncertainties requiring reduction. Resource constraints can alter strategy, execution pace, scope, and experiments. Investment should be incremental and conditioned on learning when appropriate, with explicit consideration of how much resource to commit, for what purpose, when, and under what conditions to increase, maintain, reduce, or stop allocation. Financing decisions consider capital needs, timing, duration, conditions, execution implications, growth pace, and commitments. Financial models, unit economics, cash-flow projections, scenarios, sensitivity analysis, and capital-structure analysis can support the reasoning. The Finance & Investments Design Artifact records cost and revenue assumptions, economic conditions, capital requirements, horizon, risk scenarios, financing alternatives, and investment-continuation criteria. External investment is not inherently necessary; initiatives may rely on founders, revenue, internal budgets, or existing resources. The decision question is how much should be invested to reach the next stage given current evidence and economic constraints, and under what conditions investment should continue.

Roadmap converts accumulated understanding into a current execution strategy without assuming that all dimensions are resolved. A backlog represents possible work; a roadmap represents selected work and sequencing under current constraints and priorities. An MVP is not necessarily the smallest feature set or even a complete product; it is whatever combination of product, experiment, prototype, manual operation, or other activity is necessary to test a relevant hypothesis and produce sufficient evidence for the next decision. The roadmap therefore includes not only builds but discoveries, experiments, decisions, dependencies, and validation activities. It should make explicit what must be discovered before building, dependencies, uncertainty-reduction experiments, enabling work, time-to-market activities, and commitments that can or cannot be deferred. Precision should match current knowledge because future execution cannot be predicted reliably while important hypotheses remain unresolved. The roadmap is both a consequence of the current thesis and an instrument for testing it; new learning requires revision. Its decision question is what sequence of actions, given current knowledge, best advances the thesis while reducing relevant uncertainties.

The methodology remains an initial, non-closed proposal rather than a mandatory process or execution recipe. Dimensions can be combined, deepened, omitted, or reordered according to initiative, stage, constraints, and existing knowledge. Additional dimensions could be derived from established disciplines, and existing dimensions could be decomposed further; the current scope focuses on what is considered essential for transforming a poorly structured idea into an initiative that can be understood, questioned, and executed. Existing methods such as Lean Startup, PMBOK, and TOGAF remain useful because they operate at different levels or address particular disciplines. The proposed layer helps identify which dimensions need attention and consequently which methods and tools are appropriate.

The central principle is to make the initiative's thesis explicit, identify its uncertainties, determine the evidence needed for the next decision, plan an appropriate next action, execute, observe results, update understanding, and repeat. Putting an idea on paper does not mean making it execution-ready before acting; it means making it sufficiently explicit to know what is being assumed and what needs to be discovered. Execution should then begin as part of the learning cycle.
