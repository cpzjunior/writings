# What Is the Most Suitable Market for My Company?

_A panorama of markets, ecosystems, and conditions for entrepreneurship in Brazil and abroad._

**Summary:** There is no universally better market in which to build a business: the choice depends on the combination of demand, willingness to pay, competition, entry costs, and, above all, the team's own ability to capture the opportunity. Therefore, market size, taxes, or incentives in isolation say little; it is also necessary to consider talent, capital, regulation, infrastructure, founders' advantages, and the company's stage. Brazil can be a market, laboratory, or operational base, while other countries may offer better conditions for selling, raising capital, or scaling. In the end, the most suitable market is the one in which this company can turn its advantages into economically sustainable growth.

---

My mother likes reading about entrepreneurship abroad. From time to time, she sends me an article about a company that was born in the United States, a program for entrepreneurs in Portugal, an incentive offered by a European country, or the story of someone who decided to leave Brazil to build a company somewhere else. In one of these conversations, she asked me to put together a general overview: where it is worth starting a business, which markets offer better opportunities, and when it makes sense to start in Brazil or look for another country.

The question seems simple, but choosing a market involves much more than comparing countries. It is necessary to understand where demand exists, who buys, how much they are willing to pay, who the company will compete against, and how much it costs to reach that customer. Then come factors such as culture, regulation, talent, capital, infrastructure, and growth conditions.

Moreover, the consumer market does not need to coincide with the company's location. Customers, founders, engineering, operations, and corporate structure can be in different countries. And the same opportunity can be much more accessible to one team than to another, depending on its knowledge, experience, network, and execution capabilities.

Before comparing countries and regions, therefore, it is worth understanding these variables and the main trade-offs involved. From there, we can observe how they appear in practice in Brazil, the United States, Europe, Latin America, and Asia.

## Product and market go hand in hand

Product and market need to be analyzed together because the same product can have very different economic value depending on where it is sold. Costs, problems, available alternatives, purchasing power, and willingness to pay change from one country to another.

In some cases, the entrepreneur starts from a problem and looks for the markets where it is most relevant. In others, they develop a technology and look for where there is the greatest willingness to pay for it. They may also start by observing a market and identifying products that still address its needs poorly. There is no single sequence.

In technology, this appears quite concretely. An automation tool may have little value where the process is cheap and simple, but become much more attractive where the same activity depends on expensive labor. The technology is the same; the economic return for the customer is different.

The opposite also happens. Specific characteristics of a market can create opportunities for products that would hardly emerge in another context. Brazil's tax complexity, financial system, agribusiness, and infrastructure challenges, for example, have created demand for highly specific solutions. Some may remain concentrated in Brazil; others can be adapted to countries with similar problems.

This makes the size of the economy an insufficient measure. A company may find tens of thousands of potential customers in the United States and still have a better opportunity in Brazil if it can reach a larger share of those customers, charge an appropriate price, and operate with a lower CAC. The larger market is only better if the company can capture it in an economically viable way.

TAM, SAM, and SOM help put this difference into numbers. TAM represents the total potential market; SAM, the portion compatible with the chosen product and geography; and SOM, the portion the company can realistically capture. When comparing countries, therefore, it is not enough to ask where the largest TAM is. It is necessary to estimate how much of that market is accessible and what position the company can build.

In practice, the analysis starts with five questions: what problem does the product solve, for whom does it solve it, how much does that problem cost the customer, how much is the customer willing to pay, and how many customers can be reached in an economically viable way. It is this combination that turns a potential market into a commercial opportunity.

## Who buys, how much do they pay, and who am I competing against?

Before comparing countries, it is necessary to define who actually buys the product. Population is not synonymous with customers. A B2C company may depend on millions of consumers; a B2B company can build a relevant business with a few hundred companies; a company that sells to the government depends on public procurement processes; an industrial company may depend on distributors and local partners.

It is also important to separate the user, buyer, and payer. Software may be used by a team, approved by IT and security, and contracted by the executive responsible for the budget. In healthcare, the patient may be the user, while the hospital, insurer, or government pays. The more complex the purchasing process, the less useful it is to count only potential users.

The next point is willingness to pay. Purchasing power does not mean willingness to pay. An American consumer may have a high income and see no value in a particular product. A company in a lower-income market may pay a great deal for a solution if the problem it solves is expensive. An automation tool, for example, tends to generate more value where it replaces an expensive activity.

Therefore, market size needs to be analyzed together with capture capacity. A US$100 billion market can be worse for a startup than a US$1 billion market if there are highly capitalized competitors, high CAC, difficult distribution channels, and little room for differentiation. Likewise, a small niche may be excellent for a high-ticket product but insufficient for a business that depends on millions of customers.

TAM, SAM, and SOM help estimate the size of the opportunity. CAC, LTV, margin, and payback help determine whether capturing it makes economic sense. But these metrics also need to be analyzed according to the company's stage. The most suitable market for validating a product may not be the best one for scaling, raising capital, or reaching a particular margin. A company can start in a smaller, familiar market, learn more cheaply, and then move into larger markets. In other cases, the domestic market may be too small to justify that trajectory.

Competition also needs to be analyzed. Few competitors may indicate an underexplored opportunity or simply a market in which nobody has found an economically viable model. Many competitors may confirm the existence of demand but make entry more expensive. It is necessary to consider who the leaders are, how much capital they have, which channels they control, switching costs, network effects, regulatory barriers, and where there is still room for differentiation.

There is also the disadvantage of being foreign. A company entering a new market normally knows less about customers, has fewer relationships, less local reputation, and may face greater difficulty hiring, selling, and establishing partnerships. Overcoming this disadvantage has a cost and needs to be included in the CAC, time-to-market, and required capital calculations.

There is also an opportunity cost. Choosing a market means concentrating capital, time, and attention on one geography rather than another. A market may be attractive in isolation and still not be the best use of the resources available. The comparison should consider not only "does this market work?" but "is it better than the alternatives this company can execute?" The relevant market is the one the company can access, win, and serve in an economically sustainable way. Size is only one variable in this equation.

## Culture, regulation, and execution capacity

Even when there is demand and competitive space, it is still necessary to determine whether the company can sell and operate in that market. The way consumers and companies buy, negotiate, contract, and build trust varies between countries. Language is only one part of this. Speaking English does not automatically mean understanding the American market, just as speaking Spanish does not eliminate commercial differences between Latin American countries.

This adaptation can represent a significant entry cost. A foreign company may need to hire local salespeople, establish partners, adapt prices and contracts, modify its product and support, or build a brand that does not yet have a reputation in that market. The same product may work in two countries but require completely different commercial strategies. Therefore, a larger market is not necessarily the best market to start in.

Regulation can be even more decisive. In fintech, healthcare, insurance, education, energy, telecommunications, defense, artificial intelligence, and cybersecurity, licensing, data protection, taxation, intellectual property, and technical requirements can determine who can operate, which customers can be served, and how the product needs to be built. The cost of complying with these rules needs to be included in the economics of market entry.

But regulation is not only a barrier. It can also create demand. A new compliance requirement can create a market for software; changes in data protection can increase demand for security; environmental requirements can create opportunities in energy and monitoring. In some sectors, understanding the direction of regulation can be as important as measuring the current market size.

It is also necessary to consider production and execution capacity. A software company may depend primarily on talent and digital infrastructure. A biotech may need researchers, universities, hospitals, and laboratories. An industrial company depends on energy, suppliers, logistics, and physical infrastructure. A fintech may need banking partners, financial infrastructure, and specialized professionals.

Capital follows a similar logic. A company that can finance its growth through revenue may have little need to be close to investors. A deep-tech company that needs years of research before generating revenue may depend on specialized venture capital, grants, universities, and public programs. In that case, location can directly affect the ability to finance and develop the product.

This combination is what creates hubs. A good hub concentrates customers, talent, capital, universities, suppliers, and knowledge, reducing the cost of finding the resources necessary to grow. But concentration also has a cost: salaries, real estate, and competition for professionals tend to be higher. An excellent ecosystem can therefore be economically worse for a company at a particular stage than a cheaper location.

It is also necessary to consider how reversible a decision can be. Selling remotely to another country is relatively easy to test. Hiring a local team, establishing a regulatory structure, or transferring operations is more expensive. Moving the founders' residence or building a factory can be even harder to reverse. The greater the cost of reversal, the greater the evidence that should be required before making the decision.

In the end, there are two different questions: is there a commercial opportunity, and can the company exploit it? The first depends on demand, price, and competition. The second depends on market access, culture, regulation, talent, capital, infrastructure, and the founders' capabilities. The analysis also needs to consider the company's stage, opportunity cost, and the degree to which the decision is reversible. The location decision needs to account for all these dimensions.

## The market depends on the founders

A market's attractiveness also depends on the founders' competitive advantages. Technical know-how, industry experience, regulatory knowledge, language, sales capabilities, network, and cultural understanding can be worth as much as market size or access to capital. Two teams can analyze the same country and find completely different opportunities.

This advantage is especially relevant when there is knowledge that is difficult to acquire. A founder who knows a particular industry deeply, has relationships with potential customers, or understands a regulated supply chain can enter a market with an advantage that a foreign competitor would take years to build. The same applies to technological knowledge: accumulated experience, intellectual property, access to researchers, or mastery of specific processes can determine where the company can compete more effectively.

This also explains why copying the geography of a successful company is often a weak strategy. Success may depend as much on the market as on the specific capabilities of the people who built it. The question is not only "where is there a good opportunity?" but "where does this team have an advantage in capturing it?"

This logic also makes it possible to separate the locations of different components of the company. Customers, founders, engineering, operations, capital, and corporate headquarters do not need to be in the same country. A company can keep development in Brazil, sell to the United States, hire sales locally, and use another jurisdiction for its corporate structure. The advantage is the ability to choose the location of each function; the cost is adding tax, regulatory, legal, and operational complexity.

Therefore, internationalizing the company does not necessarily mean internationalizing the founders' lives. It is possible to sell to another country without changing residence, just as moving to another country does not guarantee access to that market's customers. These are different decisions, although they may be related.

The same applies to the idea of being "global from day one." For some businesses, especially those whose domestic market is small or whose customers are already concentrated abroad, starting internationally may be necessary. For others, starting in a familiar market reduces CAC, accelerates learning, and allows the product to be validated before adding new geographies.

The decision should consider not only the objective attractiveness of the market, but also the entry cost for that team and the advantages it can take with it. The best market on paper may not be the best market for those founders.

## Taxation and incentives can change the equation

Taxation directly enters a company's economics, but it should not be reduced to the corporate income tax rate. Depending on the business, taxes on consumption, payroll, dividends, capital gains, imports, and international transactions may also matter. A software company selling to several countries faces different issues from an industrial company that imports components, operates a factory, and sells domestically.

It is also necessary to distinguish where the company is incorporated from where the economic activity takes place. A company may have an entity in one country, employees in another, and customers around the world. This does not mean that all taxation will be concentrated in the entity's jurisdiction. Tax residency, permanent establishment, withholding taxes, transfer pricing, and rules for international operations may determine where different parts of the activity are taxed.

Therefore, comparing only the nominal corporate tax rate can lead to incorrect conclusions. What matters is the effective tax burden of the structure the company intends to use and how it combines with salaries, infrastructure, operating costs, and market access. A country with lower taxes may be less advantageous if it requires much higher costs to hire, sell, or operate.

In addition to general taxation, some countries offer specific mechanisms for certain activities. R&D tax credits, intellectual property regimes, accelerated depreciation, and regional incentives can reduce the effective cost of development or expansion. For a conventional software company, the difference may be small. For a biotech, semiconductor, energy, robotics, or other deep-tech company, it can represent a significant share of the capital required to reach the market.

There are also incentives that do not operate directly through the tax system. Grants, subsidies, subsidized financing, innovation programs, support for hiring researchers, incubators, technology parks, and government procurement can reduce costs, finance development stages, or facilitate access to customers. Universities and research centers can also provide researchers, laboratories, and intellectual property. In this case, the benefit of a location is not only how much the company pays the government, but what it can obtain in return and what it would cost to build that infrastructure independently.

Internationalization can also receive specific support. Some governments finance trade missions, participation in trade fairs, market development, and export programs. For a company that uses one country as an operational base and intends to sell into other markets, this type of program can reduce expansion costs without necessarily changing the company's corporate structure.

The counterpoint is that incentives do not create demand. A grant does not turn a product without a market into a viable business, and a tax credit does not indefinitely compensate for high CAC, low willingness to pay, or unsustainable competition. In addition, programs have eligibility criteria, limits, requirements, and deadlines, while tax rules can also change.

Therefore, the analysis should begin with the economics of the business without incentives. Then, it should measure how taxation, credits, financing, and public programs change those economics. A benefit is truly relevant when it reduces costs, accelerates development, lowers risk, or facilitates expansion of a company that already has a commercial opportunity. When the company remains viable only while a particular benefit exists, the incentive stops being a structural advantage and becomes part of the operation's risk.

## Brazil: a large consumer market

Brazil combines consumer scale, sector diversity, and specific problems that create opportunities in categories such as fintech, agritech, healthtech, logistics, education, energy, and infrastructure. There are more than 200 million inhabitants and an economy large enough to allow many companies to build relevant businesses without needing to internationalize immediately. In B2B, the presence of large banks, industrial companies, retailers, energy companies, insurers, and other corporate buyers also creates relevant markets in relatively specific niches.

Some opportunities are particularly Brazilian. Pix is a good example. In May 2026, more than 170 million individuals had already used the system, equivalent to about 80% of the population, and more than 7 billion transactions were recorded during the month. Open Finance also reached significant scale: at the end of 2025, it connected 96 million bank accounts and accumulated more than R$15 billion in payments and transfers during the year.

This creates infrastructure on which companies can build products. Fintechs can explore payments, credit, financial management, and services for companies; other companies can use these infrastructures as components of their own products. The important point is that the opportunity lies not only in the size of the financial market, but in the existence of specific regulatory and technological infrastructure that can reduce the cost of building certain products.

The same logic appears in other sectors. Brazil's tax complexity has created demand for tax and accounting software. Agribusiness supports markets for credit, management, monitoring, and productivity. The healthcare system creates opportunities in management, diagnosis, and operational efficiency. Logistics, energy, and infrastructure have problems of sufficient scale to justify specific solutions. Some of these companies will be essentially Brazilian; others can turn a solution developed for a local problem into an exportable product.

Brazil also has public research and innovation infrastructure that deserves to be included in the analysis. Finep operates credit and economic subsidy instruments; under the latter, companies can receive resources for research, development, and innovation projects without having to repay them, provided they meet the program's conditions and provide financial counterpart funding.

Embrapii adds another layer. Rather than simply financing the company, its network connects companies with research institutions to develop technological projects. Currently, the network has 91 active research units and records more than R$8.79 billion in investments in R&D projects and 4,306 supported projects. Its units include institutions such as CNPEM, ESALQ, CIMATEC, CERTI, CESAR, and centers linked to federal and state universities.

This is especially relevant for companies that need scientific infrastructure. A software startup may not need a university to develop its product. A biotechnology, materials, robotics, energy, or applied artificial intelligence company may need laboratories, researchers, and equipment that would be too expensive to maintain internally.

There are also financing and investment instruments. BNDES operates both through financing and capital markets, while BNDESPAR and Finep continue to use investment funds as instruments to strengthen startups. In 2026, for example, the institutions launched a call for an investment fund for innovative and scalable startups, with special focus on companies that had already gone through development, subsidy, or acceleration programs. A specific investment fund for artificial intelligence-intensive startups was also launched, with a planned minimum capital of R$160 million.

Taxation can also create opportunities and reduce costs for certain sectors. The ICT Law, for example, establishes tax credits associated with investments in research, development, and innovation in the technology sector. For companies that meet the requirements, mechanisms of this kind can change the economics of R&D projects.

The ecosystem, however, is highly regional. São Paulo concentrates a large share of capital, startups, large companies, and specialized professionals, but the ideal location depends on the sector. An agritech may benefit more from being close to producers, cooperatives, and agricultural research centers. An energy company may prefer a region close to a particular industrial chain. A biotech may choose its location based on the existence of a specific laboratory or university. A B2B software company may prioritize proximity to its main buyers. "Being in Brazil" is not a sufficiently precise decision.

There is also private support infrastructure. Cubo Itaú, for example, connects startups, large companies, investors, and partners, while Sebrae provides training, consulting, and support to small businesses. For a B2B startup, these connections may be more important than a generic entrepreneurship program: a first corporate customer can generate revenue, validate the product, and open doors to other buyers.

Brazil can also be used as a base for internationalization. ApexBrasil works specifically to promote exports and the insertion of Brazilian companies into international markets. This enables a strategy different from simply choosing between "Brazil" and "abroad": the company can develop technology and validate the product in Brazil, build an efficient operation, and use the domestic market as its first laboratory before selling to other countries.

This strategy is particularly interesting when Brazil offers a specific development advantage. A company may have access to a large bank as an initial customer, test a payment solution on infrastructure such as Pix, develop technology with an Embrapii unit, and later adapt the product for other markets. In this case, the value of Brazil lies not only in Brazilian consumers, but in the combination of customers, infrastructure, talent, and development capacity.

The counterpoint is that the size of the Brazilian market does not solve unit economics problems. Purchasing power is lower than in markets such as the United States and parts of Europe, and companies may face significant costs related to taxation, compliance, financing, and infrastructure. Capital and talent are also geographically concentrated. In addition, public programs have their own criteria, limits, requirements, and processes; they should not be treated as capital automatically available to any startup.

Brazil can therefore play different roles. It can be the main consumer market, an engineering base, a laboratory for developing specific products, a platform for accessing large companies, or a combination of these roles. For a financial technology company, the market and regulatory infrastructure may be the main differentiator. For a deep-tech company, universities, Embrapii, Finep, and other R&D mechanisms may weigh more heavily. For a conventional B2B company, proximity to large buyers may be the decisive factor.

The question is not simply whether Brazil is a large market. It is what concrete advantage Brazil offers this company: customers, infrastructure, talent, R&D, financing, operating costs, or access to specific sectors, and whether that advantage is sufficient to offset its limitations.

## United States: the world's largest consumer market

The United States combines a population of more than 340 million people, high purchasing power, a huge B2B market, and an unusual concentration of companies capable of purchasing high-value products and services. In software, artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, healthcare, financial services, defense, and industrial technology, there are very large numbers of potential customers and corporate buyers with significant budgets. For a Brazilian company, this can mean not only more customers, but also higher average ticket sizes and segments that simply do not have equivalent scale in Brazil.

The advantage is not limited to the end consumer. The country is home to some of the world's largest companies in technology, finance, healthcare, retail, industry, energy, and defense. For a B2B startup, this means that a relatively specific market can have hundreds or thousands of potential buyers. A cybersecurity company, for example, does not need to sell to millions of consumers to build a relevant business. It can operate in a specific segment of companies with large technology and security budgets.

This depth also enables specialization. In the United States, a startup can build a product for a very specific niche and still find enough customers to support a relevant company. This is particularly important for B2B SaaS, vertical software, and specialized tools. In Brazil, the same niche may have too few buyers to justify an independent company.

The counterpart is the cost of entry. Many categories already have well-capitalized competitors, established brands, and mature distribution channels. Selling to large companies may also require certifications, insurance, information security requirements, privacy compliance, integration, and procurement processes. For a foreign company, local references, English-language support, commercial presence, and partner relationships may become necessary as the ticket size increases.

This process can be particularly long in B2B. A company may need to undergo security, legal, procurement, compliance, and technical integration evaluations before closing its first contract. The potential value of the customer may be much higher, but the sales cycle and capital required to reach revenue can also increase. For a startup, this means market size needs to be analyzed together with CAC, payback, and working capital.

Innovation financing is an important advantage, especially for deep-tech companies. The SBIR program, present at several federal agencies, provides funding for small businesses to develop technologies with potential commercial and government applications. STTR follows a similar logic but requires the participation of a research institution. The America’s Seed Fund, administered by the NSF, primarily uses STTR and SBIR to fund startups in areas such as artificial intelligence, biotechnology, energy, robotics, and materials.

These programs are particularly interesting because they address a specific deep-tech problem: the distance between research and product. A company may need millions of dollars and several years before it has enough revenue to attract private capital at scale. Public resources can finance part of this stage and reduce the amount of private capital required before technological validation.

The government is also a significant buyer. Defense is the clearest example, but there are opportunities in healthcare, energy, space, security, infrastructure, and information technology. The presence of large federal agencies creates a procurement market that has virtually no equivalent in scale in many other countries. For certain companies, selling to the government can be a commercial strategy in itself, rather than merely a complementary source of revenue.

This also creates specific barriers. Government contracts can involve certifications, security requirements, procurement rules, foreign ownership restrictions, and lengthy procurement processes. For a foreign startup, therefore, the size of this market does not mean immediate access to it. In certain sectors, having an American structure and meeting specific requirements can be part of the market-entry strategy.

There is also a federal tax credit for certain research and development activities. Companies that conduct qualifying R&D in the United States can reduce part of the effective cost of those activities. Eligible small businesses can also use the credit against certain payroll taxes. For an R&D-intensive company, this difference can be relevant, but it depends on the activities actually performed in the United States and the applicable tax requirements.

The choice of state also significantly changes the economics of the business. California, Massachusetts, New York, Texas, Washington, and other hubs have different combinations of capital, universities, talent, taxes, salaries, real estate costs, and incentives. A biotechnology company may value Boston or the San Francisco Bay Area because of their concentration of researchers, universities, and investors. A technology company may prioritize another state based on the combination of talent and cost. An industrial company may consider energy, logistics, suppliers, and installation incentives more important than proximity to venture capital.

This decentralization is important because "the United States" does not represent a single cost structure. A company can have customers throughout the country and choose its operation based on completely different factors. For a physical operation, comparing states can be as important as comparing the United States with other countries.

Universities and research centers add another advantage. Institutions such as MIT, Stanford, Harvard, UC Berkeley, Carnegie Mellon, and dozens of other centers concentrate researchers, laboratories, patents, and technology-transfer structures. For a biotechnology, hardware, or deep-tech company, being close to the appropriate scientific infrastructure can be worth more than simply being close to investors. For conventional software companies, this variable tends to carry much less weight.

Private capital is another differentiator. The United States has a financing chain that includes angel investors, accelerators, venture capital, growth equity, private equity, and public markets. A company can raise capital at different stages as it reduces technological and commercial risk. There are also many sector-specialized investors, from biotech and fintech to cybersecurity, defense, and climate tech.

But the abundance of capital has a counterpart. Competition for that capital is also much greater. A Brazilian startup that would be considered exceptional in a smaller ecosystem may compete in the United States against hundreds of companies with experienced founders, commercial references, researchers from top universities, and specialized investors. The existence of capital does not mean that it is available to every company.

For foreign companies, it is also important to separate corporate structure from founder residence. It is possible to incorporate and own a U.S. company without living in the United States. A startup intending to raise U.S. venture capital can, for example, use a Delaware C Corporation while keeping part of its engineering and other functions in another country. The corporate structure can facilitate relationships with American investors and customers, but it does not automatically grant the founder authorization to live or work physically in the country.

There are specific immigration mechanisms, such as the International Entrepreneur Rule and visa categories such as O-1, as well as structures for investors, specialized employees, and executive transfers. These mechanisms have their own requirements and should be treated as a matter separate from market selection. For the entrepreneur, the first question is whether there is an economic reason to be physically in the United States; then, which immigration structure permits that.

This separation allows different strategies. A Brazilian company can keep engineering and operations in Brazil while selling to American customers. It can open an entity in the United States when contracts, investors, or strategic partners justify it. It can transfer part of the team later or structure the American operation from the beginning when customers, capital, and execution needs make that configuration more efficient.

Another differentiator is the depth of the domestic market. A company can grow for many years within the United States without depending on internationalization to reach significant scale. This is especially important for B2B companies: capturing a small share of a specific segment can represent tens or hundreds of millions of dollars in potential revenue. For B2C companies, the number of consumers and purchasing power allow different segments and pricing models to be tested before entering other countries.

The counterpoint is precisely this depth. An American customer may pay several times more than a Brazilian one and still generate a less profitable operation if CAC, salaries, support, insurance, certifications, and competition are proportionally higher. A company may also discover that the main benefit of being in the United States is not the consumer, but access to capital, researchers, strategic partners, buyers, or technological infrastructure.

There is also an important difference between selling to the United States and operating in the United States. A company can export software from Brazil and test demand before assuming the costs of hiring, corporate structure, taxation, and American compliance. If the results justify it, it can add a sales team, a local entity, or a physical operation. This allows the market hypothesis to be tested before taking more difficult-to-reverse decisions.

In practice, the United States offers a combination that is difficult to find in a single market: large-scale demand, high purchasing power, corporate buyers, private capital, public innovation funding, universities, government procurement, technological infrastructure, and a developed chain for financing and scaling companies. The advantage, however, only appears when the company can capture these structures.

For a startup without product-market fit, the same environment may simply mean higher costs and greater competition. For a company with a validated product, high ticket size, and the ability to sell to American companies, the market's scale may justify these costs. For a deep-tech company, the main attraction may lie in universities, capital, and public programs. For a B2C company, it may lie in consumer size and purchasing power.

Therefore, the relevant question is not simply whether the United States is a larger market than Brazil. It is what concrete advantage the United States provides that improves the economics and execution capacity of the company being built.

## Canada: a developed market and bridge to the United States

Canada combines a high-income market, stable institutions, strong universities, skilled immigration, and economic proximity to the United States. The domestic market is much smaller than the American one, but integration between the two countries allows a Canadian company to develop products in a relatively sophisticated environment and access the largest consumer market in North America.

This proximity is particularly relevant for B2B companies. A startup can develop technology and build its first customers in Canada while using the United States as its expansion market. The USMCA, which also includes Mexico, reduces some trade barriers and integrates the three countries across several supply chains. For industrial, automotive, energy, technology, and logistics companies, this integration can be more important than the size of the Canadian market alone.

Canada also has strong research infrastructure. Toronto, Montreal, Vancouver, Waterloo, and other regions concentrate universities, researchers, and technology companies. Artificial intelligence is one of the best-known examples, especially around Toronto and Montreal, but the country also has relevant capabilities in biotechnology, healthcare, energy, mining, materials, and environmental technologies.

This infrastructure is accompanied by public innovation-support instruments. The federal government uses programs such as SR&ED to provide tax incentives related to research and development, while organizations such as the National Research Council of Canada and its innovation programs support companies at different stages. Provincial governments also have their own incentives, creating important differences between locations.

The choice of province can therefore be as relevant as the choice of country. Ontario concentrates a large share of the corporate and technology market and has proximity to the United States. Quebec has strong research infrastructure and a relevant artificial intelligence ecosystem, as well as its own cultural and linguistic characteristics. British Columbia combines technology, natural resources, and proximity to the Pacific coast. Alberta has an economy strongly linked to energy, natural resources, and technologies related to these sectors.

Immigration is another relevant component. Canada has programs aimed at skilled professionals, entrepreneurs, and innovative companies, and has historically used immigration as part of its workforce-formation strategy. For a foreign founder, this can make the country interesting not only as a market, but also as a place to build a team and establish residence.

The counterpoint is scale. The Canadian market has just over 40 million inhabitants, a fraction of the American market. For a B2C company dependent on population scale, this can limit domestic potential. For B2B, specialized software, or high-ticket products, the limitation may be much less relevant.

There are also significant costs. Skilled labor, real estate, and operations can be expensive in Toronto and Vancouver, while taxation and labor rules vary by province. A company that chooses Canada solely for its incentives may discover that other costs offset part of the benefit.

Canada therefore occupies an interesting intermediate position. It does not have the scale of the United States, but it offers a developed market and close access to the American economy. It does not necessarily have Silicon Valley's concentration of capital, but it has universities, researchers, public programs, and relevant technology ecosystems. For a deep-tech, artificial intelligence, biotechnology, energy, mining, or B2B software company, this combination can be particularly relevant.

In practice, Canada can play different roles. It can be the initial market, the research and development base, the location for hiring skilled professionals, or a platform for later entering the United States. As with other regions analyzed, the question is not only whether Canada is a large market, but what concrete advantage its location provides for the company.

## Europe: several markets within one region

Europe may be the region where location choice requires the most care. The European Union brings together a market of hundreds of millions of consumers and facilitates the movement of goods, services, capital, and people among member states, but each country retains important differences in taxation, labor law, immigration, costs, incentives, and innovation ecosystems. In addition, some of the markets most interesting to a European company, such as the United Kingdom, Switzerland, and Norway, are outside the European Union.

There is also a European layer of funding. Horizon Europe is the European Union's main research and innovation program, while the European Innovation Council, or EIC, has instruments aimed especially at higher-risk technologies. EIC Pathfinder supports advanced research, EIC Transition helps transform scientific results into commercial applications, and EIC Accelerator combines grants and investment for startups and small companies in stages close to commercialization. For a deep-tech company, this can significantly change the financing structure required to turn research into a product. From there, each country offers a different combination of advantages.

Spain is currently one of the options that deserves attention for startups seeking to combine a consumer market, a growing technology ecosystem, and a relatively more competitive cost structure than some of Europe's main hubs. Madrid and Barcelona concentrate startups, venture capital, large companies, and technology professionals, while the country has a significant population and access to the European market. Cultural and linguistic proximity to Latin America can also be an advantage for companies intending to operate on both sides of the Atlantic.

The Ley de Startups created specific benefits for emerging companies and mechanisms aimed at attracting entrepreneurs and qualified professionals. ENISA also has financing instruments and participates in certifying companies eligible for the benefits of the legislation. For a Brazilian company, Spain can function both as a market and as an entry point to the rest of the European Union. The counterpoint is that competition and costs in Madrid and Barcelona already reflect the greater maturity of these ecosystems.

Germany represents a different thesis. It is one of Europe's main industrial centers and has an exceptional combination of industry, engineering, universities, and applied research. Automation, robotics, manufacturing, energy, mobility, materials, industrial equipment, and other deep-tech areas find there a concentration of customers and partners that is difficult to reproduce elsewhere.

The EXIST program is a concrete example of this structure. It supports students, recent graduates, and researchers who intend to turn scientific knowledge into companies and has specific instruments for technology-transfer projects. For a scientific startup, Germany's value may lie less in the end consumer and more in proximity to researchers, laboratories, suppliers, large industries, and potential buyers.

The United Kingdom needs to be analyzed separately because it is outside the European Union. This creates an additional layer of complexity for companies intending to operate simultaneously in the UK and EU, but it does not diminish the country's importance as a business center. London remains particularly relevant in venture capital, fintech, financial services, technology, and international business. Oxford and Cambridge add world-class universities and research, while other regions have their own industrial specializations.

The United Kingdom also has specific R&D and innovation mechanisms, as well as immigration instruments such as the Innovator Founder visa. For a startup, the main attraction may be the combination of capital, universities, corporate customers, financial services, and an English-speaking business environment. In some situations, a company may prefer London to continental Europe even while accepting the additional complexity of operating outside the EU.

France has a strong industrial and innovation policy and can be particularly interesting for energy, healthcare, aerospace, defense, industry, and advanced technologies. The country has large companies, research centers, and public instruments aimed at R&D and strategic sectors. For a technology-intensive company, the state's presence as a funder, buyer, and industrial-policy driver in certain sectors can be an important advantage.

The counterpoint lies in labor costs, regulatory and tax complexity, and the need to understand the French commercial environment. For a company that depends on local relationships, language can also become more than a translation issue and become part of the commercial strategy.

The Netherlands presents a thesis more closely linked to infrastructure and location. The country has a privileged geographic position, major port and logistics infrastructure, and strong trade integration with other European markets. Amsterdam also has a relevant technology ecosystem, while other regions have industrial and scientific specializations.

For logistics, international trade, supply chain, industrial technology, and European operations, the Netherlands can function more as a hub than as a consumer market. A company can establish part of its operation in the country and use that location to serve customers and suppliers across Europe.

Ireland has a similar logic. The domestic market is small, but the country has built a strong concentration of technology, pharmaceutical, and international-services multinationals. Enterprise Ireland also has instruments to support high-potential companies, including grants and investments in startups.

For a Brazilian company, Ireland can be particularly interesting when English, internationalization, proximity to major technology companies, and access to the European ecosystem are more important than domestic market size. The location can function as the base of an international operation rather than depending exclusively on Irish consumers.

Portugal has a different thesis and is particularly relevant for Brazilians. Language, cultural proximity, and residency mechanisms reduce some entry barriers for founders who also want to establish their personal lives in the country. The country has startup-support programs and initiatives to attract entrepreneurs and qualified professionals.

The problem is scale. Portugal has a small domestic market compared with Spain, Germany, France, or the United Kingdom. Therefore, a B2B or SaaS company can use Portugal as an operational base while directing sales to other European markets. In that case, the main benefit is not the number of Portuguese consumers, but the combination of cost, talent, language, European structure, and ease of entry for certain founders.

The Nordic countries form another important group. Sweden, Denmark, Finland, and Norway have relatively small populations, but combine high income, digitalization, education, infrastructure, institutional stability, and technologically sophisticated companies.

Sweden has one of Europe's best-known startup scenes, with companies such as Spotify and Klarna as examples of businesses that achieved international scale. The country can be particularly interesting for software, fintech, telecommunications, industrial technology, and climate tech.

Denmark has advantages in energy, biotechnology, healthcare, logistics, and sustainability. Finland combines engineering, research, and technology, with a strong history in telecommunications and gaming. Norway follows its own logic, with substantial capital availability and an economy strongly linked to energy, maritime industry, aquaculture, and natural resources, as well as a growing energy-transition agenda.

The Nordic countries once again show that a small market can be economically interesting. A high-ticket B2B company can find sophisticated buyers with significant purchasing power even in countries with only a few million inhabitants. For a B2C company dependent on population scale, the same characteristic can be a limitation.

Poland represents a different alternative within Europe. The country has a large population, a relevant industrial base, deep integration with the European Union, and a significant community of technology and engineering professionals. Warsaw, Kraków, Wrocław, and other cities concentrate operations of international companies.

The combination of technical talent, relatively competitive costs, and access to the European market makes Poland interesting both as a consumer market and as a base for engineering, services, and operations. For companies that need to hire large teams without assuming the costs of some of Western Europe's main hubs, it can be a relevant alternative.

Other Central and Eastern European countries follow a similar logic. The Czech Republic has an important industrial and technology base and a central location in Europe. Romania has a significant community of technology and engineering professionals and can be competitive in operating costs. Estonia has built an international reputation in digital government, digital public services, and technology entrepreneurship. Lithuania and other Baltic countries have also developed relevant ecosystems in fintech and digital services.

Switzerland deserves to be included despite being outside the European Union. The country combines high income, institutional stability, world-class universities and research centers, and strong concentrations in sectors such as pharmaceuticals, biotechnology, finance, insurance, commodities, and precision technology. For certain companies, especially deep-tech, biotech, and financial-services companies, these characteristics can offset high operating costs.

Austria presents another combination. Its central location facilitates access to Central and Eastern Europe, while Vienna has strong services, universities, and international-organization infrastructure. The country can be interesting for companies linked to industry, energy, environmental technology, and business services.

Luxembourg is an even more specific case. Its consumer market is small, but the country has a relevant position in financial services, investment funds, insurance, and international corporate structures. For a company that depends on these sectors, the country's importance may lie much more in its financial infrastructure than in domestic demand.

Taxation is also important to observe. The existence of an integrated European market does not mean that a company will pay the same taxes in every country. Corporate tax, dividend taxation, payroll costs, VAT, R&D incentives, stock-option treatment, and intellectual-property rules can significantly change the economics of a startup.

This difference becomes even more important when the company distributes its functions. It is possible to have researchers in one country, engineering in another, customers across Europe, a corporate structure in a fifth jurisdiction, and investors from several countries. The tax decision in such a case should not be reduced to finding the lowest rate. It is necessary to consider where the company actually operates, where employees are located, where intellectual property is developed, where customers are located, and how profits will be distributed.

The European catalog therefore does not present a single winner. Spain can be interesting for its combination of market and ecosystem; Germany for industry and research; the United Kingdom for capital, technology, and financial services; France for industrial policy and R&D; the Netherlands for logistics and trade; Ireland for multinationals and internationalization; Portugal for proximity to Brazil; Nordic countries for income, innovation, and specialization; Poland and Central and Eastern Europe for talent, costs, and industry; Switzerland for pharma, biotech, and finance; and Luxembourg for financial services and international structures.

Europe's main advantage is precisely this diversity. A company does not necessarily need to choose a single country for all functions. It can develop technology in one jurisdiction, sell in another, hire in a third, and use different markets according to its growth strategy. The difficulty is that this flexibility comes with tax, regulatory, commercial, and operational complexity.

Therefore, when looking at Europe, it is less useful to search for "the best country" and more useful to identify which country offers the missing piece for the business model: market, capital, talent, research, industry, logistics, cost, taxation, infrastructure, or access to other markets.

## Latin America: the same language does not mean homogeneity

The main advantage of Latin America for a Brazilian company is the ability to reuse part of its Spanish-language operation across several countries. This reduces translation, content, support, and marketing costs. For SaaS, education, fintech, marketplaces, and digital services, this characteristic can make regional expansion relatively efficient.

But the region should not be treated as a single market. Mexico, Colombia, Chile, Argentina, Peru, and Uruguay have very different combinations of population, income, macroeconomic stability, infrastructure, regulation, capital, and talent availability. The fact that two countries share a language does not mean they have the same customers, distribution channels, or unit economics.

Mexico is the main Latin American market outside Brazil and probably the country with the greatest potential for scale in the region. Its population, economy, and strong integration with the United States create opportunities in consumption, software, industry, logistics, foreign trade, and B2B services. USMCA connects the country to North American supply chains, making Mexico particularly relevant for manufacturing, automation, supply chain, and industrial suppliers.

This position also creates an opportunity that goes beyond the domestic market. A startup can use Mexico to serve companies participating in North American production chains, including by providing technology to companies that export to the United States. For Brazilian companies, this can make the country simultaneously a consumer market, industrial platform, and possible bridge to North America.

Colombia presents a different profile. With more than 50 million inhabitants, a diversified economy, and business concentration in Bogotá, the country has enough scale to support dedicated operations in several categories. Medellín adds a relevant technology ecosystem, particularly for startups and digital services.

Colombia's potential lies in its balance. The market is large enough to justify commercial investment, but it can still be an intermediate step for companies seeking to build a regional presence. Fintech, enterprise software, financial services, education, and digital commerce are some of the areas where a Brazilian company can find space. For a Latin American strategy, Colombia can function both as a market and as a second stage of internationalization.

Argentina has a particularly valuable characteristic for technology companies: talent. The country has a long tradition in engineering, science, and software development, as well as professionals accustomed to working for international companies.

This enables a different strategy. Argentina may be less attractive as a consumer market because of macroeconomic instability, inflation, and a history of foreign-exchange restrictions, but it can be quite attractive as an engineering, product, or technical-operations center. A Brazilian company can, for example, maintain customers and commercial structure in more predictable markets while using Argentina to expand its technological capacity.

Chile presents yet another profile. Its domestic market is small compared with Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, or Colombia, but the country has built a reputation as a relatively open and internationalized environment for startups. Start-Up Chile, a CORFO program, has become one of the region's best-known initiatives for attracting and developing technology companies.

Chile's potential lies less in consumption scale and more in its ability to function as a platform. A startup can use the country to establish an international operation, access investors, build regional connections, and later sell into larger markets. For a B2C company that depends on millions of local consumers, the country's size is a limitation. For an international startup, operating in a smaller market that is relatively connected to the outside world can be an advantage.

Peru has potential mainly in specific sectors. Its economy has a strong presence in mining, agribusiness, infrastructure, energy, retail, and services. This creates opportunities for B2B companies that provide technology to industrial operations and supply chains.

For a mining technology startup, for example, Peru may be more interesting than countries with much larger consumer markets. The same applies to logistics, asset management, monitoring, productivity, and infrastructure solutions. The country also has startup and innovation support programs, such as Startup Perú, linked to ProInnóvate.

Uruguay has almost the opposite logic. The domestic market is too small to be the main argument for most startups, but the country can be interesting as an operational and technology base. Institutional predictability, digital infrastructure, and the innovation ecosystem can partially offset the lack of scale.

ANII maintains innovation-support instruments, while internationalization initiatives seek to position the country as a platform for companies intending to operate in the region. For a startup that needs a relatively small structure and intends to serve other Latin American markets, Uruguay may have more value as a hub than as a consumer market.

Beyond these markets, there are smaller and more specific opportunities. Ecuador can be relevant for companies linked to trade, energy, agribusiness, and infrastructure. Costa Rica has a small economy but an interesting position in services, technology, and international operations. Panama can be relevant for logistics, financial services, and regional trade. Paraguay has specific advantages in energy and certain industrial operations, in addition to its proximity to the Brazilian market. Bolivia has opportunities associated with natural resources, infrastructure, and certain consumer sectors, although with an operating environment different from the region's main markets.

These smaller countries do not necessarily need to receive the same level of detail as the larger markets. The important point is to show the reader that Latin America does not end with Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Colombia, and Chile. There are smaller markets that can be highly relevant when there is a specific product, sector, or location thesis.

There is also a sector dimension. An enterprise software company should not necessarily choose the same country as an agritech. A fintech needs to analyze financial infrastructure and regulation. An industrial company should look at suppliers, logistics, and production chains. A mining technology company may find more value in Peru or Chile than in much larger markets. A consumer company will place much greater weight on population, income, distribution, and payment methods.

The advantage of Spanish, therefore, is real but limited. It is possible to reuse product, technology, brand, content, and part of the commercial operation across several countries. What cannot be automatically reused is the market. Currency, taxation, labor law, data protection, payment methods, sector regulation, sales channels, and buyer behavior remain national.

In practice, countries can fulfill different functions within the same strategy. Mexico can offer scale and access to North American supply chains. Colombia can be a large regional market. Argentina can provide talent. Chile can function as an international platform. Peru can be particularly interesting for mining and infrastructure. Uruguay can serve as an operational base.

This is probably the most useful way to look at Latin America. Not as a list of countries a startup needs to conquer, but as a set of markets and structures that can perform different functions within the same company.

## Asia: when the hub and the market are different things

Asia requires separating three decisions: where to sell, where to operate, and where to establish the regional structure. In some countries these three things coincide; in others, they do not. The region includes some of the world's largest consumer markets, financial and technology centers, major industrial supply chains, and a huge supply of professionals. At the same time, it presents enormous differences in income, language, regulation, business culture, and infrastructure.

Singapore is the best example of a hub. With a small domestic market, the country offers logistics and financial infrastructure, institutional stability, a strong presence of multinationals, and commercial connections throughout Southeast Asia. For a B2B company, it may make more sense to maintain a regional structure in Singapore and sell to Indonesia, Malaysia, Vietnam, and other markets than to depend on the Singaporean market. Enterprise Singapore offers internationalization, innovation, and co-funding programs, while the Economic Development Board works to attract investment and international operations.

This makes Singapore interesting even when customers are in other countries. A company can use the country as a financial, commercial, or administrative center while keeping development, manufacturing, and sales elsewhere in Asia. Operating costs, however, are high, especially for real estate and skilled professionals. The benefit needs to be related to the access the hub provides.

India is almost the opposite case. There are more than 1.4 billion inhabitants, a developed technology industry, and a huge supply of engineering professionals. The potential market is enormous, but average income is lower than in markets such as Japan, South Korea, or Singapore. In practice, a company needs to adapt pricing, product, and distribution. India can simultaneously be a consumer market, engineering center, support center, and operations base.

The country also has a relevant innovation ecosystem, with initiatives such as Startup India and policies focused on artificial intelligence, semiconductors, and electronic manufacturing. This creates a dual opportunity. A startup can sell to the Indian market or use the country primarily for its talent availability and technological-industry scale. For some companies, the second function may be more important than the first.

For software and digital services, this distinction is especially important. A product costing US$100 per user per month may have a limited market among Indian consumers but can be perfectly viable for Indian companies or multinationals operating in the country. Low-cost B2C products, on the other hand, can benefit from population scale. Therefore, 1.4 billion inhabitants does not automatically mean a TAM of 1.4 billion customers.

Japan and South Korea present another opportunity. They are high-income, technologically sophisticated markets with large corporate buyers. Japan has one of the world's largest economies and major industrial groups in automobiles, electronics, machinery, energy, and financial services. South Korea is one of the world's main hubs for semiconductors, electronics, automobiles, batteries, and telecommunications.

For a B2B company, selling to a large Japanese or Korean industrial company can be worth more than acquiring thousands of consumers in lower-income markets. Industrial software, cybersecurity, artificial intelligence, automation, robotics, advanced materials, and manufacturing technologies are examples of categories that can find high-value demand.

The problem is entry. Language, business relationships, procurement processes, certifications, and trust can significantly increase the sales cycle. In certain sectors, reputation and the existence of a local partner may weigh as much as the product's technical quality. A company entering these markets without understanding how purchasing is conducted may face a much greater commercial barrier than simply translating the software.

Japan and South Korea also have industrial policies and public programs supporting innovation. For deep-tech companies, therefore, the analysis should include not only the consumer market, but proximity to manufacturers, universities, laboratories, and industrial supply chains.

China needs to be treated almost as a strategy of its own. The consumer market is enormous and the country has highly competitive supply chains in manufacturing, e-commerce, payments, electric vehicles, and technology. For an industrial company, being close to the Chinese supplier ecosystem can be an advantage that is difficult to reproduce elsewhere.

On the other hand, foreign companies face specific rules concerning data, security, intellectual property, foreign investment, and digital-platform operations. Consumers and companies use local ecosystems that do not necessarily correspond to channels prevalent in the West. For a Brazilian startup, entering China can require much more than localizing the product.

China also has strongly targeted industrial policies in strategic sectors such as semiconductors, electric vehicles, energy, artificial intelligence, and advanced manufacturing. This can create opportunities for companies embedded in these supply chains, but it also means competing with local companies that have access to suppliers, capital, and industrial-development policies.

Taiwan deserves a specific mention for a very different reason: semiconductors. The island occupies a central position in the global chip-manufacturing chain and has a highly specialized ecosystem of manufacturers, suppliers, engineers, and technology companies. For hardware, chips, electronic components, and computing-related technologies, proximity to this ecosystem can be much more valuable than the size of the local consumer market.

Hong Kong presents another function. Its domestic market is small, but its financial and commercial importance can make it relevant for certain companies that need access to capital, financial services, and international business in Asia. The usefulness of Hong Kong, like Singapore's, may lie less in the local consumer and more in the infrastructure connecting the company to other markets.

Southeast Asia offers a different combination of scale and growth. Indonesia has more than 280 million inhabitants and is the region's largest market by population. Vietnam has around 100 million and has gained importance in Asian industrial supply chains, particularly in electronics and manufacturing. The Philippines, Thailand, and Malaysia also have relevant markets but with different economic structures.

Indonesia is particularly interesting for B2C businesses and digital platforms because of its population scale. The challenge is the geographic dispersion of an archipelago with thousands of islands, which increases the importance of logistics, payments, and distribution. For certain products, conquering the Indonesian market may require a very different operating infrastructure from selling in Singapore.

Vietnam presents a different logic. The country has been attracting international manufacturing and has become an important part of Asian supply chains. For a hardware, components, industrial automation, or manufacturer-services company, proximity to factories and suppliers may matter more than the size of the Vietnamese consumer market.

Malaysia and Thailand occupy intermediate positions, with relevant consumer markets, developed infrastructure, and important industrial chains. Malaysia has a significant presence in electronics and semiconductors, while Thailand has a relevant automotive and manufacturing industry. The Philippines has a large population and a strong presence of services, BPO, and English-language operations.

There are also markets that may be relevant precisely because of their specialization. Indonesia for consumer scale. Vietnam for manufacturing. Malaysia for electronics and semiconductors. Thailand for automotive industry and tourism. The Philippines for services and English-language operations. It is not necessary to turn every country into a complete analysis to show the reader that there are different entry theses in the region.

ASEAN adds a regional layer. The association creates mechanisms for economic integration among its members and can facilitate certain expansion strategies, although it does not turn the countries into a single regulatory market. For a Brazilian company, this means that a regional operation can reuse part of its structure, but will still need to deal with national differences.

Capital availability also varies significantly. Singapore has an international financial ecosystem; Japan and South Korea have large corporate groups and financial institutions; China has capital markets and investors linked to strategic sectors; India has a developed venture capital ecosystem; and different Southeast Asian countries have programs to attract startups and foreign investment. For a startup, therefore, it is not enough to ask where investors exist. It is necessary to verify whether specialized capital exists for the type of company being built.

The same applies to talent. India can be competitive for engineering and operations. Singapore concentrates professionals specialized in finance, technology, and international business, although at high cost. Japan and Korea have sophisticated industrial and technological knowledge. China, Taiwan, and Vietnam have highly developed industrial ecosystems and supplier chains.

These functions can be combined. A company can have its regional headquarters in Singapore, engineering in India, suppliers in Vietnam, manufacturing in China or Taiwan, and customers spread across Indonesia, Japan, and South Korea. In this scenario, asking "what is the best country in Asia?" is not very useful. The question is which country offers the best combination for each function and what it costs to coordinate these operations.

We should not confuse population with addressable market, nor hub with consumer market. In Asia, it is also important to separate where customers are from where it is most efficient to hire talent, develop technology, manufacture, raise capital, or establish a regional structure. The choice should consider demand and purchasing power, talent, infrastructure, regulation, capital, logistics, incentives, and ease of entry.

In such a heterogeneous region, the ideal location may involve two or more countries. The market may be in India or Indonesia, engineering in India, production in Vietnam or China, semiconductors in Taiwan, capital in Singapore, and industrial customers in Japan or South Korea. Asia's advantage lies precisely in offering different pieces for the same business strategy.

# Africa and Oceania: specific markets and regional opportunities

Africa and Oceania present very different situations, but they have something in common: it rarely makes sense to treat them as a single market. In Africa, the main issue is heterogeneity between countries, income levels, infrastructure, and stability. In Oceania, the challenge is almost the opposite: relatively small markets, but high purchasing power and developed institutions. In both cases, country selection may depend more on the sector and the function the country performs in the strategy than on population size alone.

Africa has more than 1.5 billion inhabitants and one of the world's highest prospects for population growth. At the same time, income, infrastructure, banking, connectivity, and economic stability vary significantly between countries. This creates markets with different problems and, consequently, different opportunities. In some categories, the lower availability of infrastructure can even create space for solutions that would not need to exist in the same way in developed markets.

Nigeria is the main example of scale. With more than 200 million inhabitants, it has one of the continent's largest consumer markets and a relevant ecosystem in technology, fintech, and digital services. Lagos concentrates startups, investors, and large companies. The country can be particularly interesting for digital products capable of operating at prices compatible with local income and solving problems in payments, credit, commerce, and services. The counterpoint lies in currency volatility, infrastructure, and operational complexity.

South Africa has a different structure. The consumer market is smaller than Nigeria's, but the country has a developed financial system, large companies, relatively sophisticated infrastructure, and a diversified economy. Johannesburg and Cape Town concentrate companies, investors, and qualified professionals. For a B2B, fintech, enterprise software, or specialized-services company, it can be a more predictable entry point into the continent than simply choosing the largest population market.

Kenya has another characteristic. The development of M-Pesa transformed mobile payments into central infrastructure for the economy and helped create a relevant ecosystem in financial technology. Nairobi has also become an important center for startups and digital services on the continent. For fintech, payments, agritech, and financial-services technology, the country can simultaneously function as a market and experimentation environment.

Egypt and Morocco have a different advantage: location. Egypt connects Africa, the Middle East, and Europe and has a large domestic market. Morocco has a smaller economy but relevant integration with Europe and industrial supply chains in sectors such as automotive, aerospace, and manufacturing. For certain companies, logistics proximity and trade integration may matter more than consumer market size.

Other countries may also be relevant for specific reasons. Ghana has a growing technology and financial-services ecosystem and can serve as a relatively accessible market for certain West African operations. Rwanda is an example of a small country seeking to position itself as a business-, technology-, and services-friendly environment. Tanzania has population scale and relevance in agriculture, mining, energy, and infrastructure. None of them needs to be treated as a universal major market; their relevance depends on the company's specific thesis.

Infrastructure remains a central variable. Energy, connectivity, logistics, payments, and distribution can simultaneously be barriers and opportunities. A company capable of reducing the cost of access to financial services, improving distribution, or solving energy problems can find demand that would not exist in the same way in a developed market. The problem is that operating in these environments can also require much greater execution capacity.

Africa also has a continental dimension. The African Continental Free Trade Area, or AfCFTA, seeks to expand trade among African countries and reduce trade barriers. The existence of an integration structure can facilitate regional strategies, but it does not turn the countries into a single jurisdiction. Currencies, taxes, regulation, infrastructure, and purchasing behavior remain different.

For a Brazilian company, this creates several possibilities. A fintech can study markets where financial infrastructure is still developing. An agritech can look for countries with large agricultural sectors and problems similar to those found in Brazil. An energy company can find opportunities in distributed generation and infrastructure. A software company can explore sectors where digitization remains low. In many cases, however, entry will depend on local partners and a concrete understanding of the available infrastructure.

Oceania presents almost the opposite logic. Australia and New Zealand have small populations compared with the United States, Europe, or Asia, but high income, stable institutions, good infrastructure, and developed business markets. The limited size restricts some B2C opportunities, but does not necessarily prevent high-ticket B2B businesses.

Australia is the region's main market and has importance in mining, energy, agriculture, healthcare, education, financial services, and technology. A company developing software for mining, industrial automation, asset management, security, energy, or agritech can find customers with significant purchasing power. The presence of large companies and capital-intensive sectors also creates opportunities for solutions that increase productivity or reduce operating costs.

Australia can also function as a gateway to the Asia-Pacific region. A company can use the country as a developed market to validate a product and later expand into Asian markets. This does not mean Australia is necessarily the best regional base, since Singapore and other hubs may have greater advantages in logistics and Southeast Asian connectivity. The choice depends on the function the operation needs to perform.

New Zealand is an even smaller market, but has interesting characteristics in agritech, food, tourism, technology, and innovation. The highly developed agricultural sector creates demand for technologies related to productivity, monitoring, automation, and sustainability. For a startup, however, the size of the domestic market means internationalization capacity needs to be considered from the outset.

There is also an important difference in terms of distance. Australia and New Zealand are physically far from the main European and American markets, but close to the Asian axis. For companies dependent on hardware, logistics, or physical presence, freight and transit time can significantly change the economics of the operation. For software, this problem is much smaller.

In the case of Africa, therefore, the main caution is not to interpret population as accessible market. A country may have hundreds of millions of inhabitants and still have low purchasing power, limited infrastructure, or high distribution costs. In Oceania, the caution is the reverse: small markets can be economically interesting when ticket size, margins, and purchasing power are high.

In practice, Africa and Oceania enter the analysis when there is a specific reason to consider them. Africa can be particularly relevant for fintech, payments, energy, agritech, infrastructure, logistics, and digital services aimed at markets in transition. Australia can be particularly interesting for B2B, mining, energy, agritech, healthcare, and industrial technology, while New Zealand tends to make more sense in specific niches and as a validation market before a larger expansion.

We should not confuse population size with commercial opportunity. In Africa, a large population can hide barriers related to income, infrastructure, and distribution. In Oceania, a small population can hide high-value customers and highly capitalized sectors. In both cases, the question remains where there is a sufficiently favorable combination of demand, purchasing power, competition, entry cost, infrastructure, and execution capacity.

# So, how do you choose?

So far, we have analyzed TAM, CAC, purchasing power, competition, regulation, talent, capital, infrastructure, taxation, and incentives. In theory, these variables allow countries to be compared. In practice, they only acquire meaning when applied to a specific company, at a particular stage, with particular capabilities.

A larger and wealthier market may be worse if CAC is too high, competition is stronger, or the team does not have access to customers. Likewise, a country with higher taxes may be better if it offers talent, infrastructure, capital, and demand that compensate for the difference.

The stage also changes the answer. A startup may start in Brazil because it can validate the product more cheaply and then open an operation in the United States when capital and customers become the main bottleneck. A biotech may follow the opposite of the traditional logic and choose a country first because of its university, laboratory, researchers, and available funding, leaving the consumer market as a secondary consideration.

Therefore, the comparison should start with the company, not with a ranking of countries. First, we identify the business model, customers, main costs, and growth bottlenecks. Then we look for the geographies that best solve those bottlenecks.

And the solution will not necessarily be to choose a single country. Engineering in Brazil, customers in the United States, an American corporate structure, and a European or Asian operation may make sense depending on the business. Theory shows which variables matter; practice determines which of them actually constrain the company at that moment.
