Whenever I start a new game, one of the first systems I want to understand is its magic and alchemy. Magic represents established knowledge: formulas, spells, techniques, and recipes that can be learned and reproduced. Alchemy represents exploration: combining ingredients, testing hypotheses, observing results, and discovering relationships that were not previously understood. The distinction is not absolute, because both figures study, experiment, learn, accumulate knowledge, and turn discoveries into reusable systems. The metaphor therefore describes two complementary modes of working with knowledge: mastering what is already known and investigating what is not.

The wizard represents the ability to apply systematized knowledge effectively. The grimoire contains what has already been studied, documented, and made reproducible, allowing known problems to be solved without unnecessary reinvention. The alchemist represents practical exploration: observing properties, combining elements, testing hypotheses, and recording the results of experiments. Both can operate in both modes—a wizard can investigate new spells, while an alchemist can reproduce established recipes—but the wizard emphasizes mastery and application, whereas the alchemist emphasizes experimentation and discovery. For an IT professional, both capabilities are necessary.

IT is particularly suited to this metaphor because the technological landscape contains an enormous and constantly changing number of ingredients: programming languages, frameworks, libraries, databases, operating systems, APIs, clouds, observability platforms, CI/CD tools, queues, brokers, AI models, and countless possible combinations. No individual can maintain complete mastery of all of them, so expertise cannot simply mean accumulating more answers. Professionals must also know how to find knowledge they do not possess, evaluate its reliability, recognize when an established solution does not fit the context, and identify when uncertainty requires investigation. Documentation, technical literature, patterns, and professional experience form the grimoire, while recorded experiences and discovered recipes form the alchemy book; when neither provides an adequate answer, the professional must go to the workbench.

The workbench represents controlled experimentation. Experimentation is not random trial and error: it begins with a question or hypothesis, controls relevant conditions, observes the outcome, and extracts knowledge from both positive and negative results. In technology, a proof of concept serves this purpose. It should answer a specific question and reduce uncertainty rather than attempt to become a miniature version of the final product. A useful proof of concept can therefore be small, incomplete, inelegant, or disposable because its primary output is knowledge. This distinction also requires separating experimentation from delivery: demonstrating that something is possible does not establish that it is safe, sustainable, observable, scalable, or appropriate for production. The workbench is for discovering; the delivery environment is for delivering. The wizard's accumulated knowledge helps formulate useful hypotheses, while the alchemist's experimentation reveals what remains unknown; discoveries can then be documented and incorporated into the shared body of knowledge.

The central problem-solving discipline is choosing the appropriate magic or potion for the actual problem rather than beginning with a preferred technology or solution. A functioning solution is not necessarily an appropriate solution: caching, parallelism, additional servers, retries, failover, database changes, or a new application may all be technically valid while addressing the wrong problem. Established knowledge is valuable because it helps diagnose situations, recognize patterns, understand constraints, and formulate hypotheses, but the professional must first determine what behavior needs explanation and what actually needs to be solved. Only then should they decide whether to use a known solution, reuse an established recipe, or conduct an experiment. The size of one's grimoire or alchemy book does not eliminate the need for diagnosis and contextual judgment.

Even when the grimoire contains a correct and proven answer, it may not provide the best answer for a particular context. General techniques necessarily abstract over differences between situations, so an accepted architecture, algorithm, or technology may introduce unnecessary complexity, inefficiency, cost, or limitations in a specific case. Experimentation therefore has a second role beyond filling gaps in existing knowledge: it can test whether an alternative is better suited to the current circumstances. Established knowledge should remain the starting point because it explains the conventional solution, its rationale, and its trade-offs; experimentation becomes useful when there is a sufficiently strong reason to investigate whether those assumptions hold for the specific context.

A technological community evolves when individual discoveries become shared knowledge. An experiment can be reproduced, challenged, refined, extended, or recombined with other knowledge by different people, eventually becoming part of a larger collective repository. Documentation, wikis, RFCs, technical articles, talks, books, communities, open-source projects, and professional discussions perform this function by transmitting and improving knowledge beyond the environment in which it was originally produced. Sharing therefore means more than publishing a recipe: it allows others to test assumptions, identify limitations, decompose solutions, and combine them with other ideas. Professional excellence consequently includes producing knowledge that remains useful to others rather than keeping expertise exclusively inside one's own head; the most complete wizard-alchemist is the one who helps the entire library grow.

Being a wizard-alchemist does not mean experimenting constantly or pursuing technology for its own sake. It means knowing when established knowledge is sufficient and when uncertainty justifies investigation, while allowing each mode to reinforce the other. The wizard represents knowledge that can be learned and applied; the alchemist represents the practical exploration of what is not yet sufficiently understood. In IT, professionals must recognize patterns, consult documentation, reuse prior experience, formulate hypotheses when necessary, experiment methodically, and turn what they learn into knowledge that can be reproduced and shared. The grimoire provides existing knowledge, the alchemy book preserves reusable experience, and the workbench produces new knowledge; effective technological work depends on moving deliberately among all three.
