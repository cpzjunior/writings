A strong IT professional operates as a wizard-alchemist: they alternate between applying established knowledge and experimentally producing new knowledge. The distinction is functional rather than absolute: the “wizard” represents mastery and application of systematized knowledge, while the “alchemist” represents practical investigation of insufficiently understood properties and relationships. Both study, experiment, learn, accumulate experience, and convert discoveries into reproducible knowledge.

Established knowledge is represented by the grimoire: documentation, books, technical articles, architectural patterns, proven practices, and professional experience. It enables reuse instead of reinvention. The alchemy book represents experiences and recipes discovered by others and recorded for reproduction. Neither repository can contain every possible solution, especially in IT, where programming languages, frameworks, libraries, databases, protocols, APIs, cloud services, observability tools, CI/CD systems, messaging technologies, AI models, and their combinations evolve faster than any individual can master them. Consequently, technical expertise includes not only knowing answers but also knowing how to find missing knowledge, evaluate its reliability, recognize when a known solution does not fit the context, and identify when uncertainty requires investigation.

When existing knowledge is insufficient—or when a known solution may not be optimal for a specific context—the professional goes to the workbench. Experimentation should be systematic rather than random: define the question, formulate a hypothesis, control relevant conditions, observe the result, and extract knowledge from both positive and negative outcomes. A proof of concept serves this purpose. It is not necessarily a miniature production system; its purpose is to reduce uncertainty about a specific question. It may be small, incomplete, inelegant, or disposable because its primary output is knowledge: determining whether a hypothesis holds, identifying limitations, comparing alternatives, or eliminating possibilities. Experimental environments should remain conceptually separate from delivery environments, because demonstrating technical possibility does not establish that a solution is safe, sustainable, observable, scalable, or production-ready.

The first task is diagnosis, not technology selection. A functional spell or potion is not inherently appropriate for every problem. Similarly, a cache, parallelization strategy, larger infrastructure, database change, retry mechanism, failover architecture, or new application may be technically valid while failing to address the actual problem. The professional should first establish what is wrong, what behavior needs explanation, what constraints exist, and what outcome actually needs to be achieved. Only then should they determine whether to consult established knowledge, reuse an existing experience, or experiment. A large repertoire of technologies does not compensate for choosing a solution before understanding the problem.

Established knowledge can also be insufficient even when it contains a correct and proven answer. General techniques are designed to apply across contexts, so a technique that works well generally may introduce unnecessary complexity, inefficiency, cost, or other disadvantages in a particular situation. An architecture may be sound but excessive for a given system; an algorithm may be generally appropriate while another is better for a specific dataset; a mature technology may work reliably while its contextual costs or limitations make an alternative preferable. Experimentation therefore has two roles: discovering what established knowledge does not contain and testing whether a conventional solution remains appropriate under particular conditions. Established knowledge should remain the starting point because it provides the baseline, rationale, and known trade-offs against which alternatives can be evaluated.

Knowledge becomes more valuable when it is shared. Individual experiments can become collective knowledge through documentation, wikis, RFCs, technical articles, talks, books, communities, open-source projects, and professional discussion. Sharing allows others to reproduce discoveries, challenge assumptions, identify limitations, decompose solutions, and recombine them with other knowledge. An isolated discovery solves one problem; a documented and shared discovery can enable an entire family of subsequent solutions. Professional excellence therefore includes creating knowledge that remains useful beyond the individual's direct presence, not merely accumulating expertise privately.

The central competence is knowing when to apply, when to experiment, and how to convert experimentation into reusable knowledge. The grimoire provides established knowledge; the alchemy book preserves reusable experiences and discoveries; the workbench produces knowledge that does not yet exist or whose applicability remains uncertain. These mechanisms form a feedback loop: accumulated knowledge guides experimentation, experimentation produces new knowledge, and successful discoveries can be incorporated into the collective body of knowledge. Excellence in IT is therefore not choosing between application and discovery, but moving deliberately between them while recording and sharing what is learned.
