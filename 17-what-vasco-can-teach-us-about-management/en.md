# What Vasco Can Teach Us About Management

_Culture, leadership, and the risks of building organizations that depend more on people than on institutions_

**Summary:** I use Vasco as a case study to argue that persistent problems within an organization can be more cultural than administrative. When leadership, decision-making, and knowledge become concentrated in individuals, the institution becomes unstable, reactive, and unable to accumulate capabilities over time. Therefore, it is not enough to seek better managers, copy competitors, or restore the strength of the past: it is necessary to build processes, incentives, and reference points that survive people and crises. To me, the true legacy of a manager is to leave the organization stronger, more predictable, and more capable of continuing to make good decisions after they leave.

---

My friends challenged me to write about what Vasco can teach us about management. The provocation came from a casual conversation, but I started thinking that perhaps there was a more interesting question there than simply discussing football: what happens when an organization spends so much time trying to solve its problems that it begins to incorporate those very problems into its culture? Instead of looking at Vasco merely as a football club, I decided to treat it as a case study to discuss something that interests me much more: entrepreneurship, management, and organizational culture.

Vasco is particularly interesting for this discussion because it has characteristics that, in other contexts, would be considered major competitive advantages. It is a century-old brand, with an enormous history, significant achievements, recognized idols, and a fan base that many businesses would never be able to build. Even so, for decades the club has seemed to struggle to turn these assets into a consistently healthy organization capable of sustaining good results over time. This leads me to a hypothesis that may be more interesting than simply saying that Vasco is poorly managed. Perhaps the problem is cultural.

There is an important difference between an organization that makes bad decisions and an organization that has developed a culture capable of reproducing bad decisions. In the first case, replacing the manager may solve the problem. In the second, replacing the manager may simply start another cycle within the same system.

This difference also separates an organization that eventually manages to achieve good results from an organization that has developed the institutional capacity to produce them consistently. It is this second possibility that I want to explore here.

## The Problem of Personalism

For many years, Vasco's management was strongly associated with the figure of Eurico Miranda. I do not intend to discuss here whether Eurico was good or bad for the club. That is a sporting, political, and historical discussion that falls outside the purpose of this text. What interests me is a broader question: what happens to an organization when one person concentrates power and becomes, for a long time, the main reference for how that organization should be managed?

The problem with personalism is that it does not necessarily end when the person leaves office. A very strong leader can establish behaviors, power relationships, decision-making patterns, and expectations that continue to exist after their departure. The organization learns that important decisions go through one person, that leadership means concentrating power, and that the figure of the executive can become larger than the institution itself. Over time, what began as a characteristic of an individual can become a characteristic of the organization.

This is a well-known problem in companies. A company can grow around an extremely capable founder and, for years, that concentration may even appear to be an advantage. Decisions are fast, there is a clear vision, and everyone knows who makes the decisions. The problem emerges when the organization needs to survive that person's absence. If knowledge, strategy, relationships, and decision-making criteria were concentrated in that person, the company discovers that it built dependency instead of institutional capacity.

This may be one of the main differences between strong leadership and a strong institution. Strong leadership can concentrate capability in one person. A strong institution can turn that capability into something that remains within the organization.

This also helps explain how a culture perpetuates itself. Culture is not merely what is written in a company's values. It is reinforced daily by the incentives an organization creates, the behaviors it rewards, the behaviors it tolerates, and the decisions it makes. If certain ways of exercising power produce results or guarantee survival within an organization, other people naturally learn that this is the path to prosper within it.

In Vasco's case, what interests me is not Eurico himself, but the institutional legacy of a prolonged period of personalism. When an organization spends decades associating its management with the figure of strong leaders, it can create a cultural expectation that the club needs someone capable of centralizing decisions, resolving conflicts, and defining the institution's direction. And this is much harder to change than simply finding a better executive.

An organization can hire an excellent manager and still continue to function poorly if the culture remains intact. The new manager arrives with their ideas, but encounters a structure that already has habits, relationships, incentives, and expectations. If the organization does not change these elements, there is a strong possibility that the new manager will need to adapt to the system in order to operate within it, rather than being able to transform the system.

That is why I believe an organization can turn even good managers into bad managers. The problem ceases to be merely who is sitting in the chair and becomes the chair itself, the structure around it, and everything the organization has learned to expect from whoever occupies it. Perhaps this is the most important point about personalism: the real problem is not having strong leadership. It is allowing the strength of leadership to become greater than the strength of the institution.

## An Organization That Never Stabilizes

There is another aspect of Vasco that catches my attention: the recurring feeling that the club never really manages to lower the temperature. Even during periods when football naturally offers a pause, such as during a World Cup, there seems to be a permanent need to produce some kind of movement. There is always a new crisis, a new change, a new discussion, or a new urgent decision.

For an organization, this is dangerous. Not every absence of movement is a lack of management. Sometimes an organization needs precisely to stop in order to analyze, consolidate decisions, plan, and allow people to execute what has been defined. Management is not about producing movement all the time. In many moments, management is about creating the conditions for what has already been decided to mature.

This is where organizational climate comes in. If culture represents patterns of behavior that become consolidated over time, climate is how people perceive and experience those patterns in their daily lives. When an organization's practices convey instability, permanent urgency, and low predictability, this tends to produce a climate in which people start working while expecting the next change. And this expectation inevitably affects how they make decisions today.

There is an important difference between being a dynamic organization and being a permanently reactive organization. The first changes when necessary. The second changes because it cannot remain stable. The first has the capacity to adapt. The second lives in a state of urgency.

When a company lives in a permanent state of urgency, people begin to interpret every decision as temporary. Today's project may be abandoned tomorrow. Today's manager may not be there tomorrow. The current priority may cease to exist at the next meeting. Over time, this creates an environment in which no one knows exactly which decisions deserve long-term commitment.

In this environment, individual rationality changes. Employees start asking less “what is best for the organization in the long term?” and more “what should I do so I am not harmed by the next change?” Instead of taking calculated risks, they avoid them. Instead of building, they wait. Instead of thinking about the next stage, they try to understand what the leadership's next decision will be.

This behavior can be perfectly rational for someone inside the organization. If priorities change constantly, investing energy in a long-term initiative can represent a personal risk. If evaluation criteria are unstable, avoiding decisions may be safer than taking responsibility for them. This is how an organization can produce behaviors that, individually, seem defensive but are logical responses to the environment it has created.

This is particularly harmful for an organization that depends on accumulated knowledge. A company needs its employees to learn, test, make mistakes, correct them, and build upon what was done previously. If everything can be undone at any moment, some of that knowledge never becomes institutional capability. The organization does not accumulate experience at the same rate at which it accumulates changes.

A healthy culture does not need to be static. It needs to be predictable enough to allow people to make decisions, take responsibility, and build something that survives the next cycle. Stability does not mean an absence of change. It means people can distinguish a necessary change from yet another temporary change.

In football, this difference becomes even more visible. A squad can perfectly well deal with pressure, demands, and occasional changes. What it should not have to do is step onto the field without knowing whether what it is building will have any continuity. When administrative instability becomes part of the work environment, it ceases to be a problem restricted to the board and begins to affect those directly responsible for execution.

And that is precisely where the discussion about culture stops seeming abstract. What happens within the administration reaches the people who work at the club and, in football, eventually reaches the field.

## The Culture That Reaches the Field

It is common to treat organizational culture as something abstract, related to values written on a wall or presentations from human resources. In practice, culture is much more concrete. It is the set of behaviors that an organization learns to consider normal and that its members begin to reproduce. It appears in the way people make decisions, deal with mistakes, take responsibility, and respond to change.

For this reason, I do not believe it is possible to completely separate administrative culture from team performance. What happens off the field inevitably reaches the field. A player may not participate in board decisions, but they live with the consequences of those decisions every day.

This does not mean, of course, that every player who fails to deliver what is expected of them is simply a victim of the environment. Some may not be sufficiently committed, others may be below the required technical level, and others may simply not work in that context. Individual responsibility still exists. The point is different: it is much harder to demand commitment, discipline, and long-term thinking from people who work in an environment where everything seems capable of changing at any moment.

Imagine working at a company where you do not know whether your boss will be there tomorrow. The strategy defined today may be completely abandoned a few weeks later, new leadership may arrive and decide that everything previously being done was wrong, and people who seemed essential may simply cease to be part of the organization. In this environment, it is natural for people to start working differently.

They stop thinking only about what is best for the organization and start thinking about how to position themselves in relation to the next change. When the future is unpredictable, protecting oneself becomes a rational strategy.

In football, this can be even more evident. A player does not need to know only whether they will start the next match. They need to be able to trust the references around them. If the coach who has just arrived may be fired tomorrow, if the director who hired them may leave the club next week, and if the strategy being built may be abandoned next month, it becomes difficult to create any sense of continuity.

The problem is not pressure to perform. Pressure is part of sport and, to some extent, is necessary. The problem is working in an environment that permanently resembles a powder keg, where any poor result can trigger another change and any change can once again alter expectations about those inside.

Such an environment can produce more insecure players, not necessarily because they lack quality or commitment, but because the references around them are constantly changing. It is difficult to maintain discipline and concentration when the organization conveys the feeling that its own ground can shift at any moment. And this applies to all of the club's professionals. Coaches, analysts, scouts, physiologists, academy staff, and administrative employees also need to make decisions knowing that their priorities may change at any moment.

There is also a less visible effect: instability also makes it harder to hire good professionals. Qualified people usually have alternatives and tend to evaluate not only salary and position, but also the environment in which they will work, the quality of leadership, opportunities for development, and the organization's predictability. A company known for constantly changing direction may still be able to hire, but it may have greater difficulty convincing the best professionals to build a career within it.

This creates a particularly difficult cycle to break. An unstable culture makes it harder to attract and retain good professionals. The loss of good professionals reduces the organization's capabilities. Reduced capabilities produce worse results. Worse results increase pressure for change, and changes reinforce instability once again.

The same phenomenon happens in a company. If no one believes that a strategy will last, no one truly invests in it. If no one knows which criteria will be used to evaluate their work, people begin to avoid decisions that could expose them. If a change in leadership automatically means a complete change in direction, the organization stops accumulating knowledge and starts accumulating only survival experiences.

This is how a culture of instability becomes a performance problem. People remain responsible for their own performance, but the environment changes the cost of taking risks, committing, and thinking long term.

In football, this eventually shows up on the field. A team is not merely the result of its players' talent. It is also the result of the trust that exists among them, the clarity about what they need to do, and the conviction that they are building something that will exist tomorrow. When these references disappear, the organization's instability begins to appear in the behavior of the people within it.

## The Search for References

Another behavior worth reflecting on is the way Vasco, like any organization in a competitive environment, needs to constantly look at what is happening around it. This is natural and, in many cases, necessary. Companies monitor competitors, study their moves, analyze their investments, and try to understand what is working elsewhere. In football, this is even more evident. Clubs observe management models, signings, structures, youth academies, and ways of assembling their squads. There is nothing wrong with this. On the contrary, finding external references can be one of the best ways to learn.

The problem begins when an external reference stops being a source of learning and becomes the primary source of direction. There is an important difference between benchmarking and mimicry. Benchmarking means looking outward to understand what can be adapted to one's own reality. Mimicry means looking outward to decide what to do.

A club can observe Flamengo, Palmeiras, Botafogo, or any other organization that is producing good results and try to understand which practices explain that performance. But there is a fundamental step after observation: understanding whether those practices make sense for its own reality. What capabilities do we have? Which ones do we still need to develop? Where are our greatest weaknesses? What resources are available? And, above all, what path do we want to build?

This difference also appears in the business world. An organization can spend so much time studying its competitors that it ends up confusing strategy with comparison. If the competitor increases investment, pressure arises to invest as well. If it changes its structure, there is a temptation to do the same. If it finds a formula that seems to work, that formula becomes a reference for its own decisions.

The problem is that two organizations can observe exactly the same practice and achieve completely different results. A company can copy another's structure, hire similar professionals, and adopt the same processes without reproducing the result. This happens because practices do not exist in isolation. They depend on people, capabilities, processes, incentives, and context.

Therefore, real benchmarking is not about copying what others do. It is about trying to understand why it works. The risk of looking too much at competitors is losing sight of one's own identity. The organization starts responding to the environment instead of trying to define its own position within it. And when this happens, every external move can produce a new internal change.

Mature organizations do not stop monitoring the market. They simply do not allow the market to determine all their decisions. They can identify external patterns without abandoning an understanding of their own capabilities, limitations, and objectives. They can learn from others without depending on others to know what to do.

Perhaps this is an important difference between learning from others and depending on others to find a direction. The first behavior increases the organization's capabilities. The second can make it permanently reactive.

In Vasco's case, therefore, the more interesting question is not merely “what are other clubs doing better?” It is “what do we need to build within Vasco so that, a few years from now, other organizations will be looking to us for references?” Looking at the competitor can show what is possible. But looking inward allows us to discover what is possible for us.

## Past Performance Does Not Reflect Future Performance

Perhaps this is one of the greatest challenges for traditional organizations. The greater the history, the greater the temptation to turn it into a justification for the present. Vasco has an enormous historical legacy. That is an asset. But history is not strategy, just as past performance is not a guarantee of future performance.

This logic is well known in the investment world. A fund that achieved excellent returns over a given period cannot simply assume that those results will continue. Past performance can reveal competence, execution capability, and even a structural advantage, but it does not eliminate the need to continue making good decisions. Markets change, conditions change, and competitive advantages can disappear.

The same happens with companies. A company that was the market leader twenty years ago may still have a valuable brand, loyal customers, and an important reputation. None of this, however, guarantees that it will remain relevant. History is evidence of what that organization has already been capable of doing. It is not a guarantee of what it will be capable of doing tomorrow.

In football, this difference may be even more evident. Fans do not follow a club merely to remember what it has already won. They need new reasons to keep following it. New players, new stories, new achievements, and new moments of identification. The past can explain why someone became a Vasco supporter. The present helps explain why someone remains one.

And there is an additional consequence for organizations with a very long history: the greater the history of success, the greater the expectations of those around them. An investor who puts money into a company with a history of growth expects that capability to continue to exist. A customer of a traditional brand expects a certain standard of quality. And a supporter of a club like Vasco does not compare the present only with its rivals. They also compare the present with what they know the club has already been capable of achieving.

This creates a particularly interesting kind of pressure. History itself becomes a benchmark for accountability. The greater the capability an organization has already demonstrated, the harder it is to convince its supporters that inferior results are simply the new normal.

It is not enough to say that Vasco is a big club. The question becomes whether it is behaving like an organization capable of sustaining the stature it has built. Likewise, a company cannot live indefinitely on the memory of having been a market leader. At some point, it needs to demonstrate again why it deserves to remain relevant.

This also has a generational dimension. A club needs to create new memories so that its history continues to be passed on. If a generation spends years without experiencing meaningful moments, it will have fewer of its own stories to pass on to the next. The historical legacy continues to exist, but its ability to generate new connections begins to diminish.

Reputation is a stock. Relevance is a flow. A traditional organization needs to use its accumulated stock to produce new value. Otherwise, it risks continuing to be respected for what it was while losing relevance because of what it has ceased to be.

The past shows what an organization has already been capable of. The true challenge of management is to turn that past capability into present and future capability.

## The Savior Problem

Perhaps there is a reason why organizations in crisis constantly look for someone capable of solving everything. We like to associate complex problems with figures capable of solving them. It is much easier to imagine a new CEO, a major investor, a renowned coach, or an exceptional player arriving and putting the organization back on track than to accept that the problem may require something much less cinematic: a gradual change in processes, incentives, and behaviors.

The savior narrative is comfortable because it turns a collective problem into an individual solution. Instead of asking what needs to change in the organization, we ask who needs to arrive to change it. But there is a structural problem with this logic: people can become single points of failure.

A manager can make mistakes. They can be fired. They can leave voluntarily. They can lose political support. They may simply fail to deliver what was expected of them. If knowledge, authority, and decision-making capacity are excessively concentrated in a single person, any of these possibilities can turn into an institutional crisis.

This does not mean that strong leaders are bad. An organization needs leadership. The problem begins when leadership ceases to be a mechanism for developing the organization and becomes the mechanism through which the organization operates.

A strong culture should produce the opposite effect. It should distribute reference points throughout the organization. People should know which principles guide decisions, which objectives matter, and how to act even when there is no central figure telling them exactly what to do. Knowledge needs to remain within the institution, processes need to survive people, and decisions need to maintain some degree of consistency even when leadership changes. The leader should be able to strengthen the culture. They should not be the culture itself.

Therefore, even if Vasco hired an exceptional manager and a Champions League-winning coach tomorrow, that would not, by itself, mean its problems were solved. They would still be entering an organization with its own history, habits, power relationships, incentives, and culture. If these elements remain unchanged, the new professionals will have to work within the same conditions that produced the previous problems.

And there is an irony in this. Sometimes the organization finds exactly the person it was looking for and, even so, that person cannot produce the expected transformation. Not necessarily because the manager or coach is bad, but because the organization is more powerful than any isolated individual.

That is why a company should not ask only who is the best person to occupy a position. It should also ask what kind of organization that person will find when they arrive and what kind of organization they will leave when they go.

This leads us to a more interesting question: if a leader's value should not lie in the dependency they create, how should we measure what they leave behind?

## The True Legacy of a Manager

Perhaps the most important question when evaluating a manager is not only what they managed to accomplish while they were in charge, but what they left behind after they left. If the CEO leaves the company tomorrow, what remains? If the founder disappears, do people still know how to make decisions? If the director is replaced, do the strategy and processes continue to exist? If a key employee leaves, does the knowledge remain within the organization?

These questions help differentiate individual results from institutional legacy. A manager can deliver extraordinary results and still leave an organization more dependent on individuals than it was when they arrived. They can centralize decisions, concentrate knowledge, create relationships that depend exclusively on their presence, and make their departure represent a rupture. In this case, an important part of their success was not transformed into institutional capability.

The true legacy of a manager should be precisely the opposite. It is about transforming individual knowledge into organizational knowledge, personal decisions into processes, personal relationships into institutions, and an individual vision into a direction that can be continued by other people. It is about making the organization learn, rather than simply making the manager get things right.

A healthy organization is not one that never changes leadership. It is one that can change leadership without losing its identity, execution capability, and direction. The manager may leave, but what they helped build should remain.

That is why I believe Vasco's problem is much more cultural than simply administrative. It is not merely about finding better managers, signing better players, or choosing the right coach. These things matter, but they happen within a larger system. And systems produce behaviors.

Perhaps this is the main lesson I take from Vasco for management. An organization should not depend on one person to know who it is, on a rival to know what to do, on a crisis to decide when to act, or on the glories of the past to justify its relevance in the present. It should build within itself the conditions to answer these questions.

The best manager is not the one who makes the organization depend on them. It is the one who leaves the organization stronger, more predictable, and more capable of making good decisions after they are no longer in charge.

In the end, perhaps this is the true legacy of leadership: not how much the organization needed it while it was present, but how well it managed to continue being after it was gone.
