# People, incentives, and culture as mechanisms for strategy execution

_How partnership, People, and culture turn strategy into behavior._

**Summary:** I argue that strategy only becomes reality when the organization's mechanisms make people repeatedly make decisions in the same direction. Partnership, People, incentives, leadership, culture, and Marketing are different ways of producing this alignment, influencing who joins, who grows, who is rewarded, who stays, and which behaviors gain meaning. The point is not to have good practices in isolation, but to ensure that they all tell the same story. When these mechanisms are coherent, strategy stops being a presentation and starts becoming an organization.

---

I recently read the article “How to build a partnership program in your company,” by Guilherme Benchimol. I liked it a lot, not only because it shares practical lessons about the topic, but also because it provides more details about XP's case and the circumstances that led to the creation of its model. There is value in understanding not only a management solution that worked, but also the context that made that solution emerge and evolve.

The article made me think about partnership, but also about a broader question. At its core, a company executes its strategy through the people it hires, develops, promotes, compensates, and retains. The mechanisms used to do this are not neutral. They signal behaviors, create incentives, and help define how people make decisions.

A strategy may define that a company wants to grow, innovate, operate efficiently, or build long-term relationships with its customers. But these objectives do not become reality because they were put into a presentation. They become reality when the decisions made daily by people in the organization begin to converge in that direction. And these decisions are influenced by the incentives the company creates, the behaviors it rewards or tolerates, whom it promotes and decides to retain, and the examples it chooses to make visible.

Partnership is a particularly interesting example because it makes this relationship quite explicit. By allowing certain people to participate economically in the value they help create, the model seeks to bring their interests closer to the long-term interests of the business. And it is precisely from this idea that I began thinking about the role of People, culture, and even Marketing in strategy execution.

## Partnership as an incentive mechanism

Partnership, in essence, is a way of aligning part of a person's compensation and career path with the value they help build in the company. Instead of the relationship being based exclusively on salary, bonus, and career progression, the professional also has an economic stake in the long-term outcome of the business. Depending on the model, this may be accompanied by greater autonomy, responsibility, and participation in decision-making.

The logic is quite powerful. In a traditional employment relationship, the professional's return is primarily associated with performing their role. In partnership, part of that return becomes dependent on creating value for the business as a whole. This can broaden the incentive horizon. A decision that reduces this year's results but increases the company's value over the next five years may be perceived differently by someone who also participates in that future value.

It is important not to confuse economic participation with ownership. Having equity does not automatically turn someone into a partner in the behavioral or organizational sense. For partnership to produce its full effect, there needs to be some connection between economic participation, responsibility, autonomy, and the ability to influence the outcome being built. In some models, this relationship also involves participation in decision-making and greater access to business information.

It is precisely this combination that makes me like the model. There is something intellectually elegant about bringing contribution and value creation closer together. If someone helps build a company that is worth much more in the future, it seems reasonable that they should be able to participate in part of that value. In addition to being a retention mechanism, partnership can create a long-term horizon and a different relationship between the professional and the business.

But strong incentives also need to be designed carefully. The mechanism can produce exactly the behavior one intends to avoid if it is tied to the wrong metric. An excessively individual incentive can undermine collaboration. An incentive based solely on growth can reward growth without quality. A model focused on short-term results can conflict with a long-term strategy. The challenge is not simply to create strong incentives, but to create incentives that reinforce the behaviors the strategy requires.

There is also a question of timing. Partnership requires some ability to define criteria, assess contribution, establish entry and exit rules, and sustain a participation logic that is understandable to people. In a very small company, especially in the early stages of a startup, there may simply not be enough structure for this. Founders still directly follow people's work, responsibilities change rapidly, and the company itself is still discovering what its critical capabilities will be in the future.

This is a curious situation. Precisely when a person's potential for value creation can be enormous, the company has less information to determine who the most important people will be in the future. An interest granted today can be an excellent incentive, but it can also represent a decision that is difficult to revisit when the organization, strategy, or that person's role changes.

Therefore, I don't think the question is whether a company should or should not have partnership. The question is when it has the conditions to implement the mechanism in a way that the alignment gain is greater than the complexity created. There is no magic number of employees or revenue, in my view. A more interesting signal is when informal alignment begins to stop scaling. While founders can speak directly with each person and explain what matters, simple mechanisms may be sufficient. As different levels of leadership emerge, responsibilities become more distributed, and a larger group of people has a significant impact on value creation, more structured mechanisms begin to make sense.

There is also a governance dimension that should not be ignored. Partnership does not only distribute part of the company's economic value. Depending on its design, it can also distribute information, autonomy, and decision-making power. This makes it even more important to define who can enter the program, what the criteria are, how exit works, and what the relationship is between partners and the rest of the organization. A poorly designed program can create an internal elite without necessarily producing the alignment it intended to create.

And this does not mean that a small company needs to wait before starting to build this alignment. There are mechanisms with different levels of complexity that can fulfill part of this function. Bonuses tied to collective results can bring the team closer to company performance. Profit sharing can create a direct relationship between results and compensation. Stock options can provide exposure to the company's future upside, typically associated with vesting mechanisms. Phantom shares can reproduce part of the economic logic of equity participation without necessarily granting shares. Long-term goals, autonomy, recognition, and development systems can also reinforce important behaviors.

These mechanisms are not necessarily inferior versions of partnership. They are different tools for different contexts. A very early-stage company may benefit more from a simple goal-setting and variable compensation system than from a sophisticated partnership program. As it gains scale, predictability, and governance capacity, it can incorporate more structured mechanisms for economic participation.

The principle is more important than the tool. The objective is to bring people's decisions closer to the outcomes the company needs to produce. Partnership is, for me, one of the most powerful ways to do this. But it does not need to be the first step, nor does it need to be applied in the same way to everyone or at every stage of the company. The question becomes less “should we have partnership?” and more “which incentive mechanism is appropriate for the company's stage and the behavior we want to produce?”

## Who exactly are we trying to retain?

This discussion leads to another important provocation. We tend to talk about People and retention as if low turnover were necessarily a positive indicator. It isn't. A company may have low turnover because it manages to retain excellent professionals, but it may also have low turnover because it is unable to renew its organization or incorporate new capabilities. Likewise, losing people is not necessarily a problem. Some departures are natural, some may be desirable, and others may create room for capabilities the company does not yet have.

The objective of People, therefore, should not simply be to maximize retention. It should be to understand whether hiring, development, compensation, promotion, and retention decisions are increasing the organization's ability to execute its strategy.

This also changes how we evaluate a mechanism such as partnership. If it is used as a retention tool, it is not enough to measure whether partners stay longer with the company. We need to understand whether the people who remain are precisely those we want to retain, whether they take on more responsibility, whether they make decisions with a longer-term horizon, and whether the mechanism produces the behaviors the organization needs.

There is also a distinction that seems important to me. A mechanism can generate retention because it creates alignment or because it creates lock-in. A person may stay because they believe in the company, want to participate in building it, and see value in the long term, or because leaving would mean giving up a significant economic benefit. Both phenomena appear as retention in the metrics, but they are very different from an organizational perspective. And this does not mean that every long-term incentive produces lock-in. The problem arises when the cost of leaving becomes more determinant of staying than the person's actual relationship with the company.

At its core, the question is not how many people we can keep. It is which people we need to keep, why, and whether the mechanisms we use to retain them are increasing the company's ability to execute its strategy.

## How do we measure People effectiveness?

This may be one of the most difficult questions for a People function. It is relatively easy to measure headcount, turnover, absenteeism, personnel costs, time to hire, training participation, or employee survey results. These are important metrics, but many of them say more about what the function did than about the impact it produced.

People has, however, a characteristic that makes its role particularly important. Dissatisfied, demotivated, or misaligned people can produce effects that go far beyond their own experience. They can affect team climate, increase conflict, reduce productivity, damage customer relationships, accelerate the departure of other people, or simply cause important decisions to be made poorly. At the same time, not every dissatisfaction is a problem the company needs to solve. Some demands need to be addressed, others need to be negotiated, and others need to receive a no.

This is where I see an important part of the People function. The area acts as an organizational mediation mechanism. At times, it needs to bring a demand to senior leadership that might not otherwise reach it. At others, it needs to explain to a person or a team why a particular decision cannot be made. It needs to understand the interests of both sides without simply becoming the representative of one of them. Its role is to help build a sustainable relationship between people and the business.

It is almost like family therapy, except for the company. There are conflicts, different expectations, power relationships, communication problems, and situations in which no one is necessarily wrong, but some decision needs to be made. The difference is that, in this case, these relationships also need to produce results.

Therefore, the most important question is not simply whether people are satisfied. It is whether People practices are helping the company execute its strategy. The answer depends on the strategy itself. If the company needs to innovate, we need to understand whether we are able to attract, develop, and retain people capable of innovating. If efficiency is the priority, we need to assess whether compensation, promotion, and management mechanisms are reinforcing that behavior. If the strategy depends on collaboration, we need to know whether we are promoting leaders who build teams or simply professionals who deliver excellent individual results.

This even changes how we think about performance. A performance review should not exist only to determine raises or promotions. It should also help the organization understand which behaviors are being rewarded and whether those behaviors are the ones it needs to multiply.

The same applies to virtually every People decision. Who we hire, who we promote, who we compensate better, who receives more autonomy, and whom we decide to retain are concrete signals about what the organization considers valuable. Each of these decisions also affects the perception of other people about what is necessary to succeed in that company.

For this reason, I think People should treat its main initiatives as hypotheses to be tested in practice. If we change an incentive, what behavior do we expect to stimulate? If we create a development program, what organizational capability do we want to build? If we change promotion criteria, what behaviors do we expect to become more frequent? And, most importantly, how will we know whether these changes produced the expected effect?

This does not mean turning every people management decision into a statistical experiment. It means establishing a clearer relationship between the intervention and the expected outcome. Without this connection, there is a risk of measuring People activity very well while knowing little about its effectiveness.

There is also an important limit to this logic. People can help build the system, identify problems, produce data, and challenge decisions, but it cannot assume responsibility for the organization alone. If leadership is not willing to make difficult decisions about people, no People framework will be sufficient.

Ultimately, the People function should help answer two questions: “Are we building the organization our strategy requires?” and “Are we managing to solve people-related problems before they become business problems?” For me, these two questions justify the existence of a People function much more than any isolated HR indicator.

## Culture as a mechanism for execution and belonging

Culture is a difficult concept to measure precisely because it is not an isolated object within the organization. It emerges from the combination of the people who are part of the company, the behaviors that are repeated, the decisions that are made, and the patterns that end up being reinforced over time. But culture is not just a way of doing work. It is also a way of belonging to the organization.

A person can deliver their projects, meet their targets, and still not feel part of the company. They can do exactly what they were hired to do without having any identification with the business, the people around them, or what the organization is trying to build. This does not mean that every professional needs to make the company part of their personal identity, but there is a meaningful difference between simply working in an organization and feeling that you are part of it.

This dimension of belonging matters because organizations are built through behaviors that go far beyond the formal description of each role. People help colleagues who are not part of their direct responsibilities, share knowledge, take on problems they could ignore, defend the company before customers, and make choices considering the impact on other areas. Not all of this can be put into a goal or policy.

In this sense, culture has a direct relationship with incentives and People practices. People observe who is hired, who is promoted, who receives more autonomy, who is recognized, and which behaviors are tolerated. They also observe who is heard, who participates in decisions, which stories are told about the company, and which people are considered examples. From these observations, they form a perception of what really matters in that organization.

A company that needs collaboration, for example, can create structures and incentives that favor collective objectives, promote leaders capable of building good teams, and recognize professionals who share knowledge. If these behaviors are repeated over time, they stop depending on an explicit instruction and become part of how the organization operates.

The opposite also happens. A company may encourage individual competition, systematically promote those who deliver isolated results, and tolerate behaviors that harm other teams. In that environment, collaboration is unlikely to become a predominant behavior simply because it was defined as a priority. More importantly, people quickly learn that there is a difference between what the company says it values and what actually produces recognition and career progression.

Therefore, culture can be understood as an execution mechanism, but also as a belonging mechanism. When certain behaviors and references become shared, people gain greater clarity not only about what they need to do, but also about how to make decisions in situations that are not covered by a rule or process. Culture does not replace processes, controls, or governance, but it helps fill the gaps these mechanisms cannot cover.

This also explains why culture can be difficult to change. Changing a narrative is simple. Changing the patterns of hiring, promotion, compensation, leadership, and recognition that sustain a given behavior is much harder. A culture changes when the signals people receive from the organization change consistently.

I also do not believe that a strong culture is necessarily a culture that is appropriate for every moment of the company. A small organization may work very well with autonomy, proximity, and speed. As it grows, however, it may need more coordination, processes, and specialization. If the behaviors that helped the company get there begin to make this change more difficult, a characteristic that was once an advantage can become a constraint.

Culture, therefore, is not good or bad in the abstract. It is more or less appropriate to the strategy and stage of the organization. And perhaps it is precisely in situations that are not covered by a rule or process that it becomes most visible. When someone helps a colleague without it being part of their target, takes on a problem that is not formally theirs, or makes a decision considering the impact on other areas, they are revealing much more about the organization's culture than any statement of values.

## Marketing also participates in culture

This brings me to a third area that normally appears separate from this discussion: Marketing. If culture is also formed by the behaviors an organization repeats, recognizes, and turns into references, Marketing inevitably participates in this process. Not because it is responsible for creating the culture, but because it helps transform organizational experiences into shared stories, symbols, and references.

Stories about founders, the people who are placed in the spotlight, the customers who are celebrated, the results that become case studies, and the behaviors the company chooses to communicate help build a narrative about who we are and what we consider important. This narrative influences both external perception and the way people within the organization understand the place they belong to.

This is particularly relevant because companies are not made only of processes and goals. They are also made of stories. People tell how a particular customer was won, how a difficult problem was solved, who helped when no one else knew what to do, and which decisions were important for the company to reach a certain point. Over time, these stories become references for the type of behavior the organization values.

A simple example is companies that make a point of celebrating professionals who complete 5, 10, or 15 years with the company. The celebration may seem like merely a recognition action, but it also communicates something about the organization. It turns tenure into a story worth telling and signals that there is value in building a long-term career within the company. This can strengthen a sense of belonging, although it is also important to understand exactly what is being celebrated: tenure itself or what the person built during that period. The same happens when an organization turns a difficult project, a customer achievement, or a decision made at a critical moment into a story that is repeated internally.

Marketing can help scale these stories. It can turn an isolated case into an example, a behavior into a symbol, and an achievement into a shared narrative. In this sense, it participates in building the sense of belonging we discussed earlier. An individual experience can become a collective reference.

There is, however, an important condition: the narrative needs to be consistent with the actual experience. Marketing can amplify a culture, but it is unlikely to sustain for long a story that contradicts what people experience. If the company communicates collaboration but its evaluation mechanisms reward exclusively individual results, communication does not correct the incentive. If it talks about autonomy but decisions remain centralized, the narrative merely makes the contradiction more evident.

For this reason, I see Marketing as another mechanism for cultural reinforcement. People influences culture through who joins, who grows, and who stays. Incentives influence it through what is economically rewarded. Leadership influences it through the decisions it makes and the behaviors it tolerates. Marketing helps give meaning, visibility, and scale to the stories that emerge from these behaviors.

When these mechanisms tell the same story, culture tends to strengthen. When they tell different stories, the organization produces conflicting signals and people have to figure out for themselves which one represents what actually matters.

Perhaps that is precisely where Marketing ceases to be merely a function focused on the market. By helping the company tell who it is, what it values, and which stories deserve to be remembered, it also helps people within it understand what kind of organization they are part of.

## What matters is that the system is coherent

In the end, partnership, People, incentives, culture, and Marketing seem like different topics, but perhaps they are manifestations of the same question: how does an organization turn strategy into behavior?

A strategy gains meaning when it begins to guide decisions. And decisions are made by people, who respond to incentives, observe leadership behavior, interpret what is rewarded and punished, form expectations about their careers, and build a perception of what really matters in that organization.

Therefore, perhaps the most important question is not whether we have a good partnership program, a good People function, a strong culture, or effective communication. The question is whether these mechanisms point in the same direction.

If the strategy says one thing, compensation incentivizes another, leadership tolerates a third, promotions reward a fourth, and communication celebrates a fifth, we have a problem larger than an HR, Marketing, or culture problem. We have an execution problem.

That is why I continue to like the logic of partnership. When well designed, it makes explicit a relationship that many organizations try to build in more indirect ways: those who help create value also participate in that value. But partnership is only one of the possible ways to produce this alignment and, especially in very small or early-stage companies, there may be simpler mechanisms that are more appropriate to the organization's stage.

The principle, however, remains. Companies execute their strategies through the people they hire, the behaviors they incentivize, the decisions they reward, the leaders they promote, the people they choose to retain, and the stories they decide to tell.

Perhaps it is possible to understand a great deal about a company by observing precisely these choices. Not only what it says it values, but who it hires, who it promotes, who it rewards, whom it decides to retain, which behaviors it tolerates, and which stories it turns into examples.

In the end, strategy is not only a definition of where the company wants to go. It is also the system it builds to make people repeatedly make decisions that bring it closer to that place. This is where strategy stops being a presentation and starts becoming an organization.
