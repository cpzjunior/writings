I recently read Guilherme Benchimol’s article “How to build a partnership program in your company” and found value not only in its practical lessons and description of XP’s model, but also in the context that explains why the model emerged and evolved. It prompted a broader reflection: companies execute strategy through the people they hire, develop, promote, compensate, and retain, and these mechanisms are not neutral because they signal desired behaviors and shape decisions. Strategic objectives such as growth, innovation, efficiency, or long-term customer relationships become real only when everyday decisions consistently move in those directions. Partnership is therefore interesting not merely as a compensation practice, but as one example of a broader system through which organizations align people's decisions with strategy.

Partnership is fundamentally an incentive mechanism that connects part of a professional’s compensation and career path to the long-term value they help create, potentially alongside greater autonomy, responsibility, and decision-making participation. Unlike a traditional employment relationship, where returns are primarily tied to role performance, partnership can extend the incentive horizon toward the value of the business as a whole. However, economic participation is not automatically equivalent to behavioral or organizational partnership: its effect depends on the connection among economic participation, responsibility, autonomy, influence, and information. The model is attractive because it can align contribution with value creation and create a longer-term relationship with the business, but incentives must be designed carefully because poorly chosen metrics can produce undesirable behavior, such as individual optimization, low-quality growth, or short-termism. Timing also matters: very early companies may lack the information, governance, and stability needed to determine who should receive participation, while founders can often achieve alignment informally. As organizations grow, responsibilities become distributed and informal alignment becomes harder to scale, making more structured mechanisms increasingly relevant. Partnership also has a governance dimension because it can distribute not only economic value but potentially information, autonomy, and decision-making power, requiring clear rules around entry, exit, criteria, and relationships with the rest of the organization. Simpler mechanisms such as collective bonuses, profit sharing, stock options, phantom shares, long-term goals, autonomy, recognition, and development systems can serve similar alignment purposes at different stages. The underlying principle is therefore more important than the specific instrument: the appropriate mechanism depends on the company’s stage and the behaviors it needs to produce.

The discussion of partnership also raises the question of whom the organization is actually trying to retain. Low turnover is not inherently positive, because it can reflect either successful retention of valuable professionals or an inability to renew capabilities, while departures can sometimes be desirable or create room for needed skills. People’s objective should therefore not simply be maximizing retention, but determining whether hiring, development, compensation, promotion, and retention decisions increase the organization’s ability to execute strategy. A partnership program should consequently be evaluated not only by whether participants remain longer, but by whether the right people remain, assume greater responsibility, adopt longer-term perspectives, and display the behaviors the organization needs. Retention can also result from genuine alignment or from economic lock-in, and these can look identical in turnover metrics while representing different organizational dynamics. The central questions are therefore which people the company needs to retain, why they need to be retained, and whether the mechanisms used to retain them improve strategic execution.

Measuring People effectiveness is difficult because metrics such as headcount, turnover, absenteeism, personnel costs, hiring time, training participation, and employee surveys often measure activity or conditions rather than organizational impact. People-related problems can have effects beyond individual employees, influencing team climate, conflict, productivity, customer relationships, and the retention of others, although not every dissatisfaction should necessarily be addressed. The People function therefore acts partly as an organizational mediation mechanism, bringing relevant employee concerns to leadership while also explaining constraints and decisions to employees without becoming exclusively the representative of either side. Its effectiveness should ultimately be assessed against the organization’s strategy: an innovation strategy requires the ability to attract, develop, and retain innovative capabilities; an efficiency strategy requires People mechanisms that reinforce efficient behavior; and a collaboration strategy requires promotion and leadership systems that develop effective teams rather than merely rewarding individual performance. Performance management should likewise reveal which behaviors are being rewarded and whether they are the behaviors the organization needs to multiply. Hiring, promotion, compensation, autonomy, and retention decisions all communicate what the organization considers valuable, shaping how others understand the requirements for success. People initiatives can therefore be treated as hypotheses: changing an incentive should imply an expected behavioral effect, a development program should aim to build a specific capability, and promotion criteria should be expected to change behavior in identifiable ways. This does not require turning every decision into a statistical experiment, but it does require connecting interventions to intended outcomes. At the same time, People cannot substitute for leadership: it can build systems, identify problems, provide data, and challenge decisions, but leadership must still make difficult organizational choices. The function is ultimately justified by whether it helps determine whether the company is building the organization its strategy requires and whether people-related problems are being addressed before they become business problems.

Culture is difficult to measure precisely because it emerges from the people present, repeated behaviors, organizational decisions, and reinforcement patterns over time, but it functions both as an execution mechanism and as a mechanism of belonging. People can perform their formal responsibilities without feeling meaningfully connected to the organization, while belonging can influence behaviors that exceed formal job descriptions, such as helping colleagues, sharing knowledge, taking responsibility for broader problems, supporting customers, and considering cross-functional consequences. People infer organizational values from who is hired, promoted, granted autonomy, recognized, heard, and used as an example, rather than from stated values alone. If collaboration is strategically important, collective objectives, team-oriented leadership, and recognition of knowledge-sharing can reinforce it until it becomes part of how the organization operates; conversely, individual competition, individual-performance promotion, and tolerance of harmful behavior can undermine collaboration regardless of formal messaging. Culture helps people make decisions in situations not covered by rules and processes, although it does not replace governance or controls. It is also difficult to change because changing narratives is easier than changing the hiring, promotion, compensation, leadership, and recognition patterns that sustain behavior. A culture that worked effectively at one stage may become less appropriate as the organization grows and requires greater coordination, specialization, or process. Culture is therefore not inherently good or bad; its suitability depends on organizational strategy and stage, and its most visible expression may appear precisely when formal rules do not determine what someone should do.

Marketing also participates in culture because it helps turn organizational experiences into shared stories, symbols, and references, even though it is not solely responsible for creating culture. Stories about founders, employees, customers, difficult projects, important decisions, and achievements communicate what the organization considers significant and help employees understand the organization they belong to. Celebrating long tenure, for example, can communicate that long-term careers are valued, although the relevant question is whether the organization is celebrating tenure itself or what the individual built during that period. Marketing can scale these stories by turning individual experiences into collective references, thereby reinforcing belonging and making behaviors more visible. Its effectiveness, however, depends on consistency with lived experience: communication cannot sustainably compensate for incentives, promotion systems, or decision-making structures that contradict the narrative. Marketing therefore functions as another cultural reinforcement mechanism alongside People, incentives, and leadership. People influences culture through who joins, grows, and remains; incentives influence it through economic rewards; leadership influences it through decisions and tolerated behaviors; and Marketing gives selected behaviors meaning, visibility, and scale. When these mechanisms reinforce one another, culture becomes stronger; when they communicate conflicting signals, employees must determine which signals actually represent what matters.

Partnership, People, incentives, culture, and Marketing can ultimately be understood as different mechanisms addressing the same question: how an organization turns strategy into behavior. Strategy becomes meaningful when it guides decisions, and decisions are made by people who respond to incentives, observe leadership, interpret rewards and consequences, develop career expectations, and form perceptions of what the organization truly values. The relevant question is therefore not simply whether a company has a good partnership program, People function, culture, or communication system, but whether these mechanisms point in the same direction. If strategy, compensation, leadership tolerance, promotion criteria, and communication reward different things, the resulting problem is fundamentally one of execution rather than belonging exclusively to HR, Marketing, or culture. Partnership remains compelling because, when appropriately designed, it makes explicit the relationship between value creation and participation in that value, but it is only one possible mechanism and may not be appropriate at every stage. The broader principle is that companies execute strategy through the people they hire, behaviors they incentivize, decisions they reward, leaders they promote, people they retain, and stories they tell. Observing these choices can reveal more about an organization than its stated values alone. Strategy is therefore not only a statement of where the company wants to go; it is also the system it builds so that people repeatedly make decisions that move the organization toward that destination, which is where strategy stops being a presentation and becomes an organization.
