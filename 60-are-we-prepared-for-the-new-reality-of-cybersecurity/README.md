# Are We Prepared for the New Reality of Cybersecurity?

_The evolution of artificial intelligence is changing the rules of the game. We need to rethink how we learn, discuss, and prepare to face digital threats._

**Summary**: The evolution of artificial intelligence is changing the dynamics of cyber threats, lowering costs and expanding attack capabilities, which calls for a new way of thinking about security. I argue that security can no longer remain restricted to specialized teams, but must become part of the decisions made by those who develop, manage, and use technology, both within organizations and in their personal lives. We also need to question whether our training, controls, and defense strategies are keeping pace with the speed of these transformations. I recognize that cybersecurity requires specialized knowledge that goes far beyond general experience in technology. That is why we need to create more opportunities for Cyber Security professionals to share their knowledge and help make security a more prominent consideration in everyone's decisions and daily lives.

---

Cybersecurity has never been more relevant. Attacks, data breaches, fraud, and service disruptions are becoming an increasingly common reality. But the evolution of artificial intelligence, especially agents capable of autonomously executing tasks, could significantly transform the dynamics of these threats.

AI is lowering costs and technical barriers for offensive activities. Target reconnaissance, vulnerability analysis, personalized social engineering, and operational automation may become more accessible and scalable. This does not mean that anyone will be able to break into any system, but it changes the economics of attacks and requires us to revisit some assumptions about exposure and protection.

Historically, individuals and small organizations could rely on a kind of statistical protection. Although they were already exposed to automated campaigns, fraud, and indiscriminate attacks, they did not always justify the investment required for a more targeted approach. With the ability to automate investigations and personalize attacks at scale, this logic may lose its strength. The cost of learning more about a target and preparing a specific approach is likely to decrease. We cannot assume we are safe simply because we are not important enough to attract someone's attention.

This change is not just another challenge for security teams. It should lead us to a broader discussion about how organizations and individuals understand, incorporate, and practice security.

## Security Cannot Be an Isolated Specialty

In another article, "[The Security Dilemma: Between Control and Speed](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/o-dilema-de-security-entre-o-controle)", I wrote about the need to bring security closer to business, architecture, and development decisions, rather than treating it merely as an approval stage or an area responsible for enforcing controls.

This reflection also needs to extend beyond organizational boundaries. Our personal and professional lives are increasingly connected. We use the same devices for different activities, store information across various services, and access corporate systems from environments that are not always under company control. A compromised personal account, an infected device, or an exposed credential can provide a pathway to corporate incidents.

There is an individual responsibility that cannot be transferred. Everyone needs to take care of their own credentials, access keys, documents, accounts, and devices. We cannot expect the company to be responsible for the security of a personal laptop or private account. This does not mean, however, shifting all responsibility to the user. Organizations and providers must also protect the environments, services, and information they make available. Security depends on shared responsibilities.

The point is that we cannot delegate our digital protection entirely. This does not require everyone to become an expert, but it does require developing enough knowledge to recognize risks and protect their own assets. Security needs to be part of everyday decisions, not just corporate processes.

## Do Our Training Programs Still Make Sense?

Many organizations still treat security awareness as a periodic activity based on presentations, videos, and questionnaires. We learn to identify phishing, create strong passwords, avoid suspicious links, and never share credentials. These guidelines remain valid, but are they enough in the face of continuously evolving threats?

An annual training session may fulfill a compliance requirement without necessarily preparing someone to recognize new situations or make decisions when faced with a real risk. The problem is not the basic content itself, but believing that it alone is sufficient to address such a dynamic environment.

We need to move toward continuous learning, with practical demonstrations, simulations, recurring exercises, and updated content. Rather than simply repeating recommendations, it is important to understand how attacks happen, what warning signs to look for, and how to respond when something unexpected occurs. But awareness alone does not solve the problem either. Processes need to be designed to encourage secure behavior, make it possible to report suspicious situations, and prevent productivity pressures from encouraging dangerous shortcuts.

Training must also extend to personal life. Protecting our data and devices cannot depend exclusively on the training provided by our employers.

## The Speed of Innovation Also Expands Our Attack Surface

The rapid adoption of artificial intelligence brings new possibilities, but also raises important security questions. AI agents, MCPs (Model Context Protocol), and other integrations may be granted permissions to access data, execute actions, and interact with corporate systems. The ease of connecting tools and automating tasks expands the possibilities for their use, but also creates new trust relationships and new exposure points.

How are these integrations assessed? Who can authorize an agent? How are its permissions defined and monitored? How can we identify unexpected behavior and revoke access when a credential is compromised or an integration presents a risk? And how can we ensure that the instructions received by an agent do not lead it to perform actions that are incompatible with the permissions granted? These decisions involve more than access control. They require attention to identity, authorization, the origin of instructions, execution context, and the actions each agent is allowed to perform. The speed at which these technologies are being adopted makes it even more important to establish clear processes for making their integrations available, authorizing them, monitoring them, and revoking them.

This evolution also highlights an asymmetry between attackers and defenders. Attackers can use artificial intelligence to automate activities, scale operations, and reduce costs. On the other side, organizations need to protect complex environments, legacy systems, distributed processes, and countless integrations. Are our defense strategies evolving at the same pace? Are we managing to anticipate new threats, or are we still focusing our efforts on reacting after incidents occur?

The software supply chain represents another challenge. Dependencies, libraries, frameworks, services, and vendors are present in virtually every modern solution. The compromise of a single component can affect countless applications and organizations, creating a cascading effect that extends beyond those directly impacted.

In another article, "[Collaborative Cyber Emergency and Incident Response Network](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/rede-colaborativa-de-emergencia-cibernetica)", I proposed a discussion about collaboration between organizations during incidents and alert mechanisms, such as panic buttons in supply chains, to quickly stop the spread of a compromise. This ability to collaborate is particularly important because not every incident can be prevented. Rapidly detecting an intrusion, limiting its spread, restoring services, and sharing relevant information are activities just as important as preventive controls.

As innovation accelerates, we need to ensure that risk assessments and our response capabilities do not fall behind.

## Overconfidence Is Also a Vulnerability

Multi-factor authentication, antivirus software, vulnerability scanners, vendor approval processes, and access controls are important resources. But none of them offers absolute protection.

A recurring risk is confusing the existence of controls with a guarantee of security. Implementing a solution, following a process, or receiving approval does not mean that all risks have been eliminated. Excessive confidence in protective mechanisms can create a false sense of security and reduce our willingness to question decisions and revisit assumptions.

Controls need to be reviewed, access rights reassessed, and processes tested. The environment changes, threats evolve, and what was adequate yesterday may not be sufficient tomorrow.

This does not mean creating barriers that make work impossible. Security must be a priority, especially when significant financial losses, sensitive data, and essential services are at stake. But it needs to be embedded in architecture and processes in a way that makes protection and productivity complementary. Excessively bureaucratic controls can encourage shortcuts and shadow solutions, creating precisely the risks they were supposed to prevent. The challenge is to build environments in which working securely is a natural part of operations, rather than an obstacle to be circumvented.

## We Need to Learn from Those Who Understand Security

I have approximately 15 years of experience in technology, participating in decisions involving architecture, systems, integrations, infrastructure, and digital products. Even so, the more I observe the evolution of threats, the more evident the gap becomes between knowing technology and having a deep understanding of security. It is a discipline that requires specialization, continuous learning, and the ability to anticipate risks that are not always apparent to those working in other fields.

That is why I believe we need to bring the knowledge of Cyber Security professionals closer to everyone who designs, develops, manages, and uses technology. Not to turn everyone into an expert, but to enable us to make more informed decisions and better understand the risks involved.

I am also writing as someone who wants to learn. I would like to see more professionals in this field sharing their experiences, explaining the challenges, and helping broaden this discussion. There is a great deal of knowledge that needs to move beyond specialized circles and reach those who make decisions every day.

We need to talk more about security. Much more.
