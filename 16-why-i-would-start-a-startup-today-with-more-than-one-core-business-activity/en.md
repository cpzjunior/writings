# Why I Would Start a Startup With More Than One Core Activity Today

_Diversifying businesses can be an innovation strategy, not just a way to reduce risks._

**Summary:** I would start a startup with more than one core activity today because I believe focus does not mean doing only one thing, but knowing where to concentrate resources while keeping other possibilities open. One core activity can financially support the others, while people, technology, and knowledge circulate among them, creating new opportunities and learning. To me, diversification can be less about protection against risks and more about a deliberate strategy of experimentation and discovery.

---

For four and a half years, I had a second workday. In addition to my professional career in technology, I was the founder and volunteer president of a social marketing NGO called Se Doar. The organization maintained a free online platform that connected NGOs, institutions, and social projects with people and companies willing to contribute through financial donations, food, clothing, or volunteer work.

The experience confronted me with a problem I had not considered in that way before. It is relatively simple to imagine that a social organization can grow as it finds more people willing to contribute. In practice, however, there is an important difference between having volunteers and taking on permanent commitments. Because we were a relatively new organization, we did not have sponsorship and relied on few donors. Our revenue was limited and unpredictable, which made it risky to hire employees even when there were activities that would clearly benefit from paid work.

For that reason, we chose volunteer work from the beginning. The model worked, but it had a structural limitation: each person had to balance the project with their own life. Volunteers could be extremely committed and still reach a point when other commitments began to take up their time. The organization's continuity depended, to a great extent, on the ability of some people to continue finding room for it.

I experienced this problem myself. To keep the organization running, I had to fit volunteer work in after the work that actually paid my bills. By March 2024, my health no longer allowed me to sustain that second workday and, since there was no successor willing to take over the presidency, I decided to shut down the NGO's activities.

The organization did not end because its mission had lost relevance or because the project had stopped working. It ended because that structure depended on an availability of time that I could no longer provide. It was a personal experience, but one that left me with a broader question about sustainability: why must an activity necessarily be able to sustain itself independently in order to exist?

Maybe it doesn't. Maybe an organization can have a core activity responsible for its financial sustainability while also maintaining other activities with different objectives. They can generate revenue, develop intellectual property, produce knowledge, generate social impact, or simply create room for experimentation. What matters is that there is a rationale for keeping them together and that the core activity is strong enough to support the structure. That is the idea that would make me start a startup with more than one core activity today.

## Focus Does Not Mean Doing Only One Thing

There is a fairly strong premise in the startup world that a company needs to choose one core activity and concentrate all its efforts on it. In many cases, that makes perfect sense. A small company has few resources and needs focus to survive.

But focus and concentration are not the same thing. Focus means knowing where to put most of your resources, not necessarily directing all of them toward a single activity. A company can have more than one core activity and still have a very clear priority.

It is possible to dedicate most of the people, capital, and time to the activity that sustains the organization while reserving a smaller portion of the company's capacity to explore other opportunities. These activities can have different objectives: generating revenue, developing intellectual property, producing knowledge, generating social impact, or simply creating room for experimentation.

This changes how we view the company itself. Instead of an organization built exclusively to maximize the return of a single product or market, it becomes a structure capable of maintaining different bets, with different levels of risk and different return horizons.

The challenge, therefore, is not necessarily choosing a single activity. It is knowing which one should receive most of the attention and how much room can be allocated to the others without compromising the core business. That is the structure I would like to experiment with.

## One Company, Multiple Core Activities

If I were starting a company today, I imagine an organization with three major divisions: Corporate, Entertainment, and Social. Not because I consider this combination universally correct, but because it brings together three interests and objectives that I would like to reconcile within the same structure.

Corporate would be the organization's economic core, responsible for developing management products, automation, and tailored solutions for companies. It would also be the division responsible for generating most of the revenue and providing the predictability needed to keep the company healthy. If the goal is to allow other activities to exist without depending on a second workday from people or on uncertain revenue, it is first necessary to build an economic foundation capable of sustaining that freedom.

Entertainment would focus on games, animation, and digital experiences. It is an area I have been interested in since I was a child and that, in a way, also influenced my decision to study Computer Science. At the same time, it is a highly uncertain industry. A company can spend years developing a game and discover that it has not found its audience. A launch delay can consume available cash, and a change in market expectations can turn a promising project into a financial problem. That does not diminish my interest in the sector. On the contrary, it is precisely because I recognize its potential that I would like to work in it. But I would not want the survival of the entire company to depend exclusively on the success of a game.

The third area would be Social, focused on social and human development through technology. The idea would be to support NGOs, institutes, foundations, and community projects with technology and knowledge, while also creating opportunities for professionals in training to participate in real projects and develop their skills. In this model, a social project would not need to justify its existence exclusively through revenue. It could exist because the company decided to allocate part of its capacity to a social purpose, just as it could decide to invest in new intellectual property or experimental technology.

These three divisions are simply the structure that makes sense to me. Someone else could arrive at a completely different combination. It could be education, software, and media. It could be industry, technology, and research. It could even be a combination that I cannot imagine today. What matters is not which activities are chosen, but whether there is a reason for them to coexist within the same organization and enough clarity to define which one should receive most of the resources.

In my case, Corporate would initially be the center of gravity, while Entertainment and Social would receive a smaller share of the company's capacity. Over time, however, this distribution could change. An activity that started as a secondary bet could become relevant enough to receive more people, capital, and attention. The goal would not be to keep three divisions artificially balanced, but to allow the organization itself to discover, over time, which activities have the potential to grow.

Ultimately, Corporate, Entertainment, and Social are just the configuration I would choose. The principle is broader: a company can have multiple core activities without necessarily losing its identity or focus. The question becomes how to distribute resources among them and, above all, what one can offer the others.

## Diversification Requires Focus

This does not mean that I would start a company trying to divide my efforts equally among three businesses. On the contrary. If the idea is to have more than one core activity, it is necessary to be even more disciplined about resource allocation. In the beginning, I imagine something close to an 80/20 Pareto distribution: approximately 80% of the company's efforts would be concentrated on Corporate, while the other 20% would be distributed between Entertainment and Social.

Corporate would be the flagship because we need a predictable economic foundation. This activity would support salaries, infrastructure, and the other projects. The other 20% would function as a deliberate space for experimentation. A game could be developed without having to pay for itself from day one. A social initiative could exist without immediately needing to find a monetization model. A new technology could be explored before there was clear demand for it.

The difference from a company that simply diversifies without criteria lies precisely in this asymmetry. It is not about doing three things equally, but about establishing a center of gravity and preserving some room to discover what may emerge outside it. Diversification only makes sense when there is clarity about which activity sustains the organization and how much of the company's capacity can be allocated to the others without compromising that foundation.

I also do not see 80/20 as a permanent rule. An activity that starts by receiving 10% of the resources may demonstrate much greater potential over time. Entertainment may develop intellectual property that justifies new investments. Social may find ways to significantly expand its reach. A new opportunity may emerge in any of the areas. As the company learns, the distribution of people, capital, and attention can change.

Perhaps, after a few years, the three divisions will be more balanced. Perhaps one of them will disappear. Perhaps another will become dominant. What matters is that this balance not be imposed from the beginning, but emerge as a consequence of what the organization itself discovers.

There is, of course, a real risk in this strategy: trying to do everything and ending up doing nothing well. A small startup has limited resources, and if they are spread too thin, none of the initiatives will have enough capacity to achieve scale. This is a legitimate argument against diversification, but not necessarily against having multiple activities. The problem is not having several activities. It is distributing resources among them without a clear rationale.

A company can say: Corporate is our priority. That is where we will put most of our people, capital, and attention. At the same time, we will reserve a limited portion of our capacity for other activities that we consider strategic. That is also focus.

The risk is not having several activities, but ending up without any activity strong enough to sustain the others. A company can operate across different fronts and still have a very clear center of gravity. What defines focus is not the number of activities, but the clarity of priorities.

## Circulation of Resources and People

There is an even more important reason to keep these activities within the same company. They do not need to share only money. Financial resources, technology, intellectual property, and knowledge can circulate among the divisions, while people can move between different projects and contexts, carrying with them the experiences accumulated in each of them.

Imagine a professional who starts working in Corporate and, after a few years, decides to participate in an Entertainment project. They could bring with them what they learned developing products for corporate clients, from software architecture and product management to user experience and understanding customer needs. The reverse movement would also be possible. Someone who spent years working on games could bring a different perspective to Corporate on interface, interaction, design, engagement, and user experience.

The same principle could work in the Social division. A professional involved in a project for an NGO could gain a deep understanding of a problem they would be unlikely to encounter in a corporate product. This experience could later influence the way they think about other products and projects within the company. Likewise, technologies and practices developed in the other divisions could be used to solve problems in the nonprofit sector.

In this model, the divisions would not be isolated boxes within the same company. They would be different environments connected by a common structure. Knowledge produced in one activity could be applied to another, technologies could be reused, and professionals could change contexts without necessarily having to leave the organization.

This would require more than a formal mobility policy. It would require a culture that understands careers in a less linear way. I do not want someone to be hired as “a Corporate person” and necessarily remain there throughout their entire career. I want the organization to offer different environments in which people can apply and develop their skills, including allowing them to move between divisions when there is interest and opportunity.

The company, in this case, begins to accumulate something beyond financial capital. It accumulates knowledge, intellectual property, experience, and different repertoires. The greater the circulation of these elements among the activities, the greater the number of unexpected connections that may generate new products, technologies, and solutions.

## Diversification as an Innovation Strategy

This is where the thesis stops being simply a risk-reduction strategy. If Corporate generates revenue and uses part of that money to finance a game, there is diversification, but the relationship between the two activities is still essentially financial. The model becomes much more interesting when there is exchange in both directions and each activity increases the capabilities of the others.

A technology developed for Entertainment could improve a Corporate product. An experience acquired in a Social project could reveal a need that results in a new product. A tool developed for a corporate client could reduce the cost or time required to produce an entertainment project. The value lies not only in keeping different businesses within the same company, but in allowing something developed in one context to find unexpected applications in another.

People also carry this knowledge. A professional who has moved through different areas begins to see problems from different perspectives. A technique that seemed specific to one industry may find a completely different application in another. An experience acquired in a social project may change the way someone thinks about a corporate product, just as a solution developed for a client can be adapted for a nonprofit organization.

In this sense, multiple activities can function as different experimentation environments within the same organization. Each division has its own problems, constraints, and users, but the knowledge produced in each of them does not need to remain confined to the context in which it was originally developed. The greater the circulation among these environments, the greater the number of combinations the company can explore.

That is why, to me, the main justification for having multiple activities is not simply reducing dependence on a single source of revenue. That is an important benefit, but it is only the first level. The more interesting goal is to create an organization in which different activities function as sources of learning for one another, increasing the ability to experiment, learn, and transform knowledge into new opportunities.

In this model, diversification stops being merely protection against the failure of a specific business. It becomes a way to expand the set of possibilities the organization can explore.

## For a Startup, Experimentation Can Be Part of the Strategy

For an established company, having multiple activities is a different strategic decision. It may make sense to separate businesses into subsidiaries, create a holding company, or simply concentrate resources on whatever delivers the greatest return. At that stage, the organization already has capital, an operating history, and enough information to make more precise decisions about where to allocate its resources.

A startup is in a different situation. It is still trying to prove its business thesis. It does not necessarily know which product will have the greatest demand, which market will be most promising, or which revenue model will be able to sustain its growth. The risk of betting everything on a single direction is therefore particularly high.

It is in this context that I see value in maintaining more than one activity. This does not mean abandoning focus or turning the startup into a conglomerate from day one. It means preserving some options while the company is still discovering where the greatest potential lies, keeping investments proportional to the degree of confidence in each one.

This can also change the way we think about pivoting. Normally, a pivot is presented as a change of direction: the company realizes that its original hypothesis does not work and begins pursuing another. In a structure with multiple activities, some of these hypotheses could be explored simultaneously, albeit with very different levels of investment. One may gain traction, another may remain small, and a third may simply not work.

In this scenario, pivoting does not have to mean starting again from scratch. The company can increase the allocation of resources to an activity that has demonstrated greater potential and reduce or shut down another. People, technology, knowledge, and infrastructure accumulated in one activity can continue to be useful in another, preserving part of the value built even when the original hypothesis does not hold.

The Pareto distribution mentioned earlier takes on an additional function here. The 20% that is not dedicated to the core business does not need to be seen as waste or distraction. It can represent the space the startup reserves to discover what could become its next core business. If none of these bets work, the company has still preserved its economic foundation. If one of them demonstrates potential, there is an opportunity to gradually increase its share of the resources.

This is, to me, an important distinction between diversification and lack of focus. Lack of focus is not knowing where to put resources. Diversification is deliberately deciding that a portion of resources will be used to explore different possibilities.

And for a company that is still trying to discover its best opportunity, it may be more rational to preserve some possibilities than to close every door before even knowing where they lead.

## What Other Companies Can Teach Us

The idea of a company having multiple sources of revenue and operating across different activities is not exactly new. Throughout their histories, different organizations have expanded their operations far beyond the activity that originally defined their businesses. Nintendo, for example, combines hardware, software, digital services, and intellectual property exploitation. Microsoft operates simultaneously in productivity, cloud computing, advertising, devices, and gaming. Amazon started in e-commerce and later built significant businesses in cloud computing, advertising, and entertainment.

I am not using these companies as models to replicate. The scale, structure, and markets in which they operate are completely different from those of a startup in its early years. What interests me about these cases is seeing that there are different ways to combine activities within the same organization and that this combination can be part of the company's own strategy.

The relationship among these activities can also take very different forms. In some cases, one division finances another. In others, there is shared technology, intellectual property, distribution, or knowledge. There are also situations in which one activity creates a platform that enables new sources of revenue to emerge. There is no single architecture for a company with multiple activities. What matters is having a rationale that justifies their coexistence.

This observation is particularly interesting when we think about a startup. An established company can decide, according to its strategy, to separate businesses, create subsidiaries, or concentrate its resources on a particular activity. A startup is in a different situation: it is still trying to discover which combination of product, market, and business model can sustain its growth.

For this reason, experimenting with more than one activity can be not only a form of diversification, but also a discovery strategy. If one of them demonstrates greater traction, the company can gradually increase the resources allocated to it. If another does not work, it can be reduced or shut down. During this process, knowledge, technology, intellectual property, and experience accumulated in one activity can continue to be leveraged by the others.

## When Integration Does Not Make Sense

There is, however, a limit to this idea. Having multiple activities within the same company is not automatically better than separating them. Integration only makes sense when there is a real benefit to keeping these activities together.

If two divisions have completely different customers, technologies, processes, and capabilities, the cost of coordinating them may outweigh any synergy they could generate. People may end up being allocated across projects merely to justify the structure, technologies may be standardized unnecessarily, and decisions may begin competing for resources that should be concentrated within each business.

In that case, perhaps the best solution is to separate the operations. They can continue to belong to the same economic group, but they do not necessarily need to share the same operating structure. The fact that two activities belong to the same group does not mean they need to function as a single organization.

Therefore, I do not see multiple activities as an absolute principle. The question I would ask before placing two activities within the same company would be simple: is there any real advantage to having them together?

If the answer is simply “because I want to diversify,” that is probably not enough. If there is sharing of people, technology, knowledge, intellectual property, infrastructure, or distribution channels, the answer may be different.

The thesis, therefore, is not that a company should do several things. It is that a company does not need to limit itself to a single activity when there are good reasons to do more than one. And those reasons need to be greater than the additional complexity that this choice creates.

## Focus Does Not Mean Having a Single Direction

My experience with Se Doar taught me that I do not want to depend on a second workday to build something I consider important. My professional experience in technology showed me the value of building products with economic sustainability. And my interest in games reminds me that some of the most interesting things we can do are also those that involve greater uncertainty. I do not necessarily want to choose between these things.

If I start a company today, I want to build an organization in which one activity can support another, but also one in which one activity can teach another. I want people to be able to move between different areas, technologies to be reusable, and knowledge to cross boundaries that would normally exist between different companies.

Corporate would be my starting point because we need focus and predictability. Entertainment and Social would initially receive a smaller share of the resources, preserving room for experimentation without compromising the organization's sustainability. Over time, this proportion could change. One of these activities could grow, another could remain small or even cease to exist. That would be part of the discovery process.

But this is simply the structure that makes sense to me. Someone else could choose completely different activities. They could have education, software, and media, for example, or any other combination that presented a logic of coexistence and synergy. The principle does not depend on the activities chosen, but on having a reason to keep them together and a clear priority among them.

In the end, the point is not to advocate Corporate, Entertainment, and Social as a universal model. It is to question the idea that a company necessarily needs to have a single core activity in order to have focus.

To me, focus means knowing what the priority is, not necessarily choosing a single direction. A company can have a main path and, at the same time, keep other possibilities open. As long as it knows why they exist, how much it is willing to invest in them, and what it can learn from them, perhaps doing more than one thing is not a distraction. Perhaps it is precisely a different way of discovering what the company can become.
