There is a widely admired distinction between resilience and persistence: the ability to endure difficulty is often treated as evidence of determination and maturity, but enduring something for too long can become insistence rather than resilience. Extreme examples such as China’s 996 work culture and Japan’s concept of karōshi illustrate that resilience should not be equated with tolerating unsustainable conditions, particularly when leaving may be more rational than learning to endure them. This raises practical questions about when to continue, when to change strategy, how to interpret unfavorable feedback, and how to recognize when rising costs have made persistence harmful. The discussion approaches these questions through the author’s experiences as a solutions architect, project manager, and entrepreneur, focusing on decisions, signals, limits, incomplete information, pressure, dependency, and concentrated responsibility rather than psychological explanations.

Persistence differs from insistence because pursuing an objective does not require indefinitely repeating the same strategy. Senna and Churchill illustrate different forms of perseverance: both pursued important objectives, but perseverance could involve adapting preparation or circumstances rather than mechanically repeating an approach. Investment of time, money, reputation, and energy can cause people to conflate an objective with the strategy chosen to achieve it, even though the two are separate hypotheses: a valid problem can have a bad product, a legitimate project can have poor implementation, and a sound architectural decision can later become a constraint. Conversely, changing direction too quickly can prevent a strategy from being adequately tested, since some results require time and repetition. Persistence therefore means continuing to pursue what matters while incorporating learning, which may require insisting, changing tactics, seeking help, reducing scope, or abandoning a particular path rather than the underlying objective.

Calibration concerns the difficulty of maintaining an accurate view of one’s own abilities between overconfidence and excessive self-doubt. Successful outcomes can create overconfidence because people may mistake favorable results for proof that their decisions or assumptions were correct, even though outcomes can be influenced by circumstances; conversely, repeated failures can lead competent people to distrust their own judgment, demand excessive evidence, or interpret difficulties as evidence of incompetence. Experience provides information but does not eliminate the possibility of misinterpreting that information. Both extremes distort decision-making: overconfidence can encourage poorly understood risks, while insufficient confidence can prevent manageable ones. The objective is therefore not to eliminate fluctuations in confidence but to recognize when they have become disconnected from the available evidence and to distinguish normal uncertainty from evidence that genuinely warrants changing position.

Feedback can amplify existing confidence or insecurity because external information is interpreted through the recipient’s current view of themselves and their decisions. Negative feedback can feel personal when someone has invested substantial effort in an idea, causing criticism of a project to become interpreted as criticism of personal capability; positive feedback can similarly reinforce excessive confidence and make decisions appear more sound than they were. Feedback is useful not simply because it is positive or negative but because of the information it contains: rejection may reflect an invalid problem, an unsuitable audience, poor timing, or weak execution, while architectural criticism may reveal a genuine weakness, an unknown constraint, or merely a preference. Like architectural diagnosis, feedback should therefore be interpreted through behavior, constraints, dependencies, history, and context rather than through isolated signals. Separating criticism of an attempt from judgment of the person is important, but this distinction should not become a defense against repeated evidence: when similar criticisms emerge independently across contexts, dismissing them all as subjective may simply be a sophisticated way of ignoring the learning they provide.

Past investment can make persistence harder to evaluate because previous choices create financial, technical, organizational, and personal forms of debt. Technical debt is an architectural analogy for decisions that accelerate delivery while creating future constraints, and similar effects occur when projects persist for years, organizations depend excessively on one person, or responsibilities accumulate because someone is repeatedly the person who solves them. Past investment should not determine future investment merely because it already occurred; the relevant question is what remains worth investing from the present forward. Imagining that nothing had yet been invested can help separate future value from sunk cost, while still recognizing that past investment may have generated knowledge, relationships, assets, or learning. The author’s experience shutting down an NGO after four and a half years illustrates the distinction: the mission remained meaningful, but sustaining it alongside a professional career required an increasingly unsustainable second shift, and in March 2024 the activities were closed because the personal cost had become too high. The decision did not invalidate the mission or the original decision to participate, and it reinforced the idea that a reasonable decision made under earlier information can later cease to make sense without having been a mistake. The relevant calculation is whether the next unit of money, time, energy, or responsibility still contributes to the objective or merely increases exposure.

Burnout and unsustainability can also result from concentration of responsibility rather than simply excessive workload. Qualitative research on RPG communities describing “game master burnout” illustrates how a voluntary and enjoyable activity can consume personal resources when one person prepares sessions, manages rules, organizes the group, guides the story, and resolves conflicts; the example does not establish a clinical cause or claim that being a game master inherently produces exhaustion, but it illustrates the structural problem of a single point of failure. Similar patterns occur when an architect is the only person who understands a system, a manager concentrates decisions, an entrepreneur performs multiple critical functions, or one family member carries income, care, organization, or decision-making responsibilities. Such systems may function for years without appearing fragile until the critical person needs to stop. Being valuable means contributing significantly, whereas being indispensable may indicate insufficient redundancy, and excessive concentration can also create reciprocal psychological or identity dependency in which the person becomes dependent on the role that depends on them. Distributing responsibilities, knowledge, attention, and sources of meaning can therefore increase resilience for both organizations and individuals; what appears to be exceptional commitment may sometimes reflect poor responsibility architecture rather than exceptional resilience.

Systems can actively encourage self-destructive persistence by rewarding people who compensate for structural deficiencies through additional effort. Organizations may praise individuals who solve everything, accept responsibilities beyond their roles, remain constantly available, or work excessive hours, because these behaviors produce short-term results while reducing the immediate pressure to repair broken processes or distribute knowledge. Founders can similarly compensate for immature organizational structures by accumulating functions and using personal effort to sustain a company, creating a dependency that persists as the organization grows. Personal projects and family structures can develop the same feedback loop: one person takes over responsibilities because it is initially easier, others become dependent on them, stopping becomes more difficult, and additional responsibilities become concentrated on the same person. Because successful problem-solving generates recognition, this pattern can resemble competence while actually reinforcing structural fragility. Eventually, persistence may conceal rather than solve the underlying problem, making documentation, delegation, knowledge distribution, boundaries, and allowing system deficiencies to become visible more valuable than continued individual compensation. A sustainable system should not require one person’s exhaustion to function.

External observers and people inside a situation possess different but complementary forms of information. The person directly involved knows variables an outsider cannot fully access, including physical capacity, recovery, responsibilities, risk tolerance, changing circumstances, and details that are difficult to quantify; therefore, outside judgments can oversimplify decisions that make sense given information unavailable to the observer. At the same time, proximity can make important patterns invisible: emotional attachment may obscure excessive identity investment, unsustainable workloads can become normalized, and repeated behaviors can become difficult to recognize from within. An outsider may notice that someone talks about nothing else, that decisions continually return to them, or that responsibility remains concentrated, even without understanding all the contextual reasons behind those patterns. External perspectives are themselves limited because they can identify patterns without understanding their causes or consequences. A better assessment combines the inside perspective, which understands the lived constraints, with the outside perspective, which can reveal patterns obscured by proximity, especially when the person’s ability to assess their own limits has deteriorated.

There is no universal point at which persistence becomes self-destruction, because the boundary depends on circumstances and changes over time. A useful question is whether, given the information available today, one would make the same decision again, although answering no does not automatically mean the original decision was wrong: circumstances may have changed, or a previously reasonable choice may have ceased to make sense. Decisions do not need to be defended indefinitely merely to prove that they were correct; they can be acknowledged as reasonable under earlier conditions, evaluated through what happened, and revised when present conditions justify it. Continuing is not synonymous with persistence, and stopping is not synonymous with giving up; sometimes persistence means changing the way forward, while sometimes it means ending the activity. The central lesson is that past decisions should not become debts that force future behavior: a choice can have been valid for a period and later become unsustainable without constituting failure, because people, circumstances, and objectives can change.
