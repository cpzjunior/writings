# The Fine Line Between Persistence and Self-Destruction

_On difficult decisions, feedback, limits, and the moment when persistence stops making sense._

**Summary:** There comes a point when persistence stops being a virtue and becomes mere insistence, but that boundary is rarely clear. I learned this when I had to shut down, after four and a half years, the NGO I voluntarily chaired, not because its mission had stopped making sense, but because the cost of sustaining it had become too high. The experience made me rethink how we deal with feedback, rising costs, responsibilities, and past decisions. Continuing to pursue a goal does not mean insisting on the same strategy, and recognizing that one way of continuing has become unsustainable does not turn the previous decision into a mistake.

---

There is a quality that tends to be admired in professionals, entrepreneurs, and managers: the ability not to give up. Persisting in the face of a problem, continuing after a rejection, or getting through a difficult period are behaviors associated with resilience, determination, and maturity. I used to see persistence that way as well. Over time, however, I began to realize that there is an important difference between being able to get through a difficulty and simply enduring it for too long. At some point, continuing can stop being a choice and become mere insistence. And what began as commitment can end up exacting too high a price from the person who persists.

This difference becomes clearer when we look at environments in which pressure is treated as a normal part of the culture. In China, the so-called 996 describes a work routine from 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week. In Japan, karōshi is the term associated with deaths related to overwork. These are extreme examples, but they help distinguish resilience from simply being able to endure suffering. Resilience should not mean working beyond what the body and mind can sustain. And when the environment itself is toxic, perhaps the most rational decision is not to find a way to endure it better, but to leave it.

It was from this boundary that I began asking myself a few questions: how do we know when it is worth continuing and when we are merely insisting? How do we distinguish an execution problem from a flawed strategy? How much weight should we give an unfavorable assessment before changing direction? And, when costs begin to rise, how do we realize that persistence has stopped serving the objective and started exacting too high a price from the person who persists?

These are questions I intend to explore based on my experience as a solutions architect, project manager, and entrepreneur, but also based on some decisions I made that, looking back, taught me more about limits than I would have liked. I have no background in psychology and do not intend to explain these phenomena from that perspective. My interest lies in the decisions, signals, and structures that influence this boundary, especially when incomplete information, pressure, dependency, and concentrated responsibility make it harder to recognize when it is time to change strategy, reduce the load, or stop.

To begin, it is worth separating two things that are often treated as though they were the same: persisting in a goal and insisting on the chosen way of achieving it.

## Persistence Is Not Insistence

There is a fundamental difference between continuing to pursue a goal and continuing to execute the same strategy. The first can be persistence. The second can simply be stubbornness.

This distinction revisits an idea I have already explored when writing about the resilience of Senna and Churchill, two very different examples of perseverance. Senna had an unusual ability to pursue excellence without ceasing to adjust his preparation and approach to circumstances. Churchill, in turn, became a symbol of resistance by maintaining a position in the face of an extreme situation, but that did not mean indefinitely repeating the same strategy. I write more about this perspective in “[What Senna and Churchill Can Teach Us About Resilience](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/o-que-senna-e-churchill-podem-nos)“.

In practice, this distinction is difficult. When we invest time, money, reputation, and energy in something, we tend to create a connection between what we want to achieve and the way we have chosen to get there. If we believe that a particular product solves an important problem, it is easy to start defending the way we decided to build it as well. The same can happen with an architecture or an initiative: a choice that should have been a means to achieve a particular outcome starts being treated as an inseparable part of the objective itself.

Objective and strategy are different hypotheses. A company can be right about the problem it wants to solve and wrong about the product. A project can have a legitimate purpose and an inadequate implementation. An architectural decision can correctly solve today's problem and become tomorrow's constraint. In all these cases, abandoning the strategy does not necessarily mean abandoning the objective.

The opposite mistake also exists. Changing strategy too early can prevent a hypothesis from being properly tested. Not every difficulty indicates that the path is wrong. Some results take time to appear, some decisions require repetition before they produce learning, and some strategies can only be evaluated after a consistent period of execution. If every frustration is interpreted as a reason to change direction, we may confuse adaptation with inconsistency.

Persistence, therefore, does not mean repeating the same attempt with greater intensity. It means continuing to pursue what we consider important while incorporating what we learn along the way. At times this requires insisting. At others, changing our approach, seeking help, reducing scope, or recognizing that a particular path no longer makes sense.

## The Problem of Calibration

Between overconfidence and imposter syndrome there is a kind of metronome. Oscillation is part of it. Our perception of our own abilities changes according to circumstances, results, and experiences. The problem arises when this oscillation repeatedly moves from one extreme to the other or when the metronome seems to get stuck at one end.

Overconfidence can grow after a sequence of good results. When a decision works, it is tempting to conclude that our assumptions were correct. But outcome and decision quality are not the same thing. We can make a poor decision and obtain a good result because of a favorable combination of circumstances. We can also make a reasonable decision and obtain a poor result because an external variable changed.

Experience can produce learning, but it can also create illusions. An entrepreneur who gets things right several times may begin to believe they can predict the market better than they actually can. An architect who has spent years without facing a particular type of incident may underestimate that risk. A manager who has repeatedly delivered projects under adverse conditions may begin to consider those conditions normal.

The opposite movement also happens. After making a few mistakes, a competent person may begin systematically distrusting their own decisions. They may demand too much evidence before acting, interpret a difficulty as proof of incompetence, or attribute their successes to luck. Imposter syndrome is a psychological phenomenon that I do not intend to explain here, but there is a practical question behind it: the confidence we place in our abilities can drift away from what our actual experience supports.

I recognize this movement because confidence and doubt do not simply disappear because we accumulate experience. We can spend years building things, making decisions, and solving problems and still go through periods in which some poor results seem to say much more about our abilities than they actually do. Experience helps, but it does not make us immune to poor interpretations of our own experience.

At both extremes, we lose the ability to use what we have learned to guide our next decisions. Overconfidence can lead us to take risks we do not understand. Lack of confidence can make us avoid risks we would be capable of managing. The goal is not to eliminate this oscillation, but to notice when it is distorting our reading of reality and when the evidence genuinely justifies changing our position.

The problem is not that the metronome moves. It is losing the ability to notice when it is out of rhythm.

## Feedback as an Amplifier

If calibration concerns how we interpret our own abilities, feedback functions as an amplifier. It brings information from outside, but it can also increase the confidence of someone who is already confident or deepen the doubt of someone who is already insecure.

Receiving negative feedback is one of the hardest parts of building something. When someone rejects a product, criticizes an idea, or questions a decision in which we invested time and energy, it is difficult not to take the criticism personally. For entrepreneurs, this can be even more intense. After so many hours devoted to an idea, rejection of the product can easily feel like a rejection of one's own ability to build it.

I have encountered this effect myself. There is a difference between hearing that an idea does not work and interpreting that as evidence that perhaps you are not capable of making it work. The first is information about the project. The second begins to affect how you see yourself and, from there, the decisions you make.

The same mechanism can work in the opposite direction. A sequence of positive results can reinforce already-high confidence and make our decisions seem better than they actually are. Positive feedback also needs to be interpreted. A favorable result can confirm a hypothesis, but it can equally be the consequence of specific circumstances, a particularly receptive audience, or simply luck.

Therefore, the value of feedback lies not only in whether it is positive or negative, but in the information it contains. A customer may reject a product because the solution does not address a relevant problem. But it may also be the wrong audience, the wrong timing, or poor execution. A criticism of an architecture may reveal a real weakness, but it may also reflect a constraint we were unaware of or a personal preference.

In architecture, I am accustomed to dealing with this kind of uncertainty. An isolated metric rarely determines a decision. We observe behavior, constraints, dependencies, history, and context before concluding that there is a problem and deciding what to do about it. Feedback should be treated similarly.

This requires separating criticism of what we are doing from an assessment of who we are. An attempt can be wrong without us being incapable. A rejection can be information about that attempt, not a verdict on the next ones. At the same time, this separation cannot serve as protection against any unfavorable evidence. When similar criticisms appear repeatedly, coming from different people and in different contexts, continuing to treat everything as opinion can simply be a sophisticated way of ignoring what we are learning.

Feedback, therefore, should not by itself determine whether we continue or stop. It needs to be incorporated into our understanding of the problem. It can confirm a perception, contradict it, or reveal something we had not yet considered. And when we are more confident or more insecure than the evidence warrants, that same feedback can move the metronome even further out of rhythm.

## When Past Costs Begin to Drive the Future

There is another element that makes this boundary particularly difficult: the weight of choices we have already made. In architecture, we talk quite a bit about technical debt. A decision made to deliver faster can create a constraint that will have to be paid in the future. The same happens outside code. Our choices can also create a kind of debt that does not appear in a backlog but weighs on us. A project that continues for years, a company that depends too heavily on a single person, or a responsibility we accumulated because we were always the ones who solved it can create costs that only appear later.

After five years on a project, it is difficult to consider shutting it down in the same way we would have considered that possibility in the first month. After building an architecture, forming a team, and establishing processes, changing direction seems more expensive. After putting our own money into a company, abandoning a hypothesis can feel like a greater defeat than simply acknowledging that it needs to be reconsidered.

The past investment, however, has already happened. When it begins to weigh on us emotionally, we risk making decisions to justify what we did rather than deciding based on what still makes sense to do. The relevant question is not how much we have already spent. It is how much it is worth continuing to invest from this point forward.

A simple way to test this is to imagine that nothing had been invested yet. With the information we have today, would we choose to start? The question helps separate the future from the past. This does not mean treating the previous investment as irrelevant. It may have produced learning, relationships, assets, or knowledge that still have value. What should not happen is allowing past cost, by itself, to determine the next step.

There is also a personal dimension to this calculation. Not every debt is financial or technical. Some choices consume energy, time, relationships, and the capacity to continue. These costs are harder to measure and, precisely for that reason, easier to ignore. A decision may continue to look rational on paper while an important part of its price is being paid by the person themselves.

I experienced this quite concretely when I had to shut down the NGO of which I was a volunteer president. For four and a half years, I balanced that activity with my professional career. The mission still made sense and I still wanted to contribute to other social organizations. The problem was that, to keep that structure running, I had to sustain a second shift. At a certain point, my body began to collect the bill for that choice. In March 2024, I decided to shut down the activities because I could no longer continue that way.

It was a difficult decision because abandoning the social project did not mean ceasing to believe in what it did. I continued to believe in the importance of helping other NGOs. What had stopped making sense was continuing to pay for it with a workload I could no longer sustain.

That experience changed the way I think about the cost of a decision. We can continue believing in a project and, at the same time, recognize that the way we are sustaining that project has become impossible. The objective can remain valid while the way of pursuing it becomes unsustainable.

It also changed the way I evaluate past decisions. Not every decision that ends badly was a bad decision. We make decisions with the information, resources, and constraints available at the time, and some variables simply are not under our control. If a decision was reasonable given what we knew at the time, we do not need to keep paying for it just to prove that it was right. We can recognize what changed, learn from the outcome, and decide again based on the present.

In the end, the question is not simply how much has already been invested or whether the current outcome is positive or negative. It is whether the next unit of investment, whether money, time, energy, or responsibility, still increases our probability of achieving the objective or merely increases the size of our exposure.

## When the Person Becomes the Single Point of Failure

This is where the discussion about burnout takes on another dimension. There is a tendency to associate it directly with overwork. The association is understandable, but it does not explain every context in which someone can become exhausted. An activity can be voluntary, enjoyable, and still concentrate enough responsibility to become unsustainable.

One example appears in qualitative research on RPG communities, in which participants reported so-called game master burnout, related to the energy and responsibility required of those who lead the sessions. The research does not treat burnout as a clinical phenomenon and does not allow us to conclude that being an RPG game master causes exhaustion. The case is interesting because it shows that an activity can consume personal resources even when it is not a job.

The game master may be responsible for preparing the session, guiding the story, managing rules, organizing the group, resolving conflicts, and keeping the experience running. When these functions are concentrated in a single person, a problem arises that, to someone in architecture, is immediately recognizable: a single point of failure. It is not necessarily a matter of workload, but of concentration of responsibility. If that person needs to stop, a significant part of the activity may simply stop functioning.

The same thing happens in the professional environment. An architect may not work more hours than the rest of the team and still be the only person capable of explaining a particular system. A manager may have a workload similar to that of their peers but concentrate all relevant decisions. An entrepreneur may consider their pace sustainable while simultaneously being the only salesperson, decision-maker, financial manager, and guardian of the business's knowledge.

The logic can also appear within a family. One person may concentrate the income, the care of a family member, household organization, or important decisions. None of these responsibilities needs to seem excessive in isolation. The risk arises when there is no other person capable of or available to take on part of them. The question ceases to be simply how much someone does and becomes how much of the functioning of that system depends on them.

A structure like this can function for a long time without seeming problematic. A company can spend years depending on a specialist. A system can operate for years with a critical component without redundancy. A family can function for a long time with all decisions concentrated in one person. This does not mean these structures are resilient. It simply means they have not yet had to deal with the absence of that critical point.

When someone needs to keep functioning despite fatigue, illness, unforeseen events, or simply the need to stop, the safety margin decreases. Burnout may be one consequence of this concentration, although it cannot be reduced to a single cause.

This also helps separate two things that may seem similar: being valuable and being indispensable. Being valuable means that your contribution makes a difference. Being indispensable may mean that the system does not have enough redundancy. In software, we know that depending on a single critical component is a risk. Organizations and families can exhibit the same fragility when knowledge, decisions, and responsibilities become concentrated in one person.

There is also dependency in the opposite direction. The person may begin to depend on that role just as much as the system depends on them. When an activity occupies too much space, it can begin to define where we put our energy, how we perceive ourselves, and where we derive our sense of usefulness. In this scenario, criticism, failure, or the need to stop no longer feels like merely a problem with that activity.

This is where an important activity can turn into an obsession. The more we concentrate identity, relationships, and time in a single thing, the harder it can be to accept that it does not work as expected. Frustration stops being simply “this project did not work out” and can turn into “I did not work out.”

Decentralization, therefore, is not only an organizational concern. We also need to distribute our attention and, when possible, our responsibilities. Having other interests, relationships, and spaces outside an activity helps prevent it from becoming responsible for filling every dimension of life. Likewise, sharing responsibilities within a family is not only about relieving the person who is overloaded. It also creates a structure capable of continuing to function when that person needs to stop.

Sometimes, what looks like extraordinary commitment is simply poor responsibility architecture. In that case, asking the person to be even more resilient may be exactly the wrong response. The problem may lie less in their ability to bear the load and more in a system that depends too heavily on them, while they themselves have become too dependent on that system.

## The System Can Also Encourage Self-Destruction

There is another aspect that cannot be placed exclusively on the individual: the system can reward behavior that later begins to exact a high price.

Organizations often value people who solve everything, who take on responsibilities beyond their role, who work beyond their hours, and who manage to keep an initiative running even when resources are lacking. In the short term, this behavior looks excellent. The difficulty appears when it stops being an exception and begins to sustain the system's normal operation.

When someone is always willing to compensate for a process failure, the process itself may never be fixed. An architect's constant availability to resolve any incident also reduces the incentive to distribute knowledge. Similarly, an entrepreneur who continues putting more money and more hours into the business may prevent the company from being forced to confront a hypothesis that is not working.

And the entrepreneur themselves can be that person. In a small company, it is common for the founder to accumulate functions, solve problems directly, and compensate with effort for what has not yet been structured. In the beginning, this may be necessary. The risk appears when the company grows but continues to depend on the same availability. What began as dedication can become a structural dependency, and the founder themselves becomes responsible for sustaining a system that was built around their ability to absorb problems. The individual may be persistent, while the system learns that their persistence is available to compensate for its own deficiencies.

This can also happen in personal projects. A person takes responsibility for organizing everything because, initially, it seems simpler to do it alone. Over time, others begin depending on them. The more they depend on them, the harder it becomes to stop. And the harder it becomes to stop, the more responsibilities end up concentrated on them. Dependency feeds overload, and overload increases dependency.

This cycle is especially difficult to notice because it can be mistaken for competence. Someone who always solves problems receives recognition precisely for continuing to absorb problems. As long as that person can sustain the load, there is little immediate incentive to change the structure that produces that load.

At some point, however, persistence stops solving the problem and starts hiding it. The more responsible decision may be to stop being the automatic solution. Documenting, delegating, distributing knowledge, establishing boundaries, and allowing a system deficiency to finally become visible can be more important ways of keeping an initiative healthy than continuing to compensate for everything alone.

A healthy system should not depend on one person's exhaustion to keep functioning. If the only way to keep an initiative alive is to demand more and more from the person who already sustains a large part of it, perhaps the issue is not a lack of commitment. It may be the structure itself that needs to change.

## There Are Things We Cannot See From the Outside. And There Are Things Only Someone Outside Can See

As useful as these analogies may be, there is an important limit to any analysis of this kind. Someone observing a situation from the outside never has all the information of the person living through it. It is easy to say that someone should delegate, change strategy, slow down, or abandon something. It is also easy to construct a rational explanation after the outcome has already happened.

It is very easy to talk about how I would drive without being in the driver's seat. The person driving knows variables that the observer does not have access to. They know their own body, recovery capacity, circumstances, responsibilities, the risks they are willing to take, and the limits that may not have even existed a few months earlier. There are details that hardly fit into a spreadsheet or an analysis conducted from the outside.

At the same time, the person inside may also stop seeing certain things precisely because they are too involved. When we are emotionally attached to a project, company, or decision, it can be difficult to notice how much of our identity is associated with it. We may normalize a workload that is no longer sustainable, rationalize signals that point toward a necessary change, or simply fail to notice that we have been repeating the same behavior for too long.

This is where an outside perspective can be valuable. Not because the person outside knows the situation better, but because they are not carrying exactly the same weight. A friend may notice that you never talk about anything else anymore. A colleague may notice that all decisions continue to reach you. Someone with no involvement in the project may ask an apparently obvious question that you stopped asking months ago.

That perspective also has limits. An observer may see the pattern without seeing the context. They may identify a dependency without understanding why it exists, recommend a change without understanding its consequences, or interpret as irrational a decision that makes sense given information they do not possess.

Perhaps, therefore, a more balanced analysis needs both perspectives. The person living through the situation can assess variables that no one outside can completely feel or know. The person outside can help reveal patterns that proximity has made invisible.

This is particularly important when the decision involves personal limits. We do not need to wait until we reach the limit to discover that we have crossed an important boundary. And when we can no longer assess this clearly, that may be precisely the moment to listen to someone who can look from the outside.

There is no universal formula for determining the exact point between persistence and self-destruction. We can, however, improve the quality of that decision by combining the perspective of the person in the driver's seat with that of someone who can observe the journey from the outside.

## Continue or Stop, the Important Thing Is to Do It Without Regret

At the beginning of this text, the question was where persistence ends and self-destruction begins. After everything we have discussed, I do not have an exact answer. Perhaps because this boundary depends more on circumstances than on a universal rule.

One of the questions that helps me most is simple: if I had today the same information I have now, would I make this decision again? A no does not automatically turn a previous decision into a mistake. Some choices made sense when they were made and stopped making sense after circumstances changed.

It took me a while to understand this. Not every decision needs to be defended to the end to prove that it was right. We can recognize that a choice was reasonable, learn from what happened, and change direction when necessary.

Perhaps this is what I have learned: continuing is not necessarily persisting, just as stopping is not necessarily giving up. Sometimes the best decision is to continue. At other times, to stop. And, often, it is simply to find another way to continue.

What matters is being able to look back without turning the past into a debt. A decision may have been valid for some time and still stop making sense. Recognizing this is not failure. It is simply recognizing that we, our circumstances, and what we seek also change.
