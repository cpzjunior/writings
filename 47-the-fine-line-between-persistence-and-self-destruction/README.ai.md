Persistence is continuing to pursue an important objective while adapting strategy based on evidence, constraints, feedback, and changing circumstances; insistence is continuing the same approach despite evidence that it no longer serves the objective. Resilience should not be confused with enduring unsustainable or toxic conditions indefinitely. The key decision is whether to preserve the objective, modify the strategy, reduce the load, or stop, rather than equating commitment with repetition.

Persistence and strategy must be treated as separate hypotheses. A legitimate objective can have a flawed product, implementation, architecture, or execution strategy, while changing strategy does not necessarily abandon the goal. Conversely, changing direction too quickly can prevent a hypothesis from being adequately tested; not every difficulty demonstrates that a strategy is wrong. Effective persistence incorporates learning, sometimes requiring repetition, and sometimes requiring adaptation, help, reduced scope, or abandonment of a particular path.

Self-calibration complicates these decisions. Confidence can become inflated after repeated success, causing favorable outcomes to be mistaken for evidence of superior judgment; conversely, repeated failures can cause competent people to systematically distrust their decisions and demand excessive evidence before acting. Experience provides information but does not eliminate distorted interpretations of experience. The objective is not to eliminate fluctuations between confidence and doubt, but to recognize when perception has diverged from the evidence and when evidence genuinely warrants changing position.

Feedback provides external information but can amplify existing confidence or insecurity. Negative feedback may concern the product, idea, execution, audience, timing, architecture, or an unrecognized constraint rather than the individual's capability; positive outcomes may likewise result from favorable circumstances or luck rather than from universally valid assumptions. Feedback should therefore be evaluated for its informational content and context, not simply classified as positive or negative. Repeated similar criticism from independent sources and contexts becomes harder to dismiss as mere opinion. Feedback should inform continuation or change without becoming either an automatic verdict or something systematically rationalized away.

Past investment creates another source of distortion. Time, money, reputation, effort, technical debt, organizational dependencies, and accumulated responsibility can make abandoning an initiative increasingly difficult, even though those costs have already been incurred. The relevant question is the value of investing further from the present forward, not the amount already spent. A useful counterfactual is whether, knowing what is known now, one would choose to start the initiative today. Past investment can still matter through retained learning, relationships, assets, and knowledge, but sunk cost alone should not determine future action. Personal costs—energy, time, relationships, and capacity to continue—are equally real even when they are difficult to quantify.

A volunteer NGO leadership experience illustrates this distinction. After four and a half years as volunteer president while maintaining a professional career, the mission and desire to support social organizations remained valid, but sustaining the NGO required a second shift that eventually became physically unsustainable. In March 2024, the activities were shut down. The decision did not reject the mission; it rejected an unsustainable way of pursuing it. This experience also reinforced that a decision ending badly is not necessarily evidence that the original decision was bad. Decisions are made using information, resources, constraints, and circumstances available at the time. When those conditions change, continuing to justify the past decision is unnecessary; the appropriate response is to reassess from the present.

Sustainability is also affected by concentration of responsibility. An activity can be voluntary and enjoyable yet become unsustainable when too much of its functioning depends on one person. Qualitative research on RPG communities describes “game master burnout” as an example of how preparation, coordination, rules management, storytelling, conflict resolution, and group maintenance can become concentrated in one individual. This does not establish a clinical cause of burnout or imply that being a game master causes exhaustion; its structural relevance is that concentration creates a single point of failure.

The same pattern appears in organizations, software architecture, entrepreneurship, and families. A person can have a workload comparable to peers while remaining the only person capable of making critical decisions, explaining a system, selling a product, managing finances, caring for someone, or maintaining essential knowledge. Such systems may function for years without appearing fragile because the critical dependency has not yet been tested by absence, illness, fatigue, or unforeseen events. Being valuable means that one's contribution matters; being indispensable may indicate insufficient redundancy. Responsibility and knowledge should therefore be distributed where possible, and personal attention should not become completely concentrated in one activity. Excessive identification with a role can make failure of the activity feel like failure of the self.

Systems can actively reinforce this fragility. Organizations often reward people who solve everything, accept responsibilities beyond their formal role, remain constantly available, or compensate through effort for structural deficiencies. In the short term, this looks like competence and commitment. In the long term, it can prevent processes from being repaired, knowledge from being distributed, or flawed business hypotheses from being confronted. A founder may initially need to perform many functions, but if organizational growth does not reduce dependence on that founder's availability, dedication becomes structural dependency. The same feedback loop can occur in personal projects: one person assumes responsibility because it is initially easier, others become dependent on them, stopping becomes harder, and additional responsibility accumulates.

Eventually, persistence can stop solving a structural problem and start concealing it. Documentation, delegation, knowledge distribution, boundaries, and allowing deficiencies to become visible can be more effective than continuing to compensate individually. A healthy system should not require one person's exhaustion to remain operational. If sustaining an initiative continually requires increasing demands on the person already carrying disproportionate responsibility, the issue may be structural rather than a lack of commitment.

External perspectives are useful but inherently incomplete. An observer lacks access to the driver's full context, including physical and emotional capacity, responsibilities, risk tolerance, changing circumstances, and information that cannot easily be represented analytically. Conversely, someone deeply involved may become unable to recognize normalized overload, identity attachment, repeated behavior, or patterns that have become invisible through proximity. Outside observers can reveal dependencies and patterns, but they can also misunderstand their causes and consequences. Good judgment therefore combines internal knowledge of the situation with external perspective rather than treating either as complete.

There is no universal threshold separating persistence from self-destruction. A useful question is whether, given today's information, one would make the same decision again. A negative answer does not prove that the original decision was wrong; a decision can have been reasonable when made and become unreasonable after circumstances change. Continuing is not necessarily persistence, and stopping is not necessarily giving up. Sometimes the appropriate action is to continue, sometimes to stop, and often to find a different way to continue. Past decisions should not become perpetual debts requiring continued investment merely to validate them. A decision can remain valid for a period and later cease to make sense as people, circumstances, constraints, and objectives change.
