# When the Sale Is Closed, the Relationship Has Only Just Begun

_Why understanding what you sell, aligning expectations, and staying close to the customer after closing are just as important as closing the deal._

**Summary:** The central idea is that a good sale begins with deeply understanding what you sell and truly understanding the customer's problem, because this allows expectations to be aligned, trust to be built, and promises the company cannot keep to be avoided. Closing the deal does not end the work of Sales: it begins a relationship that needs to remain connected to the customer's actual experience. When that relationship generates value, satisfaction, and trust, the sale stops being an isolated transaction and starts creating renewal, expansion, referrals, and new opportunities. In the end, the quality of a sale is not only about the revenue it generates today, but also about what it makes possible afterward.

---

I have been reflecting on the best Sales professionals I have worked with throughout my career. One thing caught my attention: some of those who stood out the most did not start in Sales. They came from Operations or technical areas of technology, learned the product in practice, and when they moved into Sales, they quickly learned the dynamics of selling. In a short time, they achieved remarkable growth.

In addition, there was a recurring characteristic among the best professionals I knew: they did not treat the customer relationship as something that ended when the deal was closed. On the contrary, they stayed close, followed the customer's evolution, and remained attentive to new needs. This naturally made new sales easier, either because the customer came back to them directly or because expansion or referral opportunities emerged.

I do not think this is a coincidence. It also does not mean that Sales professionals need to come from technology or operations. What seems relevant to me is something else: these professionals had the opportunity to understand the product before they had to sell it and then remained close to the people who used it. They learned how the product operated, its limitations, its problems, and the reality behind the sales presentation. At the same time, they learned more about the problems, expectations, and needs of the customers themselves.

This combination seems to produce an important difference. There is a considerable distance between knowing a product and understanding how to sell it properly. Likewise, there is a difference between winning a customer and building a relationship with that customer.

## Knowing the Product Is Not the Same as Knowing How to Sell It

Sales training can teach a great deal about a solution. It is possible to know its features, differentiators, use cases, benefits, positioning, and even the main arguments for a negotiation without ever having participated in an implementation or watched the product being used by a customer. This knowledge is necessary, but it is not sufficient.

People who have had contact with the product's operations usually develop a different repertoire. They know which integrations are simple and which tend to require more work. They know the prerequisites. They understand the limitations. They know which features work very well in certain scenarios and which may not be suitable for others. They have seen problems happen and know which questions need to be asked before making a promise to the customer.

There is also a second important dimension: knowing the customer. It is not enough to know deeply what you are selling if you do not understand what the customer is trying to solve. A product may technically meet a given requirement and still be a poor solution for that context. The role of Sales should not simply be to find where to fit its product, but to understand the customer's problem, context, and needs in order to assess whether there is, in fact, a good solution for them.

This difference changes the nature of the conversation. Instead of starting with the question, “How do I get this customer to buy?”, the conversation starts with a more important question: “Does this solution actually make sense for this customer?”

When the answer is yes, Sales can sell with much greater confidence and authority. When the answer is no, recognizing that can also be a good commercial decision. Not every opportunity needs to become a sale.

## The Technical Customer Is Also in the Room

In technology sales, it is quite likely that Sales will speak not only with the business team, but also with architects, engineers, security, infrastructure, data, operations, and other technical areas.

These people may not be responsible for the financial decision, but they often have enormous influence over it. They can identify risks, challenge assumptions, assess the feasibility of the solution, and, in many cases, directly influence the purchasing decision.

Sales does not need to know everything a technical specialist knows. Nor should it try to replace them. The technical team remains essential for going deeper into questions of architecture, integration, security, performance, or implementation.

What makes a difference is for Sales to be able to participate in this conversation with confidence, understand the context, ask the right questions, follow the reasoning, and know when an issue needs to be explored further by a specialist. These skills make the interaction much more efficient.

There is a significant difference between saying “I’ll check with our technical team” to practically every question and being able to initially answer, contextualize the issue, and then involve the specialist when necessary.

For example, instead of simply saying “I’ll check with our technical team,” Sales may be able to say: “This works in this way, but in your scenario there is a particular aspect of this integration that we need to validate with our architect.”

In the second situation, the customer realizes they are talking to someone who knows the solution, understands its own limits, and knows how to guide the conversation. The technical specialist comes in to deepen the discussion, not to rescue a conversation that Sales was unable to sustain. That is trust.

## Trust Sells

In any commercial relationship, trust is an important asset. In complex sales, it becomes even more relevant because the customer is making a decision that may involve money, processes, integration, organizational change, and risks that will only appear after signing.

The customer needs to believe that the vendor understands their problem and that what is being promised can actually be delivered. This trust is not built only through negotiating ability or the quality of the personal relationship. It also comes from the perception of competence.

When Sales demonstrates that it knows the product, understands how it works in practice, and can discuss its possibilities and limitations with confidence, the customer realizes they are talking to someone who knows what they are talking about. This credibility can hardly be built through a good sales pitch alone.

There is also an important aspect: knowing the product well makes it possible to recognize when you do not know something. That also generates trust. Trying to answer every question in order not to appear uninformed can create an incorrect expectation. Knowing when it is necessary to bring an architect, engineer, or specialist into the conversation demonstrates maturity and confidence.

In the end, it is not about Sales knowing everything. It is about the customer perceiving that they are dealing with someone who knows what they sell, understands their own limits, and knows how to guide the conversation to the right person when necessary.

Trust sells. And practical knowledge is one of the most concrete ways to build it.

## Satisfaction Is Expectation Versus Reality

A phrase I heard during my career has stayed particularly strongly with me: satisfaction is expectation versus reality. The simplicity of this idea has an important consequence for sales. Sales plays a fundamental role in shaping the customer's expectations. The salesperson is often the customer's main source of information about what is being purchased and therefore directly influences the expectation that will be taken into the organization.

If that expectation is built on a superficial understanding of the product, there is a significant risk that reality will not match what was imagined. And this can happen without any intention to deceive the customer. It is enough for the salesperson to believe that a certain feature meets a need when, in practice, there are limitations, dependencies, or conditions that were not considered.

This is where an apparently successful sale can turn into a problem. The contract was signed, the target was achieved, and the revenue came in. Months later, however, the customer discovers that the solution does not work exactly as expected. Customer Success has to manage the frustration, support receives new demands, operations face difficulties, the relationship deteriorates, and renewal is put at risk. In more serious cases, a poorly aligned sale can even result in a contractual dispute. Therefore, knowing the product in practice is not only about selling better. It is about selling correctly.

Sometimes the best commercial decision is to say that a given product is not suitable for that customer. This may mean losing an opportunity in the short term, but it can also preserve trust and create room for a future opportunity. A customer who realizes that the vendor is more concerned with solving their problem than simply closing a contract may come back when a need arises that the company can address.

Selling anything just to hit a target can generate revenue today and destroy value tomorrow. A well-executed sale, on the other hand, starts with an expectation that the company can actually turn into reality.

## When the Sale Is Closed, the Relationship Has Only Just Begun

It is common to treat closing as the endpoint of Sales' work. The opportunity entered the pipeline, was qualified, the proposal was presented, the negotiation happened, the contract was signed, and now it is time to look for the next opportunity.

In certain business models, naturally, Sales needs to have commercial targets. There is nothing wrong with that. The problem appears when closing is treated as the only indicator of a sale's success. A sale should not be considered good simply because it was closed. There is a subsequent question that also matters: did the customer receive what they believed they were buying?

This is where the relationship between Sales and Customer Success becomes fundamental. Sales does not need to take on the role of CS, but it also should not disappear from the relationship as soon as the contract is signed. Closing may end a commercial stage, but it should not end the relationship.

Maintaining some proximity to the customer allows Sales to understand whether the solution is generating value, what problems have emerged, what new needs have arisen, and how the relationship is evolving. It also allows the knowledge acquired during the sale to continue circulating within the company instead of being lost the moment the contract is signed.

The customer should not feel as though they switched companies when they moved from Sales to Customer Success. Sales knows the motivation that led to the purchase, the problems the customer wanted to solve, and the expectations established during the negotiation. Customer Success follows the customer's actual experience and sees whether the expected value is being achieved. When this information flows between the teams, the company can connect what was promised with what is being delivered.

This exchange also works in the opposite direction. CS is close to the customer's operations and can perceive changes that would hardly appear during the sales process. It can identify a new need, notice that a particular feature is being used differently than expected, or discover that the customer has started facing a problem that could be solved by another product or module. Not every discovery needs to immediately become a sales opportunity, but it represents valuable information about the customer.

Sales, on the other hand, carries information about the context that led to the purchase, the original objectives, priorities, and expectations established during the negotiation. When this knowledge reaches CS, the team taking over the relationship does not need to start from scratch. The relationship stops being a succession of handoffs and becomes a continuation.

This also requires care. The purpose of the relationship cannot be to turn every CS interaction into an upsell attempt. If every need identified by Customer Success is immediately treated as a sales opportunity, the customer quickly realizes that the relationship has stopped being about their success and has become about the next commercial target.

The logic should be the opposite: understand the customer first and sell when there is a real need that the company can address. It is this continuity that makes it possible to identify new needs, expansion opportunities, and situations in which the customer themselves can become an advocate for the company.

When this happens, the sale stops being an isolated event. It becomes the beginning of a relationship that can generate value for much longer. The cycle becomes: need, sale, delivery of value, satisfaction, trust, renewal, expansion, referral, and new opportunities.

This logic also changes the way growth is considered. A company does not necessarily need to acquire a new customer to generate a new sale. An existing customer can represent several opportunities over time, as long as the company continues to deliver value and pay attention to what that customer actually needs.

## From Satisfied Customer to New Opportunities

A satisfied customer can renew. They can expand their use of the solution. They can purchase other products. They can present new demands. And they can recommend the vendor to other companies or professionals.

This is where the relationship stops being merely a consequence of the sale and also becomes a source of new opportunities. A Sales professional who remains close to the customer can perceive needs that did not exist or were not clear at the time of the initial purchase. The customer themselves may come back with a new problem, ask about another solution, or simply remember that vendor when a new need arises.

A referral is particularly valuable because the potential customer already enters the conversation with a reference of trust. Someone they trust had a positive experience with that company and is willing to recommend it.

The customer who recommends a company is, in a sense, lending their own reputation to that relationship. This rarely happens when the experience was merely “the company fulfilled the contract.” A referral usually comes from a stronger perception: “this company solved my problem and I trust them.”

That is why a sale does not only produce revenue. It also builds, preserves, or destroys trust. A proper sale can generate satisfaction. Satisfaction can generate trust. Trust can generate renewal, expansion, and referrals. Each of these stages increases the possibility of new opportunities and reduces dependence on continuously acquiring completely new customers.

The sales process then stops being a sequence of isolated transactions and starts functioning as a cycle. The quality of a sale influences not only the revenue it generates today, but also the opportunities that may emerge from it tomorrow.

## Closing a Sale Should Not Be the Only Goal

I am not arguing that Sales should not have sales targets. The target structure depends on the business model, sales cycle, type of product, and a series of other variables. In many contexts, new revenue will necessarily be one of the main metrics. The question is different: closing the deal should not be the only way to evaluate the quality of that sale.

Two companies can generate exactly the same volume of new revenue and be building completely different businesses. In one of them, customers renew, expand, recommend, and remain satisfied. In the other, contracts are followed by churn, complaints, high support costs, and renewal difficulties. The nominal revenue may be the same. The quality of that revenue is completely different.

Perhaps the question should not only be “how much did we sell?” but also “what kind of revenue are we building?” A sale that generates revenue today and churn tomorrow has a very different value from a sale that generates revenue today and, over the years, also generates satisfaction, renewal, expansion, and referrals. The first may help hit the target for the period. The second helps build a business.

This does not mean placing all responsibility on Sales. A salesperson can make an appropriate sale and, even so, the company can fail in implementation, product, support, or Customer Success. Customer satisfaction is the responsibility of the organization as a whole. But Sales does have a specific responsibility: to begin the relationship with a promise that the company can fulfill.

And perhaps this is precisely where the quality of a sale should be measured. Not only by what it generates when the contract is signed, but also by the relationship and opportunities it makes possible afterward.

## The Sale Is Only the Beginning

When I think about the Sales professionals who impressed me the most, I return to that initial observation. The fact that some of them came from Operations or technical areas may not be the most important thing. What distinguished them was that they had known the reality of the product before they had to convince someone to buy it. They knew what they were selling.

This allowed them to speak with customers with greater authority, better understand their problems, recognize when a solution was not suitable, involve the technical team when necessary, and build an expectation closer to what the company could actually deliver. And after the sale, they remained close to the customer, following their evolution and identifying new needs.

Perhaps this is the difference between simply selling and building a sustainable business. Knowing what you sell helps build trust. Knowing the limitations helps align expectations. Aligning expectations increases the likelihood of satisfaction. Satisfaction strengthens trust. And trust creates room for new conversations, new sales, and referrals.

A sale, therefore, does not end when the contract is signed. It simply changes its nature. Closing transforms an opportunity into a customer. What happens afterward determines whether that customer will be merely a one-time source of revenue or the beginning of a relationship capable of generating value for both sides for many years.

Perhaps the most important question is not “how much did we sell?” but “what did this sale make possible afterward?” If a sale generates revenue today but leaves a dissatisfied customer tomorrow, can we really call it a good sale?
