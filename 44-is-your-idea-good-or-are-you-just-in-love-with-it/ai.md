The author describes reading entrepreneurship, product, business, and technology material as a way of accumulating reusable concepts rather than immediately applying every idea. This has also created skepticism toward advice that becomes oversimplified through repetition, such as “fall in love with the problem, not the idea.” The more interesting difficulty is recognizing when one has actually become attached to an idea, because attachment does not necessarily involve ignoring evidence outright. Instead, positive signals can be treated as validation, negative results explained away, correlations interpreted as causal evidence, and exceptions dismissed, gradually shifting the question from whether the idea is good to how to demonstrate that it is good. Validation therefore requires not only testing the existence of a problem, value proposition, and willingness to pay, but also examining whether the evidence is being interpreted according to what it actually supports.

Every idea begins as a hypothesis about a problem, its relevance to a particular group, and the existence of a solution for which people might exchange money, time, or attention. The danger begins when repeated conversations, confirming information, and product development cause the hypothesis to be mentally promoted into a fact, shifting attention from questioning the problem to figuring out how to build the solution. This transition can happen without deliberate rejection of contrary evidence; instead, new information is increasingly interpreted through the lens of what we already want to be true. The resulting problem is deeper than simply reaching an incorrect conclusion: the investigation itself can become structured to make that conclusion increasingly difficult to question, with the hypothesis being used to interpret reality rather than reality being used to update the hypothesis.

Positive reactions to an idea are easily inflated into evidence of demand. People may praise a concept, say they would use it, or ask to be notified when it launches without having a sufficiently important problem, seeking an actual solution, or ultimately paying for one. Behavioral evidence is stronger: companies already maintaining spreadsheets, hiring people, or paying for inadequate alternatives demonstrate that they are allocating resources to the underlying problem, although this still does not prove that the proposed product is the right solution. The key distinction is therefore between weak signals such as interest and stronger signals such as actual commitment or payment. Attachment to an idea can cause these different forms of evidence to be treated as equivalent, turning praise into demand, intent into purchase, and curiosity into need. The issue is not that positive feedback lacks value, but that its evidentiary significance can easily be overstated.

Negative results can be neutralized just as easily as positive ones can be inflated. When multiple potential customers show no willingness to pay, it is possible to attribute the result to the wrong audience, poor communication, an immature product, bad timing, or insufficient understanding of a new category. Any of these explanations could be correct, and the result alone may not identify which one is responsible, but their plausibility makes them useful rationalizations when they are systematically used to preserve the original hypothesis. If every negative outcome can be explained without changing the hypothesis, the data are no longer updating the investigation; they are being neutralized by it. This does not mean that every negative result disproves an idea, since experiments can be poorly designed or measure the wrong thing, but negative evidence must have a genuine capacity to weaken the hypothesis. Otherwise, the investigation has effectively become a defense mechanism.

Correlation can create a misleading sense of explanation when validating an idea. The relationship between declining pirate numbers and increasing global temperatures illustrates that two trends can move together without one causing the other. In business contexts, observed relationships can similarly be turned into narratives: an industry may complain about a problem while showing interest in a solution, or companies may grow while experiencing a particular difficulty, leading to a plausible but unproven causal explanation. A coherent story can create a stronger feeling of understanding than the evidence warrants because it fills gaps between observations. The observed relationship may instead result from a third variable, be circumstantial, or operate in the opposite causal direction. Validation therefore requires more than finding relationships that fit a desired narrative; it requires examining whether the evidence actually supports the causal conclusion being drawn.

Starting with a solution creates another source of bias because it encourages the investigation to search for a problem that justifies the product already imagined. A given problem can have many possible solutions, and the initially conceived solution may not be the best, viable, or most appropriate one; the intended audience may also be wrong, and the most effective way of delivering value may differ substantially from the original conception. Making an idea explicit before execution can help separate known facts from assumptions, observations from interpretations, and the problem believed to exist from the proposed solution. Once people identify with a product, however, they can begin viewing the problem through that product and asking how to make the chosen solution work rather than what the best response to the underlying problem might be. The investigation then shifts from exploring possibilities to finding justification for a decision that has effectively already been made.

Changing evaluation criteria after unfavorable results can make a hypothesis effectively unfalsifiable. If a test is initially defined as promising when ten customers agree to pay, but only three do so and the threshold is subsequently lowered, the experiment no longer has a stable standard against which the hypothesis can be evaluated. The same pattern can recur at progressively weaker results, allowing almost any outcome to support continued investment. Changing a hypothesis after an experiment can be legitimate when the evidence shows that the wrong thing was tested or that the original criterion was inappropriate, but this constitutes learning only if the belief itself is genuinely revised. Experimental criteria therefore need to be established before observing the result, with outcomes capable of strengthening, weakening, or leaving the hypothesis inconclusive. A validation process should have genuine capacity to alter the decision; otherwise, it risks becoming a post hoc justification for continuing.

Attachment to a product can become embedded in the structure of the company itself as resources, technology, processes, marketing, strategy, capital, and team organization accumulate around a particular solution. A company organized around solving a problem has greater flexibility because the product can remain a solution hypothesis: the audience, product, or business model can change while the underlying problem remains the object of investigation. This distinction also changes the meaning of pivoting, because abandoning a product need not mean abandoning the company when the company is not identified with that product. The author connects this to the possibility of maintaining more than one core activity or hypothesis, provided resources and priorities are disciplined and explicit. This does not eliminate the need for focus; rather, focus becomes a deliberate allocation of resources and attention among defined bets, with criteria for increasing, reducing, or stopping investment. Multiple hypotheses can also preserve optionality and reduce the psychological and structural cost of abandoning one, because discontinuing a hypothesis becomes a resource-allocation decision rather than a threat to the identity of the entire business.

Validation is presented as a decision-making discipline rather than merely a product-development stage. Instead of asking how to validate an idea, the more useful question is what evidence would cause the idea to be abandoned, because this forces the experiment to contain an outcome capable of changing the decision. Understanding a problem requires examining its frequency, consequences, and current alternatives, with relevance reflected in behavior that causes people to act; evaluating a solution requires understanding why someone would choose it over existing alternatives; and assessing willingness to pay requires distinguishing hypothetical interest from behavior demonstrating genuine intent. Problem definitions, value propositions, target audiences, and personas all represent parts of the same underlying hypothesis, but personas should be grounded in observable behavior rather than constructed narratives. Descriptions can make an idea persuasive, whereas behavior makes it testable. Positive results can strengthen a hypothesis, but evidence merely consistent with it should not automatically be treated as sufficient support. The objective of validation is therefore to reduce uncertainty enough to improve decisions about where to allocate subsequent time, money, and attention.

The principle of falling in love with the problem rather than the idea remains useful but is insufficient on its own, because someone can claim to focus on the problem while still interpreting customer conversations, research, behavioral data, and experiments in ways that favor a preferred solution. The more meaningful test is whether evidence is genuinely allowed to change both beliefs and decisions. Ideas do not need to survive every experiment, and companies do not need to remain committed to their first solutions; what needs to persist is the capacity to learn from reality before learning becomes excessively costly. The central question is therefore whether one is seeking evidence that will support a better decision or evidence that will justify a decision already made. The distinction may initially be difficult to detect, but over time it separates an organization that updates itself in response to reality from one that primarily becomes better at explaining why reality should not change its original belief.
