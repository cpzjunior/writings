# Is Your Idea Good, or Are You Just in Love with It?

_The most dangerous mistake is not defending a bad idea. It is building a mental system in which any evidence can be interpreted as confirmation of an idea we have already decided is good._

**Summary:** Validating an idea is not about finding evidence that confirms our belief, but about creating tests capable of changing it. When we fall in love with the solution, we start turning interest into demand, explaining away negative results, confusing correlation with causation, and adjusting criteria to preserve the hypothesis. Therefore, the problem needs to remain bigger than the product: the solution is merely a hypothesis that can be discarded while reality guides where to invest time, money, and attention. In the end, validation means reducing uncertainty enough to make a better decision, including when that requires abandoning what initially seemed like a good idea. The most important question, therefore, is not “how do I prove that my idea is good?”, but “what evidence would make me abandon it?”.

---

I like reading books and articles about entrepreneurship, products, business, and technology. Not necessarily because I intend to immediately apply everything I read, but because I like building my own collection, a kind of Swiss Army knife of knowledge. Some ideas stay stored away for years, until a situation makes a particular concept come back to mind. A framework that seemed abstract starts to make sense. A question read in some book becomes useful for analyzing a decision.

Perhaps that is why I have developed a certain resistance to some of the advice that repeatedly appears in this content. Not necessarily because it is wrong, but because, when a phrase is repeated often enough, it starts to seem simpler than it really is.

One example is: “fall in love with the problem, not the idea.” It is great advice. Don’t become attached to the solution. Understand the pain. Talk to people. Validate the problem before building the product. Everything seems to make sense. Until a question arises that, to me, is much more interesting: how do I know that I am in love with the idea?

The difficulty is that, if we are truly in love with it, we probably will not notice. We do not need to completely ignore the facts or insist on an obviously bad idea. The process can be much subtler. A positive response becomes validation. A negative response gets an explanation. A correlation becomes evidence. An exception becomes an isolated case. Without realizing it, we stop asking “is this idea good?” and start asking “how can I demonstrate that this idea is good?”

That is when validation stops being merely a matter of discovering whether there is a problem, a value proposition, or an audience willing to pay. It also becomes a matter of understanding whether we are interpreting the evidence consistently with what it actually allows us to conclude.

## When a Hypothesis Becomes a Belief

Every idea begins as a hypothesis. We believe that a particular problem exists, that it is relevant to a particular group of people, and that there is some solution for which those people would be willing to exchange money, time, or attention.

Imagine, for example, that I believe small businesses have difficulty tracking certain financial indicators and that a new system could solve this problem. Initially, this is merely a hypothesis. I may be right, but I may also be wrong about the existence of the problem, its relevance, the audience experiencing it, or those companies’ willingness to adopt a new solution.

The problem begins when we stop treating these statements as hypotheses that can still be revised. After talking to some people, finding information that seems to confirm our perception, and starting to design the product, that possibility can begin to be mentally treated as a fact. The question stops being “is this problem really relevant?” and becomes “how are we going to build the solution to this problem?” A hypothesis can be tested. A belief tends to be defended.

This shift does not always happen explicitly. Nobody needs to consciously decide that they will start ignoring any contrary evidence. It is enough to begin interpreting each new piece of data in light of what we already want to be true. This is the first mistake: confusing what we believe with what we have discovered.

From there, the investigation itself begins to change. Instead of using reality to update our hypothesis, we start using the hypothesis to interpret reality. The problem is not merely that we can reach the wrong conclusion. It is that we can build a process in which the conclusion becomes increasingly difficult to question.

And when that happens, we begin making other mistakes without necessarily noticing.

## Turning Interest into Demand

One of the first mistakes is turning weak positive signals into strong evidence. Imagine that you present a tool to help small businesses track their financial indicators. After the presentation, someone says they thought the idea was excellent. Another person comments that they would use it. A third asks to be notified when the product is ready. It is easy to leave that conversation with the feeling that there is demand.

But what did we actually discover? We discovered that some people considered the proposal interesting. That is information, but it is a long way from demonstrating that they have a relevant problem, that they are looking for a solution to it, or that they would be willing to pay for the product.

A person can think a feature is excellent without having the problem it solves. They can recognize a difficulty and still consider it unimportant. They can say they would pay for a solution and never actually do so. Even asking to be notified when the product is ready may simply mean that the idea sparked curiosity at that moment.

The situation changes when we find behavior. If the company already maintains spreadsheets to track these indicators, hires someone to consolidate the data, or pays for another tool that it considers inadequate, we have different evidence. These behaviors do not prove that our product is the right solution, but they indicate that there is a problem for which someone is already spending time, money, or effort.

This difference is fundamental. Ten people saying they think an idea is interesting tells us little; ten customers paying in advance tells us much more. Not because the second case definitively proves that the product will work, but because it represents behavior closer to what we are trying to discover.

When we are attached to the idea, however, we tend to put all of this evidence in the same place. Praise becomes demand, intent becomes purchase, and curiosity becomes need. A collection of weak signals starts to look like strong proof simply because they all point in the direction we would like.

The problem, therefore, is not receiving positive feedback. It is attributing more meaning to it than the observed behavior actually allows.

## Finding an Explanation for Every “No”

The same mechanism works in the opposite direction. Imagine that you present your idea to several companies that seem to be part of the audience you defined and almost none of them show interest. Instead of treating this result as information that could weaken your hypothesis, it is easy to find an explanation that allows you to keep believing in it: “these companies aren’t my audience,” “they didn’t understand the proposal,” “the product is still too early,” “we need to improve the pitch,” or “it’s a new solution, so people haven’t realized they need it yet.”

Imagine, for example, that you have spoken with twenty companies and none has shown interest in paying for the product. Maybe the problem really is the audience. Maybe the proposal was poorly presented. Maybe the product does not yet deliver enough value. But it could also be that the problem simply is not important enough to these companies. The result alone does not tell us which of these explanations is true. What it does is make the original hypothesis less comfortable.

All of these explanations may be true. And that is precisely why they are dangerous. A good rationalization does not stop being a rationalization just because it is plausible.

The problem appears when every piece of negative evidence immediately finds an explanation that allows the original hypothesis to remain intact. In that case, we are no longer using the data to update our understanding. We are using our understanding to neutralize the data.

A hypothesis that can be preserved regardless of the outcome ceases to be a genuinely testable hypothesis. If every negative response can be attributed to the audience, the product, the timing, the communication, or any other variable, there will always be a reason to continue believing in the original hypothesis.

This does not mean accepting every negative result as proof that the idea is bad. An experiment may have been poorly designed, the audience may be wrong, or the question may not have measured what we thought it did. The point is different: negative evidence needs to have a real possibility of changing our hypothesis. If it never can, perhaps we are not investigating the idea, but merely defending it.

## Confusing Correlation with Explanation

A classic example of this mistake is the famous graph relating the number of pirates to average global temperature. The number of pirates declined over the years while the planet’s temperature increased. If we look only at the two data points, there is a correlation. The problem is concluding from this that the reduction in the number of pirates caused global warming.

The example is obviously absurd, but the reasoning mechanism is quite common when we are trying to validate an idea. We notice that a particular group of people exhibits a particular behavior and construct a relationship between the two things. Professionals in an industry complain about a difficulty and also show interest in our solution. Companies in that segment are growing and facing a particular problem. So we imagine that growth creates an opportunity to solve that problem.

The narrative may make sense. And that is exactly the problem. A coherent explanation is not necessarily a true explanation. When we are emotionally involved with an idea, we tend to fill gaps with a story that connects the dots. The brain is very good at this. The result is a feeling of understanding that may be greater than what the available evidence actually allows.

It is possible to observe a real correlation and still interpret its cause incorrectly. Perhaps the observed behavior is a consequence of a third variable. Perhaps the relationship is merely circumstantial. Perhaps the direction of causality is exactly the opposite of what we imagined.

Therefore, validating an idea does not simply mean finding relationships between facts. It means questioning whether the relationship we are seeing really supports the conclusion we want to draw.

## Looking for a Problem to Justify the Solution

There is another mistake that occurs even before interpreting the results: starting with the solution.

The same problem can admit countless solutions. The fact that we imagined one of them first does not mean that it is the best, or even that it is viable. When we start with the solution, there is a tendency to look for a problem that justifies building it. When we start with the problem, we keep the space of possibilities open.

The solution I initially imagined may make sense, but there may also be a better alternative. The audience I imagined may not be the most appropriate, and the best way to deliver value may be completely different from what I had thought.

This distinction is one of the reasons why I consider it important to make an idea explicit before trying to execute it. In “[Before Taking an Idea off the Ground, You First Need to Put It on Paper](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/antes-de-tirar-uma-ideia-do-papel)“, I wrote about how this exercise helps separate what we know from what we assume, what we observe from what we interpret, and the problem we believe exists from the solution we imagine to solve it. This is more than organization. It is a way to make more visible where what we know ends and what we are assuming begins.

Because, after we identify ourselves with the product, there is a natural tendency to see the problem through it. Instead of asking “what is the best way to solve this?”, we start asking “how do I make this solution work?” At that point, the investigation stops looking for the best answer to the problem and starts looking for a justification for the answer we have already chosen.

## Moving the Goalposts When the Result Does Not Appear

Another mistake occurs when we establish criteria for testing a hypothesis and then change those criteria to preserve the decision to continue.

We define, for example, that a test will be considered promising if ten customers agree to pay for the solution. We run the test and only three agree. Instead of treating the result as information that weakens our hypothesis, we conclude that three are already enough to validate interest. If there were two, perhaps we would say that the problem was the approach. If there were one, perhaps the argument would become that we are still at the beginning.

None of these decisions is necessarily irrational in isolation. The problem lies in the pattern. If the criteria change whenever the results are unfavorable, there comes a point when any result becomes compatible with the decision to continue. The hypothesis becomes practically impossible to falsify.

This also happens more subtly when we confuse learning with justification. It is perfectly legitimate to change a hypothesis after an experiment. Learning may show that we were testing the wrong thing, that the chosen criterion did not measure what we thought it did, or that we needed to reformulate the question. What we cannot do is use any result as a justification for maintaining exactly the same belief.

That is why the criteria of an experiment matter as much as the experiment itself. Before looking at the result, we need to know what would be enough to consider the hypothesis strengthened, weakened, or inconclusive. Otherwise, we risk deciding first what we want to do and only afterward finding an interpretation that makes the result compatible with that decision.

A validation should be able to change our decision. If the experiment has no real capacity to do so, perhaps we are merely looking for evidence to support a decision we have already made.

## Confusing the Product with the Company

Attachment can also stop being merely a problem of interpretation and become part of the very structure of the business.

A company created to sell a particular product becomes dependent on the success of that solution. Team, capital, technology, processes, marketing, and strategy begin to be organized around it. The more resources we invest, the harder it becomes to admit that perhaps we should abandon it. Attachment stops being merely psychological and becomes embedded in the structure of the business itself.

A company oriented toward solving a problem has a different relationship with its products. The product is a solution hypothesis. If it does not work, we can try another. If the audience is wrong, we can change the audience. If the business model does not work, we can test another. If we discover that we understood the problem incorrectly, we can revise our own hypothesis. The problem is what we want to solve. The product is one of the ways in which we attempt to do that.

This distinction also changes the meaning of pivoting. When the company is identified with the product, abandoning the product feels like abandoning the company itself. When it is identified with the problem, abandoning a solution can simply be part of the process of finding a better solution. The product should be disposable before the company needs to be.

This logic connects to the thesis I defended in “[Why I Would Start a Startup with More Than One Core Activity Today](https://cpzjunior.substack.com/p/por-que-eu-abriria-hoje-uma-startup)“: a startup does not necessarily need to concentrate all its energy on a single activity from day one. It is possible to maintain more than one bet, as long as there is discipline in resource allocation and clarity about what is being tested.

This does not mean giving up focus. Focus remains necessary. The difference is in understanding focus as a deliberate choice about where to concentrate resources and attention, rather than necessarily as an obligation to bet everything on a single solution. We can test different hypotheses, as long as we know which ones are priorities, how much we are willing to invest in each, and which results would justify increasing, reducing, or stopping that investment.

There is an additional advantage to this approach: optionality reduces the cost of abandoning a hypothesis. If there is a single idea into which we have put all our capital, time, and identity, any negative evidence threatens much more than a product. It threatens the narrative we have constructed about the business itself.

When different hypotheses are being tested, abandoning one of them can simply be a resource-allocation decision. The more options we have, the less we need to turn one of them into a question of identity.

## Validate to Discover, Not to Confirm

At this point, validation stops being merely a stage of product development and becomes a decision-making discipline.

The question “how can I validate my idea?” can be replaced by a more uncomfortable question: “what evidence would make me abandon this idea?”

The question changes because it forces us to recognize, before the experiment, that there is an outcome capable of changing our decision. If no evidence could make us give up, perhaps we are not testing a hypothesis, but merely looking for arguments to maintain it.

For a particular audience, we need to understand how frequently the problem occurs, what consequences it produces, and how these people deal with it today. The relevance of the problem needs to be observed through its ability to provoke some action. In the case of the solution, we need to understand why someone would choose it over the existing alternatives. And when we talk about willingness to pay, we need to separate hypothetical interest from behavior that demonstrates genuine intent.

Problem, value proposition, target audience, and persona are different ways of confronting parts of this same hypothesis. A persona, for example, needs to represent a hypothesis based on reality, not a character created to make the plan more convincing. “Carlos is X years old, is a manager, likes technology, and seeks productivity” tells a story. “Carlos loses a few hours a week to a particular problem, currently tries to solve it in a particular way, and has already taken some initiative to improve the situation” offers something that can be investigated.

The difference is fundamental. A description can make an idea more convincing. Behavior can make it more testable.

And that is the purpose of validation: not to build an experiment capable of confirming the hypothesis, but an experiment capable of producing information that can change our decision. This does not mean looking only for negative evidence. A hypothesis can also be strengthened by positive results. The point is not to confuse evidence that is consistent with the hypothesis with evidence sufficient to support it.

The goal is not to prove that we had a good idea. It is to reduce uncertainty enough to make a better decision about where to put the next unit of time, money, and attention.

## Being Willing to Discover That You Are Wrong

In the end, perhaps “fall in love with the problem, not the idea” really is good advice. But it does not, by itself, solve the problem it intends to prevent. We can say that we are focused on the problem and still interpret every piece of evidence in a way that favors the solution we want to build. We can talk to customers, conduct research, measure behavior, and run experiments and still construct a narrative in which every discovery serves to confirm the original hypothesis.

The real test lies elsewhere: in our ability to accept that evidence can change what we believe and, above all, change what we decide to do.

An idea does not need to survive every test. A company also does not need to defend the first solution it chose forever. What needs to survive is the ability to learn from reality before the cost of learning becomes too high.

Perhaps this is the most useful question to ask before trying to validate any idea: “am I looking for evidence to make a better decision or evidence to justify the decision I have already made?”

The difference may seem small. At first, it is almost invisible. Over time, it can be the difference between a company that learns from reality and a company that only learns how to explain why reality is wrong.
