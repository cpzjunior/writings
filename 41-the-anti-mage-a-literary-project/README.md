# The Anti-Mage: A Literary Project

_Who watches over those who have the power to govern an entire society, and what happens when someone is given enough power to judge them without suffering consequences?_

**Summary:** In this literary project, I develop the Universum Arcanum through Alden's journey, a man who is born on the margins of the magical elite, earns his place within it, and discovers in anti-magic a way to act outside the traditional mechanisms of control. As he becomes an investigator, he begins confronting the corruption and contradictions of the institutions that concentrate power, until his pursuit of justice becomes an increasingly difficult crusade to distinguish from revenge. When he finally reaches the top as an archmage, Alden must deal with the consequences of his own choices and with the central contradiction of his journey: how can someone who learned to act without answering to anyone wield power without becoming what he spent his life fighting?

---

I am developing a fantasy story and wanted to test its reception before deciding what the best format would be to develop it. My initial intention is to turn it into a book, but I also see potential for exploring the universe in other formats, such as a game. The idea was born from a few works I enjoy, such as Harry Potter, The Lord of the Rings, The Saxon Stories, D&D, Star Wars, Game of Thrones, and The Witcher. I do not want to reproduce the style of any of them, but rather use these influences as a starting point for building an original universe, with its own history, institutions, conflicts, privileges, and contradictions.

The narrative is part of a universe I intend to develop called Universum Arcanum. What follows is one way of presenting one of the stories I imagine for this universe, following Alden's life and the events that take him from a boy raised on the margins of the magical elite to the center of power.

Before continuing, it is worth making one thing clear: this is a work of fantasy and should be read as such. Veridion, the Arcane Council, Alden, and the other characters and institutions belong exclusively to Universum Arcanum and do not represent real countries, governments, courts, parties, or people. The narrative addresses universal themes such as power, corruption, privilege, and accountability, but it was not created as an allegory for specific events or institutions in the real world. The proposal is to work within a dark fantasy atmosphere, with morally gray characters and institutions, in which apparently justifiable decisions can produce consequences that are difficult to defend.

The premise begins in a society in which only a minority is capable of using magic, but that minority concentrates much of the power, prestige, and positions of influence. Alden is born among the non-mages, the majority of the population, but they belong to a class without the same rights and opportunities as those who possess magic. He grows up experiencing the weight of this structure firsthand and develops a deep distrust of the magical elite, without, however, believing that every mage is his enemy.

His own magic awakens late, something unusual in a society where it normally manifests during childhood. This opens up an opportunity for Alden that his family never had, but it does not erase his origins or automatically grant him the prestige of the old magical bloodlines. To learn to control what has awakened within him, he must enter a world he has spent his entire life observing from the outside. Among studies, rivalries, training, and adventures, he begins to realize that the magical elite is not made up simply of good or bad people, but of an entire society organized around a power that is not always accompanied by the same capacity for accountability.

It is only later, during an expedition into a particularly difficult dungeon, that his path truly changes. There, Alden encounters a creature from another reality and, faced with the opportunity to obtain a power that should have disappeared centuries earlier, makes a conscious decision: to make a pact and accept anti-magic.

Anti-magic does not simply give him more power. It offers him something much more dangerous: the ability to interrupt the magical traces that normally allow a mage's actions to be identified and investigated. Alden gains a way to act that the magical system itself is practically unable to track.

From that moment on, his life takes a path that leads him far beyond the academy. Alden returns to his studies after the pact, completes his training, and chooses to pursue a career as an investigator. The job puts him face-to-face with corruption, criminal organizations, disputes within the magical institutions themselves, and the limits imposed by those who are supposed to enforce the law. At the same time, anti-magic allows him to act in the shadows without leaving the magical traces that would make his actions investigable. The more he learns about how the system works, the more he understands not only how investigations work, but also where they can fail. Little by little, Alden moves ever closer to the center of power, until he reaches the very place he spent his entire life questioning.

This is the kind of journey I want to explore through Alden. More than telling the story of someone who possesses an unusual power, I want to follow what that power does to his choices and to the way he begins to see the people and institutions around him. How far can someone go when they believe they are doing the right thing? And at what point does the pursuit of justice begin to become confused with the desire to have the power to decide what is just?

The text below is not the finished book. It is a literary demo organized into topics from Alden's life, a condensed version of the story I imagine writing. Some things are still open, including what happens when he finally reaches the top.

My intention here is to discover whether the concept generates enough interest to justify a larger project. If you knew this premise and these events, would you want to follow Alden's journey? Does the universe spark enough curiosity for you to want to know more about it, whether in a book, a game, or another format? That is the kind of opinion I am looking for.

## The Boy from the Countryside

Alden was a citizen of Veridion. At least, that was what the records said. For those who possessed no magic, however, the word had little meaning. Non-mages did not enjoy the same rights as mages and, in many regions, lived under the authority of the magical elite. Alden's family worked for one of those families. The land did not belong to them, their decisions were limited, and their future depended on those above them.

That was the environment in which Alden grew up. From an early age, he learned that effort and freedom were not necessarily rewarded in the same way. He could work as hard as anyone else, but that did not guarantee him the same opportunities. He could do everything correctly and still remain exactly where he had been born.

The countryside, however, also taught him things no academy could offer. Hunting was part of the family's routine. Alden learned to follow tracks, recognize footprints, observe the wind, and remain silent until the right moment to act. First he learned to use a bow; later, a spear. They were tools for survival, but over time he developed enough skill to turn necessity into competence.

Above all, he learned patience. In the countryside, force rarely solved everything. One had to observe before acting, understand the environment, choose the right moment, and know when to retreat. A mistake could mean losing the hunt, wasting resources, or putting someone in danger. Those lessons would stay with him long after he left the countryside.

Alden also learned to observe people. Mages represented only about ten percent of the population, but they had shaped the institutions, professions, academies, hierarchies, and very idea of prestige. The other ninety percent were part of that society, but did not occupy the same place within it.

Alden did not need to understand Veridion's political structure to perceive this. He only had to look at his own family. He saw people obeying orders they could not question. He saw decisions about land, work, and resources being made by individuals who possessed authority simply because they had been born with magic. He saw non-mages treated as inferior without anyone needing to say so openly.

Contempt was rarely accompanied by a declaration. Sometimes it was in a joke, a glance, or the way a conversation changed when a mage entered the room. It was also in the naturalness with which someone could decide the fate of a family without considering that they had the right to object.

Alden did not develop his resentment because of a theory about society. He developed it because he lived within it. That did not mean, however, that he believed all mages were evil. There were those who protected cities, saved lives, and took their responsibilities seriously. Some treated non-mages with genuine respect. Alden knew how to recognize these differences.

What he could not accept was that the kindness of some individuals did not alter the structure that determined who would be born with power and who would spend their life serving those who possessed it.

That was how he began to form his worldview: distrusting people less than the structures that allowed them to command without having to answer for their decisions. When his magic finally awakened, years later than normal, Alden already carried with him everything he had learned about power, privilege, and the limits imposed on those who were born without it.

## The Arcane Academy

Alden's awakening was a rare event. In most people, magic manifested during childhood. When it appeared much later, it was regarded as something unusual, almost like a favor from the Creator. For Alden, however, the awakening did not erase what he had experienced until then. He remained distant from the magical-blood families that, generation after generation, accumulated prestige, influence, and power.

Still, for the first time, there was a concrete possibility of changing his family's fate. If he could learn to use magic, Alden could attain a position that would never have been within reach of a non-mage. He could stop serving the elite and, perhaps, offer his family a future different from the one into which he had been born.

That was what led him to seek precisely what he had spent his life despising. To learn magic, Alden would have to live among the same mages who had so often made him feel inferior. He would need to enter their world, learn their rules, and prove that he was capable of earning a place among them.

The problem was that awakening did not mean knowing how to use magic. Veridion's mages were trained from an early age in a discipline that combined study, practice, and martial training. Controlling magic required years of preparation. One had to learn to channel energy, master techniques, develop endurance, and transform supernatural abilities into something that could be used precisely under pressure.

Alden had missed all those years. For someone who awakened so late, reaching the level of a mage trained since childhood would be much more difficult. And magical academies, besides being demanding, were expensive. Alden did not have the money to pay for his education.

While looking for a way into that world, he took to the roads. He became an adventurer, explored dungeons, fought creatures, and learned to survive without depending on a structure interested in protecting him. The countryside had already taught him to hunt, track, and use a bow and spear. The roads added other things: combat, exploration, reading environments, risk assessment, and resource management.

In the dungeons, he discovered a rule that no training could soften: a bad decision did not result in a poor grade. It could result in a corpse.

His experience eventually drew attention, but not exactly in the way Alden imagined. To the magical institutions, someone like him represented a potential resource. He had no important lineage, money, or political connections, but he possessed something many young mages took years to develop: combat experience, survival skills, and familiarity with real-life risk. He was the kind of talent that could be put to use in field service.

That was how Alden arrived at the Academia Arcana Militaris Imperialis, one of the oldest institutions of magical education in Veridion. The name was a relic from another era. The Aurelian Empire had disappeared centuries earlier, replaced by independent arcane city-states, but the academy had preserved its old designation, just as other institutions had preserved titles, symbols, and traditions from a world that no longer existed.

Despite the imperial name, its function remained quite practical. The academy trained mages to serve in defense forces, accompany expeditions, fight creatures, and operate in regions where magic needed to be used far from the safety of cities.

The children of the great families continued to have access to the most prestigious positions. For candidates like Alden, however, there were openings intended for those who demonstrated potential for field service.

Alden fit that profile. Not because he was a prodigy, but because he already knew how to fight and survive in hostile environments. The bow, the spear, hunting, dungeons, and months on the roads had taught him things no classroom could reproduce. The academy could teach him magic. For the most part, he had already learned the rest on his own.

To Alden, it seemed to be exactly the opportunity he had been looking for. If he accepted the training and fulfilled his role, he could turn his magic into a profession, earn a better position, and change his family's circumstances.

But he soon realized that there was a difference between being accepted by the academy and being accepted by those who belonged to that world.

Alden was still the boy from the countryside. He had no important surname, known magical lineage, or family capable of opening doors for him. Even though he had awakened his own magic, he still occupied an inferior position within the elite he had just joined.

Some classmates called him “the Ascendant,” as if it were a compliment. It was a convenient way of acknowledging his journey without truly considering him one of them. He later discovered the name they used when he was not around: social climber.

Alden did not want to belong to that world. He wanted to prove that he could defeat it on its own terms. He wanted to master the magic that had been used throughout his entire life to keep him beneath those who possessed it.

At that moment, he still did not want revenge. He wanted recognition. He wanted to show that someone like him could go as far as any member of the great families. And to do that, he would have to achieve in a few years what the others had learned throughout their childhood.

## That Which Should Not Exist

During a break from the academy, Alden began to question whether he really wanted to continue on that path. His training was giving him what he had been looking for, but it was also bringing him closer and closer to a world that had never been made for people like him. He missed the roads, the dungeons, and the freedom that existed far from institutions.

It was then that he decided to face a dungeon that few adventurers were willing to explore. Not only because of the number of creatures or the difficulty of its challenges, but because of one particularly inconvenient characteristic: magic seemed to lose its strength as someone advanced.

Alden, however, did not depend exclusively on it. He knew how to use a bow and spear, hunt, track, manage resources, and fight without needing spells. His experience on the roads had taught him to survive when what normally worked stopped working. To him, the dungeon represented a considerable risk, but not necessarily an impossible one to face.

At first, he noticed nothing extraordinary. The dungeon itself seemed to demand more from his body than from his magic, and Alden simply adapted the way he fought. He used spells when they were useful, but also relied on the same skills he had developed before he even knew he possessed powers.

The farther he advanced, however, the harder it became to ignore what was happening. His mana was decreasing. It was not merely exhaustion. His ability to channel magic was being reduced little by little, as if something around him were consuming whatever he tried to use.

It was only after advancing much farther than any other group had managed to that he understood why that dungeon was so avoided. At its end was a creature that did not belong to that world.

Its presence interfered with the very manifestation of magic around it. The mages' energy was drained simply by being nearby, keeping the creature alive while making it increasingly difficult for any spellcaster to remain there.

Alden observed it carefully. The creature was weakened. It did not seem to be there by choice, nor did it behave like a beast waiting for an opportunity to kill someone. There was something about its existence that seemed as out of place as he himself had felt throughout his life.

The creature spoke first. It explained that it had crossed a boundary between realities and become trapped in that world. It did not know whether it could return. To survive, it needed to consume a particular form of energy.

It was not simply mana. It was something more difficult to define. The creature needed a specific combination of magical nature and intense emotional states. When those conditions came together, they produced what it needed to continue existing.

Mages possessed the necessary nature, but did not produce that energy in sufficient quantities. Non-mages carried the necessary emotions, but did not possess the compatible magical nature. Alden possessed both.

He quickly understood why. He had been born without apparent magic and spent his childhood being treated as inferior by those who possessed it. He knew powerlessness before he knew power. He knew the resentment of someone who depended on people who would never need to depend on him. And he knew the anger of wanting to prove to all of them that what they had built to keep him beneath them was not a measure of his worth.

The creature did not try to hide its interest in Alden. Nor did it pretend that it was offering help out of kindness. It needed a compatible source of energy to continue existing, and Alden was the first one it had found.

But if it needed something from Alden, it would also have to offer something in return: anti-magic.

Alden knew the concept only from ancient records. Most scholars considered the ability lost, associated with a lineage that, according to official history, had disappeared centuries earlier.

The creature explained that anti-magic was not simply a different way of casting spells. It was a way of interrupting the relationship between magic and the traces it normally left behind. A mage could produce fire, alter matter, hide objects, or kill someone, but their actions would leave some form of magical signature that would allow investigators to link them to the person responsible.

Anti-magic made it possible to break that connection. The effect continued to exist, but the cause could no longer be identified through conventional magical methods.

Alden immediately understood the significance. This was not merely a powerful technique. It was something capable of breaking one of the primary forms of control existing in magical society.

He also knew that pacts were extremely dangerous. Mages themselves treated such agreements with caution because the consequences could exceed what those involved imagined when they accepted the terms. A poorly understood pact could turn a temporary advantage into permanent dependence.

Alden was not naive. He knew he was facing something he should fear. Even so, the more he thought about what had happened, the harder it became to believe that the encounter was merely a coincidence. He had awakened his magic too late. He had realized that the academy might not be the path he imagined. He returned to the roads just as he began questioning the future he was building. Then he found a dungeon that the mages themselves could not properly explore because their magic was being drained. And at the end of that place, he found a creature from another reality that knew precisely the power that could allow him to stop depending on the magical rules governing his society.

Alden believed in the Creator, but he did not need to turn that belief into an explanation for what was happening. He did not know whether there was any will behind those events. He did not even know whether there was a purpose.

Perhaps it was simply chance. Perhaps there was some reason everything had happened in that order. Alden had no way of knowing.

He only knew that he did not want to let the opportunity pass. The creature needed him to continue existing. Alden needed it to learn how to control anti-magic. There was no trust between them, much less friendship. There was mutual need and an opportunity that might never appear again.

Alden knew the risks. Even so, he accepted the pact.

## The Secret of the Anti-Mages

As soon as he left the dungeon, Alden returned directly to the academy. He did not want to wait to discover what he had just accepted. He still remembered every word the creature had spoken and, although he had decided to make the pact, he knew that he did not fully understand the nature of the power he now carried. Before attempting to use it, he needed to discover what the mages of Veridion knew about anti-magic and, above all, why such a rare ability had disappeared from more recent records.

He went straight to the library. During the following days, he researched discreetly, avoiding teachers, classmates, and anyone who might notice what he was looking for. He began with treatises on magic, moved through historical records and studies on different forms of channeling, and finally reached documents related to the investigation of magical crimes.

The more he searched, the stranger the absence seemed. Most books simply did not mention anti-magic. When there was a reference, it was almost always brief, vague, or hidden among events from centuries earlier. Some authors treated the subject as superstition. Others mentioned an ancient lineage of sorcerers who had supposedly disappeared during conflicts in a poorly documented era. Alden no longer believed it was merely a legend. He had just received that power.

The search continued until he found an old book, different from the others. It did not appear to have been consulted for a very long time. The pages were worn, and some notes in the margins had been partially erased, as though someone had attempted to remove information that should not remain there.

The book recorded events from an era the magical institutions preferred to forget. It was there that Alden discovered that anti-mages were not a legend. They had existed. And they had been exterminated.

At first, this seemed merely like historical confirmation. But as he moved through the pages, Alden realized there was much more to the story than the few modern records allowed to show. The anti-mages had not simply disappeared. They had been sought out, hunted, and killed for generations.

The descriptions varied depending on the period and author, but there was one element common to practically all the records: when someone was identified as an anti-mage, their ability was considered a threat that could not be tolerated by the magical institutions.

Anti-magic was not powerful because it destroyed more than other forms of magic. It was not a more brutal version of an offensive spell or a superior source of energy. It was dangerous for a much simpler reason: it left no magical traces.

A mage who cast a spell left a magical signature. Trained investigators could identify changes in the environment, follow traces of energy, reconstruct events, and, under certain circumstances, establish a connection between an act and the person who had carried it out. Magic could hide many things, but it did not completely eliminate the relationship between cause and effect.

Anti-magic eliminated precisely that relationship. The effect continued to exist. The fire burned. The door remained open. An object remained missing. Blood continued to flow. A person remained dead. But the magical connection between the event and the person who had caused it disappeared.

There was no signature to follow. No magical trail to reconstruct. No trace an investigator could point to and say: it was this man.

Alden then understood why those men had been hunted with such determination. The problem was not simply what an anti-mage could do. It was what no one could prove he had done.

A person capable of acting without leaving magical traces could not be subjected to the same forms of investigation and accountability applied to other mages. There was no reliable way to distinguish someone perfectly honest from a murderer who had simply learned to conceal their actions. Even an anti-mage who had never committed a crime carried an ability that the institutions could not verify.

The fear made sense. Alden had spent much of his life distrusting a system that granted power to a small portion of the population and demanded little from those who possessed it. Now he discovered that the same system had exterminated an entire lineage because it believed certain people could not exist without threatening the very structure of accountability within society. The extermination had been monstrous. But the danger posed by anti-mages was not imaginary.

Alden closed the book for a few moments. Until then, he had thought of anti-magic as an opportunity. A rare ability that could give him independence, allow him to challenge the limits imposed by magical society, and perhaps finally give him an advantage over those who had been born above him.

Now he realized that he had received something that could get him killed simply by existing. If anyone discovered his secret, his innocence might not matter. He had committed no crime. Even so, the records were clear about the fate of those who were identified: they would be hunted and killed. The mere discovery that an anti-mage existed could be enough to condemn him to death.

Alden felt the weight of that conclusion in a way unlike anything he had felt since the pact. The problem was not learning to use anti-magic. It was making sure no one discovered that he could use it. And that completely changed the nature of the risk.

He needed to understand how mages investigated. Not only how they identified a criminal, but how they reconstructed an event when they could not rely on a magical signature. He needed to know what evidence replaced the traces that anti-magic eliminated, how investigators formulated hypotheses, how they eliminated suspects, and how they realized that a scene had been manipulated. He needed to know who had the authority to conduct such an investigation, who could order searches, access records, interrogate, and accuse.

The more he thought about it, the clearer an uncomfortable conclusion became. His military career, already unexciting, was no longer enough. As a soldier, Alden would learn to fight, developing discipline, strategy, and techniques for surviving combat. That could be useful, but it would not solve his greatest problem.

Anti-magic would not be discovered because he lost a battle. It would be discovered because someone investigated an event and realized that something did not make sense. Alden needed to learn how to prevent that from happening. So he decided to change his path: to become an investigator.

The decision had nothing to do with the idea of pursuing justice or with any particular desire to solve crimes. Alden had not discovered a calling. He was trying to survive. He needed to understand the other side of the equation.

If someone ever suspected that an anti-mage existed in Veridion, Alden wanted to understand exactly how that person would be hunted. He wanted to know the methods, procedures, instruments, and people involved before anyone had reason to apply them against him.

He needed to learn how investigators thought. Which details they observed first. What kinds of inconsistencies aroused suspicion. How they distinguished coincidence from evidence. How they reconstructed the sequence of events when there was no magical signature. And, above all, he needed to discover what mistakes could expose someone who believed they had acted without leaving any trace.

At that moment, Alden still thought of this as a form of protection. The more he knew about investigative methods, the lower the chances of being discovered. The better he understood those who might search for him, the more time he would have to realize when he was being hunted.

But there was a consequence he did not yet fully understand. To protect himself from an investigation, it was not enough to know how an investigation worked. He had to know where it could fail.

He needed to recognize which pieces of evidence could be challenged, which hypotheses could be diverted, and which conclusions an investigator could be led to accept without realizing that they were looking in the wrong direction.

Anti-magic had given him a way to act without leaving traces. Alden decided to learn how traces were found. And, if he ever needed to, how to make them lead somewhere else.

## The Investigator

Convincing his tutors to allow the career change was not easy. Alden had to insist that his experience as an adventurer could be useful in field investigations, particularly in conflicts, war crimes, and operations against clandestine groups. His tutors did not like the idea, but they recognized a political use in the proposal: if Alden could gather information capable of incriminating Veridion's enemies, that evidence could be used to pressure them, negotiate agreements, or even blackmail them.

Alden found the idea abhorrent, but he needed to survive. If that was the condition for leaving the military and moving closer to investigations, he would accept it.

During the following years, he received specific training to become an investigator. He learned to analyze crime scenes, interpret magical evidence, conduct interrogations, reconstruct events, and work with different investigative methods. He also studied legal procedures, institutional hierarchies, and the limits of an investigator's authority. These were enough years to transform what had begun as a survival strategy into a profession in which his previous experiences finally found their purpose.

Anti-magic remained a secret. Alden still did not know exactly how far he could take it, but he had already understood its value. While other investigators depended on magical traces to reconstruct events, he possessed an ability capable of interrupting precisely what they were looking for. Even so, he did not intend to depend on it exclusively.

The first major opportunity came when a sect of necromancers was discovered. Alden's supervisors did not want to put him in charge of the investigation. They considered him too young and preferred to hand the case to more experienced investigators.

Alden insisted. He wanted to prove that he was capable. He assumed leadership against the wishes of his superiors and began investigating.

Anti-magic was decisive. Alden could enter certain places without leaving a magical signature that would reveal his presence. He could observe, test hypotheses, and investigate areas protected by magic without immediately alerting those being sought. When necessary, he used his own spells, although they were still inferior to those of many more experienced investigators.

The rest came from what he already possessed before even learning magic: strategy, patience, the ability to observe and act, knowledge of hostile environments, and the experience acquired on the roads, where ignoring information could mean the difference between returning home and dying.

The investigation advanced. Alden found the sect, identified its members, discovered how the operation worked, and gathered enough evidence to dismantle it. He recovered dangerous artifacts, prevented new rituals, and handed the perpetrators over to the authorities.

His supervisors were forced to acknowledge the result. The investigation they had tried to take from him had been successfully concluded, and Alden received a commendation for his work.

It was the first time he felt he had found his place. Throughout his life, Alden had tried to prove that he could survive in worlds that had not been made for him. In the countryside, he had learned to hunt. On the roads, he had learned to survive. At the academy, he had learned magic. Now he had found an activity in which all those experiences seemed to fit together.

His reputation grew from that case. Then came other investigations. Then more difficult cases. Alden began receiving greater responsibilities and moving among people who, only a few years earlier, would never have had a reason to know his name.

The more prestige he accumulated, the closer he came to the center of Veridion's magical power. Until he received a seat as an observer on the Arcane Council. He did not yet possess the power of a councilor, but he could attend discussions, hear decisions, and closely observe the men and women who shaped the course of magical society.

To Alden, that represented much more than recognition. It was access. For the first time, he was inside the structure he had spent his life observing from below. He could hear what normally never reached the streets, understand how decisions were made, and closely observe the people who possessed enough authority to change the fate of an entire city.

And most importantly: no one there knew that, for Alden, being so close to power had never been merely a matter of prestige.

## The Arcane Council

As an observer on the Arcane Council, Alden began frequenting an environment that, until recently, had existed for him only as a distant institution.

To the society of Veridion, the Council was one of the city's principal guarantees of stability. Its members were among the most experienced and powerful mages of their time, responsible for protecting the city, preserving order, and making decisions when conflicts between factions threatened to exceed acceptable limits. They were the guardians of Veridion.

Alden, however, had known one of those men long before taking his seat as an observer. Aurelius Aurelian Septimus had been part of the magical elite for decades and, for much of Alden's life, had been among the men who represented precisely what he despised most about Veridion. Aurelius knew Alden's family and frequently mistreated them. He did not need to threaten or directly punish them. The way he spoke to them, treated their work, or made it clear that he considered people like them inferior was enough.

Alden had learned to live with that since childhood. Now, seated inside the Council, he could observe Aurelius closely. And he began to realize that the reality behind the institution was less perfect than the image that existed outside it.

The information surfaced in discreet conversations among employees, investigators, and members of different sectors within the magical structure itself. They were comments made away from official sessions, complaints that never reached the records, and rumors circulating among those who had some contact with the Council's inner workings.

That was how Alden heard about Tiberian Valerian. Tiberian was a respected, powerful, and influential archmage. Officially, his career was marked by decades of service to Veridion. Behind the scenes, however, his name repeatedly appeared in connection with smuggling, extortion, and other activities conducted by criminal factions. There were investigators who believed they possessed enough evidence to justify a formal investigation and, under certain circumstances, even his preventive removal.

But there was a problem. Tiberian Valerian had the president of the Arcane Council, Aurelius Aurelian Septimus, in his pocket. The investigation existed, but it did not move forward. The request for removal also existed, but never reached the agenda.

Alden discovered that there was an institutional reason for this. Under the Council's own rules, an archmage could not be removed simply because another member wanted him gone. The matter had to be formally placed on the agenda by the president before the Council could deliberate on it.

The rule had a legitimate purpose. It prevented personal rivalries, political disputes, or internal groups from using the Council to remove someone from a position of power without an institutional process. In an organization composed of some of the most powerful mages in Veridion, establishing limits on the removal of its own members was a way to prevent the Council from becoming an instrument of internal disputes.

In that case, however, the same protection had become a shield. As long as Aurelius refused to put the matter on the agenda, the Council could not even formally discuss Tiberian's removal.

What bothered Alden most was not merely discovering that an archmage was involved with criminals. It was seeing how the people around him treated the situation. No one seemed genuinely surprised.

Some considered Aurelius's behavior simply another inevitable consequence of politics. Others complained privately but avoided confronting him directly. Some knew exactly what was happening and preferred to wait for the problem to disappear on its own.

Corruption was not hidden. It happened in the Council's corridors, in front of people who knew the rules, understood their limitations, and knew exactly how to use them to their own advantage.

And yet, outside, none of it existed. To Veridion's society, those same men remained the city's guardians. They were treated with respect, honored in ceremonies, and regarded as examples of wisdom, power, and responsibility.

Alden began to perceive a difference that troubled him. The problem was not simply the existence of corrupt people within an institution. It was an institution's ability to continue functioning legitimately while some of its own members found ways to prevent it from correcting their abuses.

He had known injustice since childhood. He had grown up watching powerful men make decisions about the lives of people who did not even have the right to challenge them. But this was different.

At that moment, he was seeing injustice from the other side. Not as someone subjected to power, but as someone beginning to understand how power protected itself.

And there was something particularly disturbing about that discovery. The Council was not composed only of incompetent or corrupt men. Many of its members were exactly what society believed them to be: powerful, experienced, and genuinely committed to protecting Veridion. There were mages who sincerely believed that the rules existed to prevent abuses and that the Council needed to preserve its own stability in order to continue protecting the city.

The problem was that this did not prevent others from exploiting those same rules. Alden began to understand that an institution did not need to be entirely corrupt to produce unjust results. It was enough for a few people to know where its weaknesses were.

For the first time, he was not merely looking at injustice from below. He was looking inside it. And he was beginning to discover that power did not need to break the rules to protect itself. Sometimes, it only needed to control who was allowed to enforce them.

## The Pyromancer Vigilante

Alden did not take long to realize that an opportunity lay before him. Throughout his childhood, Aurelius Aurelian Septimus had represented a part of the world Alden had learned to hate. He was one of the men who could look down on his family, mistreat them, and decide about their work without ever having to answer for it. Now, for the first time, Alden had something Aurelius did not know about and against which his institutions had no defense: anti-magic.

The investigation into Tiberian Valerian had shown Alden that the Council possessed mechanisms capable of preventing a powerful man from being held accountable when another powerful man decided to protect him. Aurelius was in Tiberian's pocket, and Alden knew that, within the rules, he probably could not do much. But perhaps he did not need to follow the rules.

The opportunity found Alden at precisely the point between indignation and resentment. For years, he had carried the humiliations of his childhood without the means to respond. Now, just as he discovered that the system he despised so much was incapable of confronting a man like Aurelius, he possessed an ability that allowed him to act outside it.

For the first time, he could turn old resentments into something concrete. He thought about what he could really hurt Aurelius through. He could not confront him directly. He did not want to expose his existence as an anti-mage, nor did he believe a threat would be enough. So he reached a simple conclusion: what hurts a corrupt man most is his wallet.

Alden began with the president's properties. In the first action, he chose a remote property, far from the center of Veridion. He knew the risks and did not intend to leave any magical trace that could lead back to him. He used anti-magic to bypass the protections, entered the property, and set it on fire.

The fire Alden left behind, however, was not like an ordinary fire. He had infused the flames with mana, making them faster, more intense, and much harder to extinguish. The fire moved through the building at unusual speed, consuming wood and weakening structures before anyone could contain it.

Before acting, he had made certain no one was there. When the flames began consuming the building, Alden remained hidden and watched. For a few moments, he felt satisfaction. Throughout his life, men like Aurelius had been untouchable. Now he had found a way to hurt one of them without asking anyone's permission.

In the following days, he continued. One property after another. Businesses began to suffer losses. Warehouses were set on fire. Buildings were destroyed. Alden studied routines, checked who might be present, and used anti-magic to ensure that no investigator would find a signature capable of leading them to him.

Fire became his preferred weapon. It was simple, efficient, and, thanks to anti-magic, left no magical signature that could be traced back to him. The flames did the rest.

Alden also established a limit for himself: no one would die. He checked the properties before every action and chose times when he could be certain they were empty. He wanted to destroy Aurelius's property, not his family.

For a while, he believed that would be enough, but it was not. Aurelius resisted. He would not put Tiberian's removal on the agenda, no matter what happened.

Alden continued. New properties were targeted. New businesses began to collapse. The president's wealth diminished, but he remained silent. Pressure within the Council increased, but Aurelius did not yield.

Then Alden realized he was entering a cycle he no longer knew how to stop. He needed to plan new incursions, choose new targets, study new routes, cross new protections, and risk being discovered with every action. The incursions did not happen every night, precisely to avoid creating a pattern that might attract attention, but they were nevertheless beginning to consume more and more of his time and energy. He was tired. The president continued to resist and, with every new property hit, Alden had to decide whether it was worth taking the risk again.

Until he reached a desperate conclusion. If Aurelius did not care about his own property, perhaps Alden needed to strike what was closest to him: his family.

Alden knew that this was different. Aurelius's family had not put Tiberian in power. They had not prevented the Council from acting. They had not necessarily participated in any of the decisions Alden considered corrupt.

Even so, he began constructing a justification. Those people lived surrounded by the wealth and privileges produced by Aurelius's position. They benefited from his influence. If some of that wealth came from bribes and agreements built on corruption, then they were also living off the fruits of that system. It was a convenient justification. Deep down, however, Alden knew he was trying to convince himself.

That night, he decided to target the main property of Aurelius's family. He did not intend to kill anyone. He did not even intend to hurt anyone. He wanted to cause enough fear to show Aurelius that his resistance had a price.

This time, the effect was different. The property belonged to an arcane aristocratic family. The people were evacuated in time, but panic spread rapidly. Guards were called, protections were reinforced, and messages began circulating among relatives and allies. No one understood how someone had managed to get through those defenses and attack the property without leaving any magical signature. The family was terrified.

Alden watched everything from a distance, relieved that no one had died. But the relief did not last. For the first time, he clearly realized that he had used the fear of people who had not necessarily committed any crime as a tool of pressure. Remorse came almost immediately.

He felt it as something physical, difficult to push away. He had spent so much time telling himself that he was merely fighting an unjust system that, for a few moments, the justification lost all its force. There was no Council, corruption, or Tiberian in that property. There was only a family that had just discovered that someone had managed to enter their home, get through their protections, and set fire to their belongings without anyone being able to explain how.

Alden could stop. This time, however, he was the one who did not yield. He buried the remorse and waited.

At the next meeting, Aurelius finally yielded. He put Tiberian Valerian's removal on the Council's agenda. When he received the news, Alden was exhausted. He had spent weeks planning incursions, moving in and out of the shadows, destroying properties, and taking care that no one noticed a connection between the attacks. He expected that, to an outside observer, it would appear to be nothing more than another conflict between egotistical arcane families.

This time, Alden would not need to make another incursion. He smiled inwardly. He was satisfied. At last, he could rest and let the Council handle the rest of the process. After so many actions, Aurelius had finally yielded.

The next day, however, something happened that would turn that victory into a much bigger problem. Aurelius himself sought Alden out. When he found him, there was no hostility in his behavior.

Aurelius recognized him immediately. He called him by name, greeted him, and asked how things were going. He spoke to him as he would to any promising young investigator who was beginning to make a name for himself within the Council.

For a few moments, Alden did not know how to react. For years, he had carried the image of Aurelius in his memory as an almost monstrous man, someone who represented everything that had humiliated his family. Now, standing before him, there was simply a man.

A powerful and corrupt man, perhaps. A man who had protected Tiberian and used his position to prevent the Council from acting. But not the monster Alden had built inside his own memory. Aurelius might even be a man genuinely concerned about his family.

That possibility made everything more difficult. Alden had burned that man's properties and brought panic into his family. And yet Aurelius stood before him knowing nothing, treating him with a cordiality Alden had not expected to receive. For the first time, the image he had constructed of Aurelius began to conflict with the man standing before him.

Then the president explained the real reason he had sought him out. Aurelius knew Alden's reputation. He had heard about the investigation Alden had led against the necromancer sect and considered him one of the most promising investigators of his generation. Recently, he had begun to suspect that an anti-mage might be operating in Veridion. He wanted Alden to investigate.

Fear returned immediately. Perhaps he had been discovered. Perhaps this conversation was a test. Perhaps someone had found a clue he did not yet know about. The man Alden had just harmed was, unknowingly, placing him at the very center of the investigation that could reveal his own identity.

Alden, apprehensive, accepted. From that moment on, he would have to investigate not only a possible anti-mage. He would have to discover whether the investigators were getting close to him.

## The Investigator of His Own Crimes

Alden soon realized that Aurelius had no idea whom he was looking for. The fear he had felt upon receiving the assignment began to disappear as he understood the situation. No one had discovered his secret. On the contrary, the president of the Council himself had just placed him inside the investigation that could threaten his identity. What had initially seemed like a trap began to look like an opportunity. If he needed to investigate the possibility that an anti-mage existed in Veridion, he could use that position to discover how far the investigators were capable of going and, at the same time, observe men who would never be investigated by other means.

At night, Alden began investigating suspected mages. The residences of the most powerful were true magical fortresses, protected by barriers, runes, alarms, and detection mechanisms. For an ordinary investigator, entering without authorization would be practically impossible. Anti-magic, however, made those defenses less reliable. Alden could cross protections without leaving the signature that would normally reveal an intrusion and, once inside, search for documents, correspondence, financial records, and any other evidence capable of revealing what his targets were hiding.

It did not take long to discover that there was much more corruption in Veridion than he had imagined. He found archmages involved in smuggling, extortion, and clandestine agreements, powerful families protecting criminal operations, and individuals who used their positions to commit abuses with the certainty that their political relationships would be enough to protect them. Some of these cases were already arousing suspicion among investigators, but no one had managed to gather sufficient evidence. Others had never even come to the authorities' attention.

Alden discovered these things at night and, in the morning, returned to work as if nothing had happened. Instead of simply handing over the information he had obtained illegally, he learned to steer official investigations in the direction he wanted. An apparently casual piece of information led to a new question. A question produced a hypothesis. The hypothesis justified a request for a particular document. The document revealed another connection, which led investigators to one person, then another, until the investigation finally found what Alden had already known since the previous night.

To the other investigators, Alden seemed to possess an extraordinary ability to perceive connections that escaped notice. He seemed to know exactly where to look, which questions to ask, and which details deserved attention. His reputation grew even further because, in practice, his conclusions were almost always correct. What no one knew was that his advantage came not only from intelligence or experience. While the others were trying to discover what had happened, Alden had already been at the scene before them.

He soon realized that this could be much more useful than simply hiding his own existence. Anti-magic allowed him to reach places no investigator could enter, while his official position gave him access to the mechanisms necessary to turn what he discovered at night into legitimate investigations during the day. For the first time, Alden could use the system itself against those who believed they were protected by it.

That was how he began arresting more corrupt archmages. Some were genuinely guilty and had escaped for years precisely because they possessed enough influence to hinder investigations. Alden found the missing evidence, led investigators to it, and let the institutions handle the rest. The result seemed to confirm everything he was beginning to believe: when the system worked, it worked because someone like him found a way to make it work.

The more cases he solved, the greater his reputation became. The greater his reputation, the more trust he received from his superiors and the Council itself. And the more trust he received, the more freedom he gained to investigate. Without realizing it, Alden had created a cycle that benefited precisely what he did in secret. His official competence gave him access. Anti-magic gave him information. The information produced results. And the results increased his authority.

## When the Guilty Man Stops Being the Target

Alden's first targets were genuinely guilty. Mages who commanded criminal organizations, trafficked people, exploited non-mages, and used money, influence, and corruption to remain protected. Alden found the evidence at night, conducted investigations during the day, and, when he realized the system would not be able to hold them accountable, returned to the shadows to do what the institutions could not.

The problem was that the system could still protect some of them. Evidence disappeared. Witnesses changed their statements. Investigations were halted before reaching those responsible. Investigators were ordered to abandon certain lines of inquiry or simply transferred to other cases. The more Alden learned about Veridion's inner workings, the more he realized that corruption did not need to control the entire institution. It only needed to find the right people, pressure the right people, and make the right evidence disappear.

That was how Alden began killing. One by one, men whom no one could reach began to die. The deaths were carefully planned, and Alden always found a justification for each of them. Those men had committed crimes. They had destroyed lives. They had escaped justice. If the system refused to punish them, someone had to do it.

And the results were real. People were saved. Criminal networks disappeared. Operations that had survived for years were dismantled. Alden remained an investigator during the day and the Anti-Mage at night, but the distinction between the two roles was beginning to disappear. Investigation had ceased to be merely a way of discovering who was guilty. It had also become the mechanism by which Alden decided who should be punished.

The change became apparent when an investigator close to Alden realized that something was wrong. He had known Alden long enough to recognize his patterns. He knew there was something strange about the way certain investigations progressed, the almost impossible precision of some of his discoveries, and the frequency with which Alden seemed to know exactly where to look. He did not have enough evidence to accuse him, but he began asking questions.

Alden tried to divert the suspicion. He changed the direction of some investigations, presented other hypotheses, and tried to convince his colleague that the coincidences were merely the result of years of experience. This time, however, he could not simply wait for the problem to disappear. That man knew him. And he knew too much.

For several days, Alden searched for a solution that did not involve violence. He could discredit him. He could arrange a transfer. He could steer the investigation toward another suspect. He could even temporarily abandon some of his activities and wait for the attention to diminish.

But every alternative carried a risk. Until Alden made the decision that, not long before, he would have considered unthinkable: he killed the investigator.

There was no corruption. No criminal organization was behind him. There were no victims waiting for justice. The only thing that made him a target was what he knew about Alden and the possibility of discovering the rest.

After the death, Alden tried to find within himself the same justification he had used so many times before. He told himself that he was protecting himself. That he had no choice. That the man could expose everything he had been doing and endanger everyone who still depended on him.

But this time, the justification did not fit as well. For the first time, Alden had killed someone who did not need to be punished. He only needed to be stopped. And that was when Alden began to distrust himself.

Until then, he could believe that everything he did was connected to his obsession with corrupt archmages and the system's inability to hold them accountable. Now he had crossed a different boundary. That investigator was not his enemy. He was not corrupt. He had committed no crime.

The only threat he represented was the possibility of revealing who Alden had become. Perhaps the problem was no longer merely Veridion's corruption. Perhaps something inside him was changing.

Alden still could not say when it had begun. He did not know whether it had been with that first property he burned, when he discovered the satisfaction of striking someone he considered untouchable, or with one of the later deaths, when he began to realize that he could produce results the institutions could not.

He only knew that he had spent too long telling himself that he did everything because he had to. Now he was beginning to suspect that perhaps he was also doing it because he wanted to.

## The Archmage Anti-Mage

While Alden waged his private war against corruption, Veridion began paying a price he had not calculated. Political disputes intensified, trust in the institutions declined, and the different factions within the Council increasingly came to see one another as threats. At the same time, other magical societies realized that the cities of Veridion were more vulnerable than before. Alden had spent years eliminating corrupt archmages, but archmages were not merely politicians, councilors, or men protected by privilege. They were also some of the principal figures responsible for the cities' defense. Every death left a void that needed to be filled, and there was not always someone prepared to immediately occupy the position of a mage who had spent decades mastering particular areas of magic and protecting thousands of people.

Alden began to perceive a contradiction he had previously ignored. An archmage could be corrupt, abuse his authority, and use his position to protect criminals, yet still be responsible for the security of an entire city. Eliminating him could appear to be a victory for justice when viewed from the perspective of his victims and, at the same time, produce a disastrous strategic consequence when viewed from the perspective of society as a whole. Alden's individual logic did not account for what happened when his decisions went beyond the man he judged and affected the structure that depended on him. He had succeeded in removing people he considered unworthy of power, but he had not been able to immediately replace what they represented for Veridion's defense.

The city began to feel the effects of that absence precisely when it most needed stability. External threats increased, internal disputes became more aggressive, and the Council's old mechanisms of balance began to function with difficulty. Alden had not planned a war and had no interest in provoking a conflict between magical societies, but his actions contributed to creating a situation in which war became more likely. For the first time, the consequences of his crusade extended beyond the individuals he considered guilty. It was no longer possible to evaluate his decisions solely by the immediate result of each death. A corrupt man could deserve punishment and, at the same time, his death could put other people in danger. The justice Alden sought did not necessarily follow the same logic as collective security.

It was in this environment that Aurelius decided to appoint him an archmage. The choice seemed natural to almost everyone. Alden had built an exceptional reputation as an investigator, possessed military and field experience, had faced real threats, and had earned prestige without depending on one of the old magical families. For a society that was increasingly distrustful of its own institutions, he represented a convenient alternative: someone competent, disciplined, and apparently incorruptible, whose trajectory was not tied to the old circles of influence. Aurelius, in particular, saw Alden as a trustworthy man. He knew his work, had followed his rise, and believed that his ability to uncover corruption where others failed could be exactly what the Council needed at that moment. He had no idea that part of that reputation had been built on investigations driven by information Alden obtained illegally during the night.

The appointment placed Alden exactly where he had never imagined being. The man who had spent his life observing the magical elite from below now sat among the archmages. Those who had once represented to him the ultimate symbol of an unequal society became his colleagues, his subordinates in some circumstances, and, in certain cases, people who would have to answer to his own decisions. More ironically, Aurelius, one of the men Alden had learned to despise since childhood and whom he himself had pressured from the shadows, now presented him as an example of competence and trust.

Faced with the new position, Alden decided to suspend his activities as the Anti-Mage, at least for the time being. It was not a decision motivated by remorse or by the conclusion that everything he had done was wrong. For the first time, he possessed enough legitimate power to try to change what he had considered unjust throughout his life. The city was unstable, external threats were real, and the Council needed to recover some of its authority. Perhaps it was possible to reform the institutions without destroying them. Perhaps it was possible to protect non-mages, reduce the space available to corruption, and limit the privileges of the old families without resorting to the shadows again. Alden now had access to the people, resources, and structures necessary to attempt to do this from within.

For a while, he believed it could work. Perhaps this was finally the opportunity he had been looking for since childhood: not to destroy the system, but to transform it. He no longer needed to secretly enter the properties of powerful men to discover their crimes. He no longer needed to decide on his own when someone should die. He could use his authority, reputation, and influence to pressure the Council and build legitimate changes. The possibility was real and, precisely because of that, represented perhaps the greatest test Alden had ever faced.

But anti-magic remained within him. So did the conviction that had accompanied his entire transformation: when an institution fails, someone has to act. Alden had begun by wanting to prove that someone like him could go as far as those born above him. Then he discovered that he could deliver justice when institutions failed. Next, he realized that he could manipulate those same institutions to produce the results he wanted. Then he began killing not only those he considered guilty, but also those who could threaten his secret. Now he had reached the top of the structure he had questioned throughout his life.

And society did not see a vigilante, a murderer, or a man who had learned to circumvent the very institutions he had sworn to serve. It saw Alden. The man who had come from the countryside, survived prejudice, earned his place without a great lineage, and reached the top through his own competence. Aurelius saw a trustworthy man. The Council saw a promising archmage. Veridion saw someone who could help rebuild the stability it was beginning to lose.

Alden knew what no one else knew. He had used his position as an investigator to cover his own crimes, manipulated investigations, and transformed information obtained from the shadows into official judgments. Some of his victims were guilty. Others were not. At some point, however, he stopped asking who deserved to be punished and began asking only who represented a threat to him.

Now he was at the top and, for the first time, had a real opportunity to correct the system he had spent his life trying to defeat. The question was whether the man who had learned to act without answering to anyone could return to accepting limits when he finally possessed enough power to ignore them.

Perhaps the Anti-Mage would return. Perhaps he would never be needed again. The question was no longer simply what Alden would do with the power he had acquired. It was who would have enough power to judge him.
