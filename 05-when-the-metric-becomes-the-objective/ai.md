A small social network can rely on community members to filter what is worth seeing, but growth to millions of users makes this impossible, turning content selection into a problem that requires machine-readable proxies for subjective qualities such as relevance or interest. Metrics such as clicks, comments, shares, and time spent provide those proxies, but the central problem begins when an indicator intended to represent value becomes the mechanism that defines value. Once engagement becomes the objective, creators adapt their behavior toward formats, headlines, opinions, and emotional triggers that maximize engagement, while algorithms increasingly distribute the content that generates those signals, creating a feedback loop in which the platform influences not only what people see but also what they produce. This is an instance of Goodhart's Law: a measure can be useful as an approximation while remaining imperfect, but once it becomes a target, participants optimize for the metric and the metric becomes less representative of the underlying reality. The same mechanism can occur in education when grades become the primary objective, encouraging teaching and studying toward tests without necessarily producing equivalent gains in learning. On social networks, engagement may indicate interest, but optimizing for it creates a distinction between generating genuine interest and generating reactions, favoring content that produces strong measurable signals even when it is superficial or provocative. Over time, these incentives alter culture itself: users increasingly ask what "works" within the system rather than simply what they want to express, producing behavioral homogeneity despite greater diversity in the creator population. This does not require deliberate malice or reduce the problem to corporate greed; companies, creators, advertisers, investors, and users can each make locally rational decisions that improve their respective metrics while collectively degrading the broader experience. The same pattern extends beyond social networks to citations as proxies for academic impact, hours worked as proxies for productivity, audience as a proxy for relevance, followers as a proxy for influence, and growth as a proxy for success. Metrics necessarily simplify reality by excluding information, and the danger arises when that simplification is forgotten and the metric is treated as reality itself. Optimization therefore contains a prior question that is often neglected: what does "better" mean, and according to which criterion? A platform can maximize attention without maximizing user experience, a school can maximize grades without maximizing education, and a company can maximize revenue without necessarily maximizing value. Some valuable activities, such as conversation, friendship, art, education, community, or reading, can be improved but do not necessarily benefit from being converted into maximization problems. The deterioration of social networks can therefore be understood as a process rather than an isolated failure: growth creates the need for selection, selection requires indicators, indicators become incentives, incentives change behavior, behavior changes culture, and the resulting system can become increasingly optimized for its measurements while becoming worse at its original purpose. The conclusion is not to abandon metrics, algorithms, markets, or growth, but to distinguish between measurement and purpose: metrics can help describe reality, coordinate action, and support decisions, but they cannot independently determine what should be valued. Optimization is a tool, whereas value is a choice, and the central danger is allowing the tool to define the objective.
